This is post 4 in [Reading Stanford CS229](/en/series/stanford-cs229), covering **Stanford CS229, Spring 2021, Lecture 3**. The lecture took place on April 5, 2021, under the official title **Weighted Least Squares. Logistic regression. Newton's Method.** This article uses the Spring 2021 live lecture notes and Sections 4, 5, and 7 of the shared Supervised Learning notes assigned by the syllabus. The recording was not used.

The lecture replaces “choose a loss” with “state how the data is generated.” Once a probability model is specified, likelihood provides a way to select parameters. Least squares and logistic regression become two instances of the same modeling recipe rather than disconnected formulas.

## What noise model does least squares imply?

Suppose labels are generated by a linear signal plus error:

```text
y^(i) = θᵀx^(i) + ε^(i)
ε^(i) ~ N(0, σ²)
```

The notes assume zero-mean, independent errors with a shared variance. Conditional on `x` and `θ`, `y` is therefore Gaussian with mean `θᵀx`. The likelihood is a product of conditional densities. After taking logs, the part that depends on `θ` is proportional to the negative sum of squared residuals. Maximizing log-likelihood is thus equivalent to minimizing:

```text
1/2 Σ_i (y^(i) - θᵀx^(i))²
```

This derivation does not claim that real errors are always Gaussian. It shows that a loss encodes assumptions about data generation. If error variance differs by observation, weighted least squares points toward assigning observations different weights. The public live notes do not fully derive that variant, so this article does not manufacture a derivation that is absent from the material.

## Why classification cannot use an unrestricted linear output

Binary classification has `y ∈ {0,1}`. A raw score `θᵀx` may be negative or greater than one, so it cannot directly represent a probability. Logistic regression passes the score through the sigmoid:

```text
g(z) = 1 / (1 + e^(-z))
hθ(x) = g(θᵀx)
```

The model interprets `hθ(x)` as `P(y=1|x;θ)` and `1-hθ(x)` as `P(y=0|x;θ)`. The sigmoid is more than a bent line. It turns a linear score into a valid Bernoulli parameter, allowing classification to be trained by likelihood.

## One expression handles both labels

The conditional probability of one binary label is:

```text
p(y|x;θ) = hθ(x)^y (1-hθ(x))^(1-y)
```

When `y=1`, only the first factor remains; when `y=0`, only the second does. Taking logs over all examples gives:

```text
ℓ(θ) = Σ_i [y^(i) log hθ(x^(i))
          + (1-y^(i)) log(1-hθ(x^(i)))]
```

Training maximizes `ℓ(θ)`. The notes show that its gradient update has the familiar error-times-feature shape of LMS. That visual similarity comes from the derivatives; it does not make the two models' probability assumptions identical.

## Why Newton's method uses second-order information

In one dimension, Newton's method uses a tangent to find a root of `f(x)=0`:

```text
x_(t+1) = x_t - f(x_t) / f'(x_t)
```

To maximize `ℓ(θ)`, let `f=ℓ'`. The multidimensional update uses the Hessian:

```text
θ_(t+1) = θ_t - H^(-1) ∇ℓ(θ_t)
```

A gradient says which way the surface slopes. The Hessian also describes local curvature, so a Newton step scales movement differently across directions. The notes compare convergence against per-step cost: Newton often needs fewer iterations, but each iteration must form and process a second-order matrix. Time and storage grow quickly with the parameter dimension.

Fewer iterations therefore do not guarantee lower total runtime. With many parameters, cheap first-order updates may be more practical. The lecture presents a tradeoff, not a universal recommendation to use Newton's method.

## Where Lecture 3 sits in the eighteen-lecture path

Lecture 3 adds probability to Lecture 2's hypothesis-objective-optimization pipeline. Lecture 4 places Gaussian, Bernoulli, and other distributions inside the exponential-family framework. Lecture 5 then moves from directly modeling `p(y|x)` to generative models of `p(x|y)p(y)`. The durable habit introduced here is to ask which assumptions are hidden inside a loss.

## Beyond the lecture

For one binary example, compute `z=θᵀx` and `g(z)`, then substitute both `y=1` and `y=0` into the log-likelihood. Push `z` far in the positive and negative directions and observe how the objective penalizes a confident wrong prediction. This exercise makes the direction of the classification loss clearer than memorizing the sigmoid derivative alone.
