The course schedule dates it May 3, 2021, under the official title **K-Means. GMM (non EM). Expectation Maximization.** This article uses the Spring 2021 Live Lecture Notes and the assigned shared notes on K-means, mixtures of Gaussians, and EM. The recording was not used as a source.

This is the first lecture to remove labels. K-means assigns each point to one cluster directly. A Gaussian mixture model instead assumes that an unobserved source generated each point and represents uncertainty about that source probabilistically. EM addresses the resulting circularity: the sources are unknown because the parameters are unknown, and vice versa.

## K-means alternates between two sets of variables

Given `x¹,…,xⁿ ∈ R^d` and a cluster count `k`, K-means maintains assignments `cᵢ` and centers `μⱼ`:

```text
cᵢ ← argmin_j ||xᵢ-μⱼ||²
μⱼ ← mean of all xᵢ with cᵢ=j
```

Its distortion objective is

```text
J(c,μ) = Σᵢ ||xᵢ-μ_{cᵢ}||²
```

With centers fixed, the nearest-center assignment minimizes `J`. With assignments fixed, each cluster mean minimizes the sum of squared distances. Every step therefore leaves `J` unchanged or decreases it. This gives monotonic progress in the objective, not a global guarantee: `J(c,μ)` is not jointly convex. Different random initializations can reach different solutions, so a common practice is to run the algorithm repeatedly and retain the lowest distortion.

The data also do not supply one uniquely correct `k`. Two clusters and four clusters may both be meaningful descriptions under different modeling goals. K-means answers a narrower question: given `k` and squared Euclidean distance, find a locally stable hard clustering.

## A GMM turns the cluster assignment into a latent variable

A Gaussian mixture model assumes that each point is generated by

```text
zᵢ ~ Multinomial(φ)
xᵢ | zᵢ=j ~ N(μⱼ, Σⱼ)
```

The latent variable `zᵢ` identifies the Gaussian source; `φⱼ` is its mixture weight. After observing `xᵢ`, the posterior responsibility is

```text
wᵢⱼ = p(zᵢ=j | xᵢ; φ,μ,Σ)
     = φⱼ N(xᵢ;μⱼ,Σⱼ) / Σ_l φ_l N(xᵢ;μ_l,Σ_l)
```

Unlike a single K-means assignment, `wᵢⱼ` is soft: one point may retain different probabilities for several sources. Components may also have different covariances and mixture weights instead of being determined only by distance to a center.

If every `zᵢ` were observed, estimating the component means, covariances, and proportions would be straightforward. The difficulty is that `zᵢ` is hidden while its posterior requires the current parameters. EM begins from that dependency.

## What the E-step and M-step alternate

For a GMM, the E-step computes all responsibilities under the current parameters. The M-step treats them as fractional counts:

```text
φⱼ ← (1/n) Σᵢ wᵢⱼ
μⱼ ← Σᵢ wᵢⱼxᵢ / Σᵢ wᵢⱼ
Σⱼ ← Σᵢ wᵢⱼ(xᵢ-μⱼ)(xᵢ-μⱼ)ᵀ / Σᵢ wᵢⱼ
```

These are almost the same maximum-likelihood estimates as in the observed-label case. Posterior probabilities `wᵢⱼ` replace indicators `1{zᵢ=j}`. The E-step does not select one most likely source and pretend it is certain; it preserves the full discrete posterior.

## How Jensen's inequality produces the ELBO

A latent-variable likelihood places a sum inside a logarithm:

```text
log p(x;θ) = log Σ_z p(x,z;θ)
```

Introduce any distribution `Q(z)`:

```text
log p(x;θ)
= log Σ_z Q(z) p(x,z;θ)/Q(z)
≥ Σ_z Q(z) log[p(x,z;θ)/Q(z)]
```

The last step applies Jensen's inequality because `log` is concave. The right side is the evidence lower bound, or ELBO. When

```text
Q(z) = p(z|x;θ)
```

the ratio `p(x,z;θ)/Q(z)` is constant in `z`, making Jensen's inequality tight. The E-step chooses a lower bound that touches the likelihood at the current parameters. The M-step holds `Q` fixed and maximizes that bound over `θ`.

## Monotonic improvement is not global optimality

Let the current parameters be `θ_t`. The E-step makes the ELBO equal the likelihood at `θ_t`, and the M-step does not decrease the ELBO:

```text
ℓ(θ_{t+1}) ≥ ELBO(Q_t,θ_{t+1})
             ≥ ELBO(Q_t,θ_t)
             = ℓ(θ_t)
```

This proves that log likelihood does not decrease. It does not prove that EM reaches a global optimum. The GMM likelihood is nonconvex, and initialization can change the final result. K-means and EM both improve a stated objective step by step while remaining vulnerable to local solutions.

Model assumptions impose another limit. Gaussian components, cluster count, and covariance structure are chosen in advance. EM can increase likelihood inside that model; it cannot determine whether those assumptions describe the data well.

## Where Lecture 11 sits in the eighteen-lecture path

Lecture 10 studied supervised-model error under changing training sets. Lecture 11 infers structure without labels. It establishes K-means, GMMs, and the general EM framework. Lecture 12 finishes the GMM properties and applies the same EM framework to factor analysis with a continuous latent variable.

For a small check, run one K-means iteration on four one-dimensional points and verify that assignment and mean updates do not increase distortion. Then specify two Gaussians for the same points and compute one set of `wᵢⱼ`. The contrast is exactly the difference between a hard assignment and a posterior responsibility.

## Beyond the lecture

Run K-means and a GMM repeatedly on the same data and record final distortion or log likelihood. In addition to selecting the best run, plot the distribution across initializations. It makes visible how monotonic improvement during each run and sensitivity of the final solution can both be true.
