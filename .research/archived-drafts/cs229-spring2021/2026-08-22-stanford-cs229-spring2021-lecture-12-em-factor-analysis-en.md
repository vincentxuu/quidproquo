Lecture 11 used a discrete latent variable to identify which Gaussian generated a point. Lecture 12 replaces it with a continuous low-dimensional vector: a high-dimensional observation may be generated by a few shared factors plus coordinate-specific noise. The framework remains EM, but the E-step now handles Gaussian posterior moments rather than a short list of cluster probabilities.

## Finishing the GMM M-step

The GMM E-step provides responsibilities

```text
wᵢⱼ = p(zᵢ=j|xᵢ;θ)
```

The M-step maximizes the ELBO. Mixture weights must also satisfy `Σⱼφⱼ=1`, so introduce the Lagrangian

```text
L(φ,β) = ΣᵢΣⱼ wᵢⱼ log φⱼ + β(Σⱼφⱼ-1)
```

Setting derivatives to zero and applying the constraint yields

```text
φⱼ = (1/n) Σᵢ wᵢⱼ
```

A component's mixture weight is its total responsibility divided by the sample count. The same weighted maximum-likelihood calculation gives the `μⱼ` and `Σⱼ` updates. EM is therefore not an informal alternation of guesses; each M-step solves a defined optimization problem for the current ELBO.

## Why high-dimensional, small-sample data break a full Gaussian

For `x ∈ R^d`, empirical covariance is

```text
Σ_hat = (1/n) Σᵢ (xᵢ-μ)(xᵢ-μ)ᵀ
```

When the examples span only a low-dimensional subspace, `Σ_hat` is singular, so its inverse and determinant cannot be used in the usual Gaussian density. Restricting covariance to a diagonal matrix avoids the problem but assumes that coordinates are uncorrelated. Restricting it further to `σ²I` leaves only spherical noise with one scale.

Factor analysis aims to preserve useful parts of both models: represent cross-coordinate correlations with relatively few parameters without fitting an unrestricted `d×d` covariance.

## The factor-analysis generative model

The model is

```text
z ~ N(0,I_k)
ε ~ N(0,Ψ)
x = μ + Λz + ε
```

Here `k<d`, `Λ ∈ R^{d×k}`, and `Ψ` is diagonal. The latent vector `z` carries shared factors, `Λ` maps them into observation space, and `ε` adds coordinate-specific noise.

Because `z` and `ε` are independent and have zero mean,

```text
E[x] = μ
Cov(x) = ΛΛᵀ + Ψ
```

The term `ΛΛᵀ` produces cross-coordinate covariance but has rank at most `k`. The diagonal `Ψ` adds independent residual variance to every observed coordinate. This is the structural assumption of factor analysis: a few common factors explain most correlations, while remaining noise is independent in the observed coordinates.

## The E-step uses Gaussian conditioning

The joint Gaussian over `z` and `x` is

```text
[z] ~ N([0], [I       Λᵀ      ])
[x]     [μ]  [Λ  ΛΛᵀ + Ψ]
```

The multivariate Gaussian conditional formula gives

```text
z|x ~ N(m,V)

m = Λᵀ(ΛΛᵀ+Ψ)⁻¹(x-μ)
V = I - Λᵀ(ΛΛᵀ+Ψ)⁻¹Λ
```

This is the E-step distribution `Qᵢ(zᵢ)`. It is not a single most likely `zᵢ`; it is an entire posterior. For each example, the M-step needs at least

```text
E[zᵢ|xᵢ] = mᵢ
E[zᵢzᵢᵀ|xᵢ] = mᵢmᵢᵀ + V
```

The covariance `V` in the second expression cannot be discarded. Substituting only the posterior mean treats latent uncertainty as zero and no longer produces the EM update derived in the notes.

## Why the M-step resembles regression with uncertainty

With `Qᵢ` fixed, maximize expected complete-data log likelihood over the loading matrix. The update resembles a normal equation:

```text
Λ ← [Σᵢ (xᵢ-μ)E[zᵢ]ᵀ]
     [Σᵢ E[zᵢzᵢᵀ]]⁻¹
```

If each `zᵢ` were observed, this would look like a linear regression from latent factors to `xᵢ`. EM differs because the factors are hidden. The denominator uses posterior second moments rather than merely `E[zᵢ]E[zᵢ]ᵀ`. The mean `μ` becomes the sample mean, while `Ψ` keeps the diagonal of expected residual covariance.

Continuous latent variables therefore do not force EM to replace a sum with one best guess. General EM replaces the discrete sum by an integral or expectation under the posterior density.

## Limits of the lecture

Factor analysis does not uniquely identify latent axes. An orthogonal rotation of `z`, paired with the inverse rotation of `Λ`, leaves the observation covariance `ΛΛᵀ+Ψ` unchanged. A latent coordinate should not be declared the one true factor without additional constraints.

The model also assumes a linear Gaussian map and diagonal residual covariance. A nonlinear manifold, heavy-tailed data, or strongly correlated residuals may not fit these assumptions. EM's monotonic likelihood guarantee still does not imply global optimality, and both initialization and latent dimension `k` remain modeling choices.

## Where Lecture 12 sits in the eighteen-lecture path

Lecture 11 moved from K-means to EM with a discrete latent variable. Lecture 12 shows that the same framework handles continuous Gaussian latent variables. Lecture 13 turns the low-dimensional representation problem toward PCA, finding a linear subspace that preserves variance rather than beginning with the full probabilistic generative model.

One compact understanding check is to derive covariance from `x=μ+Λz+ε`. Expand `Cov(x)` and state why every cross term vanishes. Reaching `ΛΛᵀ+Ψ` isolates how factor analysis represents high-dimensional correlation with low-dimensional latent structure.

## Beyond the lecture

Generate synthetic data from a two-dimensional `z`, a high-dimensional `Λ`, and diagonal `Ψ`. Compare a full empirical covariance, a diagonal Gaussian, and a factor-analysis covariance. In addition to likelihood, inspect their reconstructed off-diagonal correlations; that is where the assumptions differ most directly.
