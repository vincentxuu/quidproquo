## 3. Topic map (chapters from the uploader's description; content from subtitles)

### 00:00–00:36 Intro
- Claim: more recorded accidents happen on the way down than in any other part of climbing (00:04–00:13). **Speaker claim, unsourced in video** — needs an editorial source (e.g. ANAC / Accidents in North American Climbing) if used as fact.
- "Relatively simple, but the devil's in the detail" — nuances make it safer and more efficient.

### 00:36–02:59 How NOT to rappel (evolution of the system)
1. Traditional: tube device clipped straight to belay loop, two strands through (00:48–00:59).
2. Problem: works only with a hand on the brake strand; let go = freefall; rockfall / sneeze → no redundancy, "could cost me my life" (01:04–01:32).
3. Fix #1 "third hand": friction hitch (he ties an **autoblock**, not a prusik) on the brake strands, clipped to the **leg loop** (01:32–02:16).
4. Problems with leg-loop third hand (02:16–02:50):
   - carabiner sits on the leg-loop metal buckle; "the leg loop isn't rated for this";
   - autoblock must stay separated from the device — if they touch, the device deactivates the autoblock and it is "essentially useless".
→ leads to the modern extended system.

### 02:59–03:37 Anchors
- "We should always be building our anchors every abseil station" — for safety and equalising anchor points (03:06–03:18).
- Clipped to master point with lanyard (Petzl Connect; "any kind of lanyard would do").
- Recommends an **adjustable lanyard** because it makes the function test easier later (03:26–03:35).

### 03:37–06:59 Ropes setup (two ropes, multi-pitch arrival)
1. He is tied into both ropes on arrival (03:38).
2. **The first climber's (leader's) rope ends get untied and threaded first** (03:45–04:09). Reason: the seconder's ends are on top of the pile, so when you throw, the ropes unfurl cleanly instead of a mess (04:12–04:33).
3. **Thread the rope behind the anchor carabiners** (rope on the rock side, carabiners in front) (04:36–05:10). Reason: the last person has to clean the anchor while the rope is weighted; if the carabiners are behind the rope, the loaded rope pinches them and they're hard to remove.
4. Join the two ropes (05:10–06:53): options = rethreaded figure 8, simple overhand (flat overhand), "but those come with their issues". **His pick: double overhand** (he means a double-wrapped overhand / "double overhand bend" tied with both strands together).
   - Tying trick: wrap both strands twice around index + middle finger, pull fingers out → tunnel → feed both ends through → tighten and dress (05:27–05:46). [visual needed for exact form]
   - Reasons: (a) easy to see if well dressed — with two colours you should see **alternating colours** (05:49–06:03); (b) avoids the simple overhand's tendency to "roll"/capsize towards the ends (06:03–06:28); (c) knot sits **off-axis**, to one side of the rope, so when pulling through a crack at the next station it's less likely to snag (06:28–06:51).
   - Note for article: he does not mention tail length.
5. Pull the knot up to the anchor (06:55).

### 06:59–11:18 Rappel/abseil set-up
1. **Autoblock first**, clipped to the **belay loop** (not leg loop) with the prusik loop (07:01–07:27).
2. Why autoblock before the device (07:27–08:21): at hanging stances the rope weight hangs on you; an autoblock can be wrapped on tensioned ropes; once set, pull slack through above it — the rope weight below loads/activates the autoblock — so you have slack to mount the device comfortably instead of fighting rope weight.
3. **Extend the device** (08:21–09:47): device straight on belay loop would hit the autoblock and stop it extending/locking. Easiest: **60 cm sling**, rigged as a **basket hitch through the belay loop** (not girth-hitched) (pass sling through belay loop, bring two ends together, clip device carabiner through both ends) (09:17–09:34).
   - Alternatives: sling from an alpine quickdraw; 120 cm sling doubled = 60 cm (08:57–09:17).
4. Pass the two bights through the device, close the carabiner (09:35–09:47).
5. Pull all slack through device and autoblock (09:47–09:55).
6. Ropes (09:55–11:18):
   - **Stopper knot in each end** — double overhand ("same thing we did to connect the ropes") (09:58–10:11).
   - Pull up enough rope to have weight in the hand so the throw drags the rest down (10:26–10:36).
   - **Shout "rope"** before throwing so people below can cover their heads (10:37–10:48).
   - Throw each strand separately (10:48).
   - If it snags on bushes, sort it out on the way down, but **never rappel past the point where it's caught** — if you can't free it from below you'll have to climb back up (11:02–11:17).

### 11:18–13:32 Function test
1. Lengthen the adjustable lanyard (so the lanyard goes slack when the device takes weight) (11:28–11:43).
2. Weight the rappel set-up; check everything works (11:43).
3. Check carabiners are closed — on camera one **was open a bit** ("so good check there") (11:48–11:53).
4. Only then remove the lanyard (11:54–11:58).
5. Efficiency: clip the lanyard carabiner to **the strand you'll need to pull** at the next station (memory aid) (12:00–12:11).
6. If last down: clean the anchor while weighting the ropes — works because rope is behind the anchor carabiners (12:14–12:30).
7. **Hands-free backup** ("backup or catastrophe knot"): don't trust the autoblock alone — tie an overhand / figure 8 on a bight below the device and clip it to the belay loop with another carabiner (12:30–12:53).
8. Clean anchor, organise gear, don't leave things dangling; slings over the shoulder (12:54–13:19).
9. Hand on brake strands → unclip catastrophe knot → stow carabiner → start (13:21–13:37).

### 13:32–14:25 The main event (technique while rappelling)
- Brake hand (his right) creates the friction and controls the descent; the autoblock is just for emergencies (13:42–14:17).
- Goal is to keep the autoblock from activating, not let it grab and release repeatedly; criticises people making "obscene gestures" with their hand (i.e. hand wrapped around/pulling the autoblock).

### 14:25–15:10 Outro
- "A basic system… with a few tips to make it a bit more efficient." Invites questions.

## 4. Ordered technique list (as taught)

1. Build/clip to anchor with (adjustable) lanyard at every station.
2. Untie and thread the **leader's** rope ends first.
3. Thread rope **behind** the anchor carabiners.
4. Join ropes with **double overhand** (finger-wrap tunnel method), dress, check alternating colours; pull knot to anchor.
5. **Autoblock first**, on **belay loop**.
6. Pull slack above the autoblock.
7. **Extend device** with a 60 cm sling basket-hitched through belay loop (or doubled 120 cm / alpine draw sling).
8. Load both bights into device, lock carabiner, take in slack on device and autoblock.
9. **Stopper knots** (double overhand) in both ends.
10. Coil weight in hand, **shout "rope"**, throw each strand; never rappel past a snag.
11. **Function test**: lengthen lanyard, weight device, check autoblock + carabiners locked.
12. Remove lanyard; clip its carabiner to the **pull strand**.
13. Last person cleans anchor while hanging on the rappel.
14. **Catastrophe knot** clipped to belay loop when hands are needed; remove before descending.
15. Organise gear (slings over shoulder).
16. Descend: brake hand controls, autoblock idle as backup.

### S1 — Why the old system has no redundancy · 48.10–92.78 s (44.7 s) · `start=48`

### S2 — Thread the rope behind the anchor · 276.60–310.28 s (33.7 s) · `start=276`
> Now you'll notice that I've threaded the rope behind the anchor here, and this is a really nice little detail, okay. Because when it comes to the second abseilor [abseiler] or the last person to abseil wanting to begin their abseil, they're going to have to clean the anchor. And if these carabiners are behind the rope here, then it can be really tricky to get them out from behind that rope, because the rope is going to be it's going to have a lot of weight on the end of it, and it's going to be pinching those carabiners. So we want to make sure that we're threading the rope behind the carabiners on our anchor here.

### S3 — Why the double overhand · 346.36–388.00 s (41.6 s) · `start=346`
> Okay, this knot is really nice because first of all, it's easy to see if it's well dressed. Usually when we're climbing multi pitch routes, we're climbing with two different coloured ropes and I should always have alternating colours. As you can see here is the case. And also it avoids a slight issue with just a simple overhand knot where the knot could kind of unravel itself. It kind of starts flopping over itself. I don't really know how to explain it. It kind of starts rolling and and could potentially roll to the ends of the ropes. With the double overhand knot it's very, very difficult for that to happen, especially if it's well dressed.

### S4 — Why the autoblock goes on first (best, most self-contained) · 447.44–501.76 s (54.3 s) · `start=447`
> Now why are we doing our auto block before setting up our belay or our abseil device? And there's a pretty important reason. And that is that... Right here we've got the ropes on a pretty comfortable ledge, and they're all piled up here. But that's not always the case. Sometimes we're literally hanging on the edge of a cliff, and we've got all of the weight of the ropes hanging on us here. Now, an auto block we can set up on ropes with tension in them because we're just wrapping around those tense ropes. So it's really easy to set up first. And then once we've got the auto block set up, we can pull some slack through the weight that's hanging on. The end of the ropes is going to make the auto block activate, and it means that I can have some slack up at this end to be able to comfortably set up my abseil device, rather than fighting against the weight of the ropes as I do.
