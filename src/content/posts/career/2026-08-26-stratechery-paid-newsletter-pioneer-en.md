In April 2014, Ben Thompson went full-time on Stratechery from his apartment in Taipei. Substack wouldn't exist for another four years. The idea that someone could make a living writing a newsletter by themselves struck most people as wishful thinking.

Twelve years later, Stratechery has 40,000+ paid subscribers, annual revenue north of $5M, and Substack's seed-round pitch to investors was literally "Stratechery-in-a-box." Thompson didn't just prove the model first — he *is* the model.

## Background: A Winding Road from Small-Town Wisconsin to Taipei

Ben Thompson grew up in a Wisconsin town of about 2,000 people. Both his parents were ministers. He attended the University of Wisconsin–Madison, and after graduating, moved to Taiwan to teach English — planning to stay for one year. He stayed for twenty-two.

During his time in Taiwan, he earned an MBA from Northwestern's Kellogg School of Management and a Master of Engineering Management (MEM) from McCormick School of Engineering, focusing on strategy, marketing, and human-centered design.

His post-grad career was impressive but nonlinear:

- **Apple**: Interned at Apple University
- **Microsoft**: Joined the Windows Apps team
- **Automattic**: Worked as a growth engineer for WordPress.com

In 2013, while still at Microsoft, he started Stratechery as a blog. A few months later, he returned to Taipei and kept writing from his apartment. In April 2014, he went full-time — partly because a consulting gig fell through, forcing him to confront the question of "what next."

The answer: just keep writing.

## Growth Timeline

| Date | Milestone |

Going from 1,000 to 40,000 paid subscribers took roughly a decade. Compared to Morning Brew's millions of free subscribers, that number looks modest — but every single one is paying.

## Content Strategy: Analyst, Not Journalist

Stratechery isn't about breaking news. It's about **strategic analysis**. Thompson doesn't chase scoops. He takes what already happened and explains *why* it matters and *what comes next*, using frameworks he developed himself.

The most famous is **Aggregation Theory** — published in July 2015, it explains how platforms like Google, Facebook, and Amazon dominate markets by aggregating demand (not supply). It's widely considered the most important business framework in tech analysis since Clayton Christensen's theory of disruptive innovation.

His publishing cadence has evolved over time:

- **2013–2014 (side project)**: 2 free posts per week
- **After going full-time in 2014**: 2 free + 5 paid posts per week
- **2015 to present**: Settled at 4 posts per week (1 free + 3 paid Daily Updates)

Free posts are long-form strategy essays (~3,000 words). Paid Daily Updates run ~1,500 words, offering real-time analysis of the day's tech news. This combination — free long-form pieces to build trust, paid short-form to maintain engagement — became the template that countless subscription newsletters copied.

Another distinctive trait: Thompson wrote about Silicon Valley from Taipei. The physical distance turned out to be an advantage — he wasn't inside the tech industry's social bubble, wasn't influenced by launch parties and coffee chats. He's said the distance allowed him to analyze company strategy more objectively.

## Business Model: The Purest Subscription Play

Stratechery's business model is radically pure: **nearly 100% of revenue comes from reader subscriptions**. No ads, no courses, no speaking fees.

Pricing evolution:

1. **Three-tier (2014)**: $50/$100/$300 per year, with different content depth at each level
2. **Single plan (2015)**: Simplified to $100/year (~$10/month), all paid content unlocked
3. **Stratechery Plus (2020s)**: $12/month or $120/year, later raised to $15/month or $150/year, bundling all articles plus the podcast lineup

After 2020, Thompson faced a pivotal choice: subscriber growth was plateauing. Should he raise prices, or increase the value of a subscription? He chose the latter — adding podcasts to the paid bundle so loyal subscribers felt they were getting more, while effectively raising average revenue per subscriber.

The current podcast lineup includes four shows:

- **Dithering** (with John Gruber): Twice weekly, exactly 15 minutes per episode
- **Sharp Tech** (with Andrew Sharp): 45–60 minute discussions on tech's broader impact
- **Sharp China** (Sharp and Bill Bishop): China tech analysis
- **Greatest Of All Talk** (Sharp and Ben Golliver): NBA analysis

Podcasts cost far less to produce than long-form essays — conversation is faster than writing, yet subscribers value the extra content. This let him expand the perceived value of the paid bundle without sacrificing article quality.

He also did something rare: **built his own subscription infrastructure**. Passport, launched in 2021, is a custom subscription management system handling personalized delivery across email, SMS, RSS, and podcast feeds, with seamless authentication — subscribers click a link and are logged in automatically. He doesn't use Substack or third-party platforms beyond Memberful, which lets him retain nearly all subscription revenue (no 10% platform cut).

With 26,000–40,000 paid subscribers at $120–150/year, annual revenue falls in the $3M–$5M+ range. A 2020 industry analysis estimated his profits had already crossed $3M — because there's almost no fixed cost. One person writing, one person managing.

## Three Lessons in His Own Words

**Distance is a feature, not a bug.** Writing about Silicon Valley from Taipei sounds like a disadvantage. But Thompson believes it kept him free from the tech industry's social dynamics, allowing him to analyze purely from business logic. He didn't move back to the US until twenty-two years later — and the reason was his daughter finishing high school, not a need to be closer to Silicon Valley.

**Frameworks are worth more than opinions.** Individual takes expire, but an analytical framework like Aggregation Theory can be applied to new situations indefinitely. Readers aren't just paying for "Ben Thompson's opinion on this thing" — they're paying for a thinking tool that helps them understand the tech industry. That's the deepest moat a subscription business can have.

**Don't rush to build a team.** He ran solo for over a decade. No co-founder, no full-time employees. The podcast co-hosts (Gruber, Sharp) are collaborators, not hires. He chose to focus his energy on the content itself rather than on managing an organization. The trade-off: growth is capped. The upside: margins are extremely high, and decisions happen instantly.

## The Bigger Picture

Stratechery's success rests on several hard-to-replicate conditions: Thompson's work experience at Apple, Microsoft, and Automattic gave him genuine industry perspective (not an outsider guessing). His Kellogg MBA provided systematic strategy training. And he proved the paid subscription model before anyone else did — first-mover advantage is real.

But what truly made Stratechery a benchmark is his unwavering commitment to the analyst role: no chasing traffic, no taking ads, no letting anyone besides readers become the customer. For twelve years, his readers have been his only boss.

When Substack launched in 2017, co-founder Chris Best pitched it as "letting everyone build their own Stratechery." That wasn't just a tribute — it acknowledged a fact: Ben Thompson, from an apartment in Taipei, had single-handedly defined an entire industry's business model.
