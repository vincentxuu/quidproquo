You lead comfortably at the gym, and one day a friend invites you to Long Dong. At the base of the wall you find only a line of bolts with hangers, no quickdraws already in place. The ground is not padded; it is a sloping scree bank. There is no colored tape on the route, just one line in the guidebook.

This post is for that step. It is part 4 of the [Sport and Trad Climbing](/posts/climbing/2026-10-17-sport-vs-trad-overview-en) series; the previous part is [Outdoor Belaying](/posts/climbing/2026-10-17-outdoor-belay-en). Clipping direction, back-clips, Z-clips, paying out slack and soft catches are covered in [Learning to Lead at the Gym](/posts/climbing/2026-10-17-gym-lead-climbing-en); here we only cover what is new outside.

[REI's lead climbing basics](https://www.rei.com/learn/expert-advice/lead-climbing-basics.html) puts it plainly: learning to lead in a gym is a good start, but outdoors you also need to build and clean an anchor, two things you don't do at the gym. The anchor work is covered in [Top-Rope Anchor Setup](/posts/climbing/2026-10-17-top-rope-anchor-setup-en) and [Lower or Rappel](/posts/climbing/2026-10-17-lower-or-rappel-en). **This post does not replace an outdoor course**; go the first time with an instructor or an experienced climber.

## Adjust your expectations first: gym grades don't transfer directly

The American Alpine Club's [Know the Ropes: Human Factors](https://publications.americanalpineclub.org/articles/13201217363) lists "gym-to-crag overconfidence" as an accident pattern. Its example is a climber who had been "climbing 5.12 in the gym" leading a 5.12a outdoors. He looked shaky from the start, drifted off route, missed a clip, and flipped upside down in the fall, hitting his head.

The AAC's conclusion: the gym prepares you with only a fraction of the skills you need outdoors. Footwork, sharp or loose rock, runouts and route-finding make outdoor leading a different sport.

REI says the same thing: being able to climb 5.10 on top-rope does not mean you can lead 5.10. For your first lead, pick a route where you feel completely stable and in control, so you can focus on clipping and managing the rope.

**One thing to do tonight**: open a guidebook or the [Taiwan Outdoor Climbing Club (TOCC) Long Dong page](https://www.tocc-climbing.org/ld-rock-climbing). Pick three sport routes at least two grades below your gym lead grade as candidates for your first day.

## Gear: what to bring beyond the gym

Most gym lead routes have quickdraws hung and anchors in place, and some gyms even provide ropes. Outdoors you bring all of it.

| Gear | Gym | Outdoors |
|---|---|---|
| Quickdraws | Hung on the wall | Bring your own: as many as the route has bolts, plus what you need at the anchor |
| Rope | Provided by the gym | Check the route height first |
| Helmet | — | Wear one |
| Stick clip | Not needed | For the first bolt |
| Anchor gear | Not needed | Depends on whether you top-rope, lower or rappel |

A few details:

- **How many quickdraws**: REI says it depends on the number of bolts. The AAC's [Cleaning Sport Routes](https://publications.americanalpineclub.org/articles/13201216058) recommends clipping at least two quickdraws or slings to the anchor for lowering or top-roping, opposed and roughly equalized, instead of using the fixed hardware directly.
- **Some longer draws**: [REI's clipping guide](https://www.rei.com/learn/expert-advice/clip-quickdraws.html) suggests carrying two lengths. Where the rock has an edge, use a long draw so the bottom carabiner hangs completely clear of it.
- **Helmet**: [REI's falling guide](https://www.rei.com/learn/expert-advice/climbing-how-to-fall.html) notes that a helmet that is dented, cracked or more than 10 years old needs replacing.

## Risk 1: ground falls around the first few bolts

The gym floor is padded. Outdoors, the base of a route can be a slope, tree roots or boulders, and wherever you land may not be flat.

### Spotting: guide, don't catch

Until the first bolt is clipped, the belayer spots. [Petzl's tip on spotting at the start of a route](https://www.petzl.com/us/en/sport/tech-tips/basic-techniques/spotting-and-belaying-at-the-start-of-the-route/Escalade-parer-en-debut-de-voie_SPO_a77w0000000GnbDAAS.html) says the point is to guide the climber to land on their feet and not flip backwards, with hands aimed at the pelvis and upper back.

### Stick clip: on rope from the ground

[One accident report collected by the AAC](https://publications.americanalpineclub.org/articles/13201215928): a climber was leading a 5.10c at Mars Wall in Utah. The base was uneven and sloped away from the wall, with a fallen tree. Her belayer offered to spot, but she declined because the spot looked dangerous for the belayer too. She fell three times before clipping the first bolt; on the third, she landed on her ankle and somersaulted backward down the slope, fracturing her calcaneus and talus. Her own analysis: had she brought and used a stick clip, she would have been protected before the first bolt.

The report adds one option: when a stick clip isn't practical, you can often preclip the first or second bolt of a hard route by climbing and lowering from an adjacent route.

### Not only the first bolt

[A 2025 AAC accident analysis](https://americanalpineclub.org/news/2025/10/15/the-prescriptionground-fall): a climber was leading a four-bolt 5.12a at Red Rock, well below his limit, as a cool-down. He had stick-clipped the first bolt, but at the third he pulled up an armful of slack to clip, his foot slipped, and he fell about 15 feet straight onto his backside, with lumbar compression fractures and a fractured sacrum. He had considered stick-clipping beyond the first bolt "cheating" and changed his mind afterwards: "Had I stick clipped the third, this accident wouldn't have happened."

This is the same DAV rule from [Learning to Lead at the Gym](/posts/climbing/2026-10-17-gym-lead-climbing-en): a failed clip within the first five draws can put you on the ground. Outdoors, the ground is harder and less even.

**One thing to do tonight**: find out whether people at the crag you're heading to use stick clips, and where to borrow or buy one. Bring it on day one and treat it as standard gear, not something to be embarrassed about.

## Risk 2: you hang your own quickdraws

At the gym the draws are already up and you only clip the rope. Outdoors you first clip each draw to the bolt, then clip the rope. REI splits this into two steps, and the first one is new:

- **Straight gate on the bolt, bent gate on the rope.** Ready-made quickdraws usually come with one straight-gate and one bent-gate carabiner. The straight gate clips the bolt; the bent gate is shaped for clipping the rope. **Never swap the ends**: the carabiner that has been on a hanger can develop sharp edges that would damage the rope.
- **Gate direction**: the rope-end gate faces away from your direction of travel. That's the same as at the gym, except now you decide how the draw hangs.
- **Check what the carabiner touches**: after clipping, see whether the bottom carabiner hangs partway over an edge. In a fall it can pry against the edge and break, so switch to a longer draw. Also check whether a bulge could push the gate open in a fall, and use a longer or shorter draw to avoid it.

**One thing to do tonight**: take out your quickdraws and check which end is the straight gate and which is the bent gate on each one. If any are hard to tell apart, wrap a ring of tape on the straight-gate end so you don't grab it backwards on the wall.

## Risk 3: the rope is too short

Outdoor routes vary in height. If the rope is too short, the end slides out of the belay device halfway through the lower.

[REI's lead belay guide](https://www.rei.com/learn/expert-advice/belay-lead-climber.html) gives the rule: **the rope must be comfortably more than twice the distance to the top anchor**, or the climber can't be lowered to the ground. [Petzl's rope-too-short warning](https://www.petzl.com/us/en/sport/tech-tips/basic-techniques/rope-too-short--warning---danger-/CORDES-Corde-trop-courte---attention-danger--_SPO_a77w0000000PCKGAA4.html) adds two more:

- Study the topo before you go and bring a long enough rope; taking one 5 m longer than you expect to need can help.
- **For crag climbing, always tie a knot in the end of the rope.**

The knot closes the system: if the rope runs out, the knot jams at the device and the climber stops in midair instead of falling.

**One thing to do tonight**: check the rope label or manual for your rope's length. Divide it by two, subtract a few meters, and write the result on your rope bag. That is the tallest route you can lower from.

## Risk 4: rockfall and loose holds

Outdoor holds can break. The [DAV booklet](https://alpenverein.de/files/2409-indoor-klettern-basics-english_ol.pdf) reminds you that a fall is always possible, for example from a loose hold, which is why you clip every draw.

The AAC's Human Factors article advises avoiding areas with signs of recent rockfall and minimizing time at the base of steep, exposed walls. A helmet helps, but only if you are not in the direct line of fire.

In the Mars Wall accident above, the climber was wearing a helmet when she tumbled down the slope, and she believes it likely prevented a minor concussion. Rockfall is just as dangerous for the belayer standing below; [Outdoor Belaying](/posts/climbing/2026-10-17-outdoor-belay-en) covers where to stand.

**One thing to do tonight**: find the manufacturing date on the label inside your helmet. If it is more than 10 years old, or the shell is dented or cracked, replace it before your trip.

## Risk 5: ledges in the fall line

Outdoor walls have ledges, protruding blocks and corners.

- **Identify the no-fall zones.** REI recommends spotting the sections where a fall would hit something when you choose a route. Their difficulty should be well within your ability; if not, pick another route.
- **Watch traverses.** The [BMC's article on falling](https://services.thebmc.co.uk/climb-skills-falling-down) warns that swinging into a corner from 20 feet to the side of your protection hits as hard as a 20-foot vertical fall onto a ledge, and you land on your side.
- **The belayer reads it too.** REI's lead belay guide says to make a hard catch when there is a ledge or the ground in the fall line, and a soft catch only when there is free space below.

**One thing to do tonight**: take your three candidate routes and use the guidebook description or photos to mark which sections have ledges and which traverse. If you can't tell, look from the base before deciding whether to climb.

## One table: five risks and fixes

| Risk | Outdoor fix |
|---|---|
| Ground falls around the first few bolts | Belayer spots; stick-clip the first bolt, and more if the start is hard |
| Hanging your own draws | Straight gate on the bolt, bent gate on the rope, never swapped; check gate direction and edges |
| Rope too short | Rope comfortably more than twice the route height, with a few meters to spare; always knot the end |
| Rockfall and loose holds | Helmet; don't linger at the base; clip every draw |
| Ledges in the fall line | Identify no-fall zones when choosing the route; belayer decides hard or soft catch |

Beyond these five, there are two more things the gym never asks of you, each with its own post later in the series:

- **Can you trust the bolt?** Long Dong is a seaside crag and bolts corrode. The next post, [Judging Bolts](/posts/climbing/2026-10-17-judging-bolts-en), covers how to check a bolt's material and inspect it on site.
- **How do you get down from the top?** Agree on lowering or rappelling before you leave the ground. The AAC's Cleaning Sport Routes says lowering is widely accepted, even recommended, at many sport crags, but some local organizations still recommend rappelling, so check the local rules. At Long Dong, [TOCC's note on top anchors](https://www.tocc-climbing.org/post/%E9%BE%8D%E6%B4%9E%E7%9A%84%E4%B8%8A%E6%96%B9%E5%9B%BA%E5%AE%9A%E9%BB%9E) asks climbers to retreat by double-rope rappel where possible, avoid lowering, and always top-rope from their own gear. Details are in [Lower or Rappel](/posts/climbing/2026-10-17-lower-or-rappel-en).

## Adjustments for the belayer outdoors

The lead belay technique is the same as at the gym; the terrain is not:

- **Position**: REI says standing too far back swings you into the wall on a catch, and you shouldn't stand directly under the climber. Outdoor ground is uneven, so check that nothing on your path to the wall, like a rock or a root, will trip you.
- **Weight difference**: when the climber is much heavier than you, or there is an obstruction between you and the wall, REI suggests considering tying in to a ground anchor.
- **Rope prep**: REI says to flake the rope before starting to make sure there are no knots or kinks that could hang up in the device.

More outdoor belay details (position, rockfall, communication distance) are in [Outdoor Belaying](/posts/climbing/2026-10-17-outdoor-belay-en).

## Learning in Taiwan

Intro courses for outdoor leading are run by private outfitters. Two examples (checked on the official sites on 2026-10-17, pages in Chinese):

- **[ZA Adventure: Outdoor Sport Lead Climbing Course](https://www.zaadventure.com/2020/12/outdoor-rock-climbing-level-2.html)**: two days at Long Dong, NT$9,800. The prerequisite is completing a 5.9 on top-rope at a gym. Day one covers placing quickdraws (first practiced bouldering-style), lead belaying and mock leads; day two covers transitions, retreating from a lead, and solving common problems with a prusik. The page says 2026 public courses are being planned and notes that not every student will actually lead.
- **[GOFormosa: Advanced Outdoor Climbing 1, Lead Climbing and Retreat](https://www.go4mosa.net/products/rockclimbing-lead)**: two days of 6–8 hours each, at Long Dong, Re Hai crag or Defulan. One day of lead climbing and belaying, one day of transitions and retreating from a lead.

Both courses include retreating from a lead (getting down from the top), because gym lead courses don't teach it.

**One thing to do tonight**: write down your current gym lead grade, whether you have a lead belay card, and whether someone can take you outside. If you have all three, pick a weekend; if one is missing, work on that first.

## Practice: four scenarios

Answers are under each question; think first, then open. For more questions, try [nobodyclimb's rope system practice: sport lead](https://nobodyclimb.cc/games/rope-system/learn/sport-lead) (in Chinese).

**1. A sport route is 25 m tall. You brought a 50 m rope and plan to lower off after leading it.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

No. Lowering needs twice the route height, and 50 m is exactly twice 25 m. REI asks for comfortably more than that, and Petzl suggests about 5 m extra. Bring a longer rope or pick a shorter route, and knot the rope end either way.

</details>

**2. The base is scree sloping away from the wall, the first bolt is about 4 m up, and the first two moves are the hardest on the route.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Stick-clip the first bolt, and the second too if needed. The two AAC accidents above show why: one climber fractured her ankle falling before the first bolt, and another stick-clipped only the first and hit the ground at the third. The belayer should also make sure they won't slip on their own footing while spotting.

</details>

**3. You clip a quickdraw and notice the bottom carabiner sits partly over an edge.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Swap in a longer draw so the bottom carabiner hangs completely clear. REI explains that in a fall the carabiner can pry against the edge and break.

</details>

**4. You finish a sport route at Long Dong and want to lower off from the top, cleaning the draws on the way down.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Check the local rules first. TOCC asks climbers at Long Dong to retreat by double-rope rappel where possible and avoid lowering, except in emergencies such as sudden weather, lightning, earthquakes or bee stings, to reduce wear on the anchors. Whichever you do, agree on it before leaving the ground, not at the top.

</details>
