The first ten posts in this series covered what IFMGA is, how to hire a guide, what guides learn, and how each country trains them. This one answers the question Taiwanese readers end up asking: **I'm from Taiwan and I want an IFMGA qualification. How do I get there?**

The short answer: there is nothing to convert, so you have to go abroad and start from the beginning. This post puts the language, residence, cost and years of every member country into one table, then looks at how far a Canadian working holiday gets you, the closest Taiwanese cases found so far, and what you can do in Taiwan right now.

## Why there is no direct route from Taiwan

### Taiwan is not a member and has no candidate association

Taiwan is not on the [IFMGA member list](https://ifmga.net/member-associations/member-associations), and the [candidate associations page](https://ifmga.net/member-associations/candidate-associations) reads "No Pending Candidate Associations". So no association in Taiwan can train for or award the IFMGA qualification.

### Taiwan's mountain guide certificates cannot be converted

Taiwan's system is the Sports Administration's [Regulations for Mountain Guide Qualification](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawAll.aspx?pcode=H0120058) (山域嚮導資格檢定辦法). It has four separate categories, hiking, river tracing, rock climbing and snow climbing, and requires 48 hours of training within 2 years plus 8 hours of first aid. IFMGA's minimum is 94 training days ([Training and Assessment](https://ifmga.net/professional-mountain-guide/training-and-assessment)). The two are on different scales, and there is no mutual recognition.

Reciprocity applies only to "active members in good standing of an IFMGA member association" ([ACMG](https://acmg.ca/ifmga-mobility/)), meaning people who already belong to a member association. Holders of certificates from non-member countries have no conversion route.

### Foreigners can train, and the qualification belongs to the association that trained them

IFMGA Platform 3.3 says any qualified climber can start training, and "The qualified Guide will obtain IFMGA recognition when they become a member of an IFMGA Member Association". Article 3.4 says member associations "may train applicants/candidates from other countries" ([Platform, as republished by EEMGA](https://www.eemga.org/about-eemga/the-ifmga-platform/)).

So a Taiwanese candidate who finishes training in some country becomes a member of that country's association and holds IFMGA status through it. There is no Taiwanese IFMGA association to belong to.

### How "one association per country" applies to Taiwan is not stated

The IFMGA [history document](https://ifmga.net/images/Documents/240212_History_IFMGA_update.pdf) records that from 1974 only one association per country was accepted, and notes a case where it was "only possible to accept one association per country". But the IFMGA Bylaws are not public, and IFMGA has not said how "country" is defined or whether Taiwan could apply as a separate candidate association. The only way to ask is to write to IFMGA (office@ifmga.net).

The candidate process is in Platform 5.4–5.12: training supported by at least 2 member associations, evaluation by the Technical Committee, then a vote at the general assembly. Past examples:

| Country | From applicant to full member | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Nepal | 2006 → 2012 | Already had 24 IFMGA guides when it joined |
| Kyrgyzstan | 2010 → 2017 | Funded by Mammut, supported by the Swiss and British associations |
| Georgia | 2018 → 2021 | Supported by the Swiss and German associations |
| Chile | 2019 → 2024 | Sponsored by the Argentine association |

An inference: for Taiwan to form a member association, it would first need a group of Taiwanese guides who qualified abroad to act as instructors, and then several years as a candidate. That is drawn from the table above, not from IFMGA.

### How to read the table

An inference: for a Taiwanese candidate who speaks only Chinese and English, the realistic options are the English-taught ones that do not filter by nationality at enrolment: Canada's ACMG, the US AMGA, Kyrgyzstan's KMGA, and EEMGA on a case-by-case basis. Their shared problem is not enrolment but apprenticeship: most associations require guiding real clients under supervision, and that needs local work status.

Learning another language opens more doors: German for Austria, Germany and Switzerland, French for France and Switzerland, Japanese for Japan, Spanish for South America. Graduates of No Ski associations (Nepal, Peru, Bolivia, Ecuador) have no ski-guiding rights in other countries, though Switzerland's regulations include a clause letting No Ski guides take extra courses to upgrade to the full qualification.

## Canada as an example: how far a working holiday gets you

Canada is worth a closer look because [ACMG](https://acmg.ca/the-acmg-training-and-assessment-program-tap/) teaches in English, publishes its fees and states no nationality or residence requirement. But the claim that Canada is the most common route for Taiwanese climbers is not supported by any evidence found so far.

### Apprenticeship needs a work permit

ACMG apprentices must log 20 days (rock) or 30 days (alpine, ski) of supervised industry experience, and the [Scope of Practice](https://acmg.ca/app/uploads/2025/10/ACMG_SOP_2022.pdf) allows at most half of the required direct-supervision days to be field training without clients; the rest must be with clients. Canada's immigration department IRCC defines "work" to include "unpaid employment undertaken for the purpose of obtaining work experience, such as an internship" ([What is work](https://www.canada.ca/en/immigration-refugees-citizenship/corporate/publications-manuals/operational-bulletins-manuals/temporary-residents/foreign-workers/what-is-work.html)). So with or without pay, apprenticeship needs a work permit.

### IEC working holiday: 12 months, once in a lifetime

Young Taiwanese can apply for an [IEC](https://www.canada.ca/en/immigration-refugees-citizenship/services/work-canada/iec/eligibility.html) working holiday: ages 18–35, an open work permit, up to 12 months. Taiwan's [Ministry of Foreign Affairs youth site](https://youthtaiwan.mofa.gov.tw/WorkingHoliday/Cus_WHNation.aspx?n=6386&s=113459) states that each Taiwanese can participate in IEC only once, across all three categories.

Twelve months is not enough for the full ACMG programme. IRCC says that before an IEC permit expires you can apply for "another type of work permit (not IEC)" or a study permit ([IRCC Help Centre](https://ircc.canada.ca/english/helpcentre/answer.asp?qnum=1476&top=25)). So IEC can be followed by an employer-specific work permit or a study permit, but not by a second IEC.

### Other sticking points

- **The school route**: Thompson Rivers University's Adventure Guide diploma and certificate are not eligible for a post-graduation work permit (PGWP); the four-year Bachelor of Tourism Management (Adventure Studies) is ([TRU international programmes](https://tru.ca/truworld/future-students/programs.html)).
- **Avalanche training**: both the Alpine and Ski final exams require CAA Operations Level 2, which in turn requires 100 operational days first; the course and assessment cost about CAD 6,700 together ([CAA](https://avalancheassociation.ca/itp/avalanche-operations-level-2)). That is another stretch of working in Canada.

An inference: the more workable use of IEC is to log supervised apprenticeship days during those 12 months, with another work permit or study permit for the rest. Whether permits can be chained in practice is decided by IRCC case by case.

## The closest Taiwanese cases found so far

Searches of the JMGA list, IFMGA-related news, Chinese, English and Japanese web searches, and Taiwanese communities (PTT, Dcard, Hiking Biji) turned up **no publicly self-identified Taiwanese IFMGA guide**. Not finding one does not prove there is none: IFMGA's own guide-verification system requires a login, and Facebook was not searched from inside an account.

The closest cases:

- A Taiwanese climber profiled by [Hanchor in 2026](https://www.hanchor.com/news/台灣攀岩人物誌二十五-冠瑋wei-gw-chen) holds ACMG **Apprentice Rock Guide** and plans to return to Canada to finish the full Rock Guide exam, and later the Ski Guide. That is an apprentice qualification in the Canadian system, not IFMGA. The profile also notes that Taiwan is not part of IFMGA.
- An AMGA rock and alpine guide working in Taiwan is Singaporean and aims to become the first IFMGA guide in Southeast Asia ([Taiwan Rock Climbing](https://taiwanrockclimbing.com/about)).

## What you can prepare in Taiwan

Most of what you can do before leaving is building up the things you will hand in when you apply. The six items below cover paperwork and certificates; for where to train the skills themselves (sport, trad, snow, ice, glacier and ski, and which parts Taiwan, Japan, Korea, New Zealand and the Alps can each cover), see the full training map in [what to train before applying](/posts/climbing/2026-10-18-ifmga-pre-entry-prep-en).

### 1. Take Taiwan's mountain guide certificates

Under the Regulations for Mountain Guide Qualification, the hiking, river tracing, rock climbing and snow climbing categories can be taken separately. The practical exam fees are NT$3,000 for hiking, NT$5,000 for rock climbing and NT$10,000 for snow climbing. They do not convert to IFMGA, but they are Taiwan's current formal guide qualification and a starting point for building guiding experience at home.

### 2. Start a route log today

IFMGA's entry threshold is at least 55 varied routes (rock, ski mountaineering, ice and so on). Associations also apply their own time windows:

- JMGA counts only the last 15 years and excludes trips as a client
- AMGA requires most items within the last 5 years, so older climbs expire ([AMGA Rock Guide Program](https://www.amga.com/programs/mountain-guide-programs/rock-guide-program))
- ACMG's rock training course asks for 20 sport routes flashed in the past year and 15 multi-pitch trad routes ([Rock Guide Certificate](https://acmg.ca/rock-guide-certificate/))

How: keep a spreadsheet with date, location, grade, number of pitches, lead or follow, and partners for every route. Keep guided days separate from personal climbing.

### 3. Get a WFR first-aid certificate

ACMG's training courses require a WFR, and Chile's ANGM requires a WFR of at least 80 hours. NOLS charges about US$745–1,350 for its WFR ([NOLS](https://www.nols.edu/courses/wm/wilderness-first-responder-wfr/)). This post did not check whether any WFR course in Taiwan is accepted by these associations; ask your target association before you book.

### 4. Take avalanche courses

AMGA requires Pro 1 and Pro 2 avalanche training; Silverton Avalanche School's Pro 1, for example, costs US$1,799–2,399 ([Silverton](https://www.avyschool.org/professional)). ACMG requires Canadian Avalanche Association courses. JMGA accepts JAN Level 1 for an avalanche exam exemption. No such course offered in Taiwan was found, so plan time abroad (an inference).

### 5. Learn the language of your target country

English is the minimum. Learn German for Austria, Germany or Switzerland, French for France, a high level of Japanese for Japan (an inference: exam documents and textbooks are only in Japanese), Spanish for South America, and at least B1 Norwegian for Norway.

## Bottom line

The realistic route for a Taiwanese climber is to pick a country whose language you can learn and whose residence you can obtain, and live there for several years. Before you leave, what Taiwan can give you is certificates, a route log, first aid and language.
