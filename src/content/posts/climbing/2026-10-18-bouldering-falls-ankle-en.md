The mats in the bouldering area are thick and soft, so letting go from the top feels harmless. Ask people in an emergency department where their bouldering injury is, though, and the answer is usually the ankle.

The previous posts covered the upper body, from fingers to shoulders. This one moves to the legs: which bouldering falls are most likely to hurt, how to come down more safely, where pads hide traps, and when a twisted ankle needs an X-ray.

This is part 6 of the [Climbing Injuries and First Aid series](/posts/climbing/2026-10-18-climbing-injury-overview-en). General first-response principles and which specialist to see are in the series overview.

## Why bouldering injuries land in the legs

The answer to "where do climbers get hurt" depends on where the data come from.

- **Questionnaires following ordinary boulderers**: [Auer et al.'s 2021 prospective study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33966976/) followed 507 German-speaking indoor boulderers for a year. The upper body accounted for 63% of injuries and the legs only 23%. But leg injuries were more severe, and more often caused by falling or jumping off the wall than injuries elsewhere.
- **Emergency departments**: at [a Level 1 trauma centre in Munich](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35144805/), 430 bouldering patients over 10 years, the most commonly injured region was the ankle, at 36.7%. The authors say finger injuries were barely seen in the emergency department.
- **Major trauma**: among bouldering patients at [a UK major trauma centre](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42155532/), the most common diagnosis was ankle fracture, at 40%.
- **Gym accident reports**: the [German Alpine Club (DAV) 2025 climbing gym accident statistics](https://www.alpenverein.de/verband/bergsport/sicherheitsforschung/kletterhallen-unfallstatistik/kletterhallenunfallstatistik-2025) received 174 bouldering accidents, 55% of them leg injuries. Among 21- to 40-year-olds, the dominant pattern was rolling an ankle on landing.

Finger injuries tend to go to outpatient clinics; broken ankles go straight to the emergency department. Both datasets are right; they just see different people.

<details><summary>More numbers</summary>

- Auer 2021: leg injuries had 2.23 times the odds of being rated UIAA grade 2 or higher (moderate or worse), and 4.39 times the odds of being caused by jumping off.
- Munich trauma centre: legs 61.1% in total; sprains 53.0%, fractures 22.8%, dislocations 11.9%; 19.9% needed surgery.
- UK trauma centre: 176 patients, mean fall height 2.9 m, 24% needed surgery, and ankle fracture fixation made up 41% of all operations.
- [US emergency data (Buzzacott 2019)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30857822/): the ankle was the most commonly fractured body part, 27% of fractures; falls caused 60% of all injuries.

</details>

**Something you can do tonight**: think back to the last three times you came off a boulder. Did you climb down, jump from low, or let go from the top? If all three were the last kind, the rest of this post is for you.

## Which falls are most dangerous

[Beurienne et al. (2025)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12312403/) asked 245 French adults who were injured while bouldering how they fell, where they landed, and what they hurt.

Some findings:

- **85% of injurious falls were involuntary**: foot slips, missed holds, and failed dynamic moves made up most of them.
- **The legs accounted for 67% of injuries**, and the most common single diagnosis was an ankle sprain.
- **The riskiest scenario**: falling from the upper part of a vertical or steep wall, staying upright without rotating, and landing on the feet, injuring the ankle. The authors suggest the feet sink into the pad on impact, letting the ankle roll in a long way.

Two limits are worth knowing. It is a recall questionnaire, so details blur for older injuries. And two of the authors work for Tyyny, a climbing mat manufacturer.

The first finding is the most useful one. Landing technique is designed for falls you choose. Most injurious falls are ones you have no time to prepare for. So the main lever is reducing how often you fall unplanned from high up, which is what downclimbing is for.

There is also a newer injury pattern. [Eichler et al.'s 2024 case report](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38049098/) says the authors are seeing more and more leg injuries after competition-style run-and-jump moves. That is a clinical observation without incidence data. For how to train dynamic moves, see [Dynamic Moves](/posts/climbing/2026-10-18-climbing-dynamic-moves-en).

**Something you can do tonight**: before you try a problem on a vertical wall, find two things first: the downclimb after you top it, and roughly which pad you would land on if you came off halfway.

## Downclimb first, then think about landing

The DAV's [bouldering booklet for children and teenagers](https://www.alpenverein.de/files/3610003_dav_boulderschein_brosch_148x210_online.pdf) (in German) is blunt: "The mat is harder than you think. Downclimbing is always safer than jumping." When downclimbing you can use every hold on the wall, whatever its colour; some gyms set dedicated downclimb holds, which the DAV says are usually grey.

The DAV's 2025 accident report makes the same recommendation: lower your jump-off height where you can, and downclimb where possible.

If you have to jump, the booklet teaches a landing it calls "Babyboxer":

1. Climb as low as you can first, and check the landing zone is clear of people and objects.
2. Feet roughly hip- to shoulder-width apart, landing on both at once.
3. Bend your knees as if you were sitting down.
4. Roll backward with the momentum.
5. Arms bent and close to your body, hands in fists.

[Climbing magazine's bouldering safety guide](https://climbing.com/skills/learn-to-climb-safer-landings-for-bouldering) adds two points. First, do not try to stick the landing; roll with the fall. Second, hug your arms to your chest and do not reach down to catch yourself, because landing on an outstretched arm can injure it.

These are association guidelines and practitioner advice, and no study has shown they reduce injury rates. In Auer 2021, none of the potential preventive measures evaluated showed a statistically significant protective effect, and [Josephsen's 2007 bouldering study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18076293/) also found traditional preventive measures largely ineffective. Downclimbing and landing technique are still sensible; just do not assume that learning them means you will not get hurt.

**Something you can do tonight**: pick the easiest problem in the gym, climb halfway up, and downclimb all the way. Then, from less than a metre off the mat, practise the five Babyboxer steps three times, including the backward roll.

## Pad gaps and spotting

[Schöffl and Küpper's 2013 review of foot injuries](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3801241/) describes a typical indoor bouldering injury: falling onto the mats and the foot getting stuck between two of them. The review notes that the International Federation of Sport Climbing (IFSC) now requires these intersections to be closed in competitions.

Outdoors the gap problem is more obvious. Climbing magazine says cracks between crash pads are by far the bigger problem, because a foot can go through to hard ground. A thin pad laid over them helps, but it can slide when landed on and can also hide the gaps.

On spotting:

- The DAV considers spotting usually unnecessary in fully matted gyms.
- When you do spot, the goal is for the climber to land feet first on the mat, not to catch them. The DAV booklet says you cannot catch a falling person.
- Climbing magazine describes it as arms up, elbows bent, ready to take the climber's hips or waist and push them toward the safest part of the landing zone.

**Something you can do tonight**: walk the bouldering area and look for three things: seams or splits between mats, gaps between the mat and the wall, and water bottles or phones left on the mat. Move them, or tell the staff.

## Ankle sprains, fractures, and the heel: when to see a doctor

### Do not decide for yourself whether it is a sprain or a fracture

The usual first thought after rolling an ankle is "it's just a sprain." Emergency doctors decide whether to X-ray using the [Ottawa ankle rules](https://aci.health.nsw.gov.au/ecat/appendices/ottawa-ankle-adult). Any one of the following means an X-ray:

- Tenderness at the back edge (lower 6 cm) or tip of the outer or inner ankle bone
- Tenderness at the base of the fifth metatarsal (the bump halfway along the outside of the foot) or the navicular (top of the inner midfoot)
- Unable to walk four steps right after the injury

This is a tool for clinicians, not a way to rule out a fracture at home. Treat it as a reminder: if any one of these applies, go to the emergency department or an orthopaedic clinic and let a doctor decide.

### After a sprain

The [2018 Dutch clinical guideline on ankle sprains](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/52/15/956) makes three points:

- How badly the ligaments are injured is assessed most reliably 4 to 5 days after the injury.
- RICE (rest, ice, compression, elevation) on its own has no evidence of improving pain, swelling, or function.
- If the ankle is immobilised, it should be for at most 10 days, followed by a brace plus exercise therapy.

For what to do at the moment of injury and the debate over ice, see the [series overview](/posts/climbing/2026-10-18-climbing-injury-overview-en).

### A sprain that still hurts after 6 weeks

Schöffl and Küpper warn that a sprain sometimes comes with an osteochondral lesion of the talus, which the first X-ray often misses. If ankle pain persists beyond 6 weeks and the X-ray is normal, the authors consider an MRI indicated. In one case they describe, a 20-year-old woman sprained her ankle landing from a boulder and was diagnosed 1.5 years later.

### Heel (calcaneus) fractures

A fall landing on the heels from height can break the calcaneus. No study has looked specifically at calcaneus fractures in climbers. The same review states that a ground fall from as little as 8 feet (about 2.4 m) can cause serious fractures of the calcaneus, talus, and ankle. The authors also say that because climbers are mostly young and very active, they favour surgery for most talus and calcaneus fractures. That is expert opinion.

A swollen, painful heel you cannot stand on means going straight to the emergency department.

<details><summary>Return to bouldering: proportions and time</summary>

- Munich trauma centre follow-up: 75.8% of men and 59.5% of women returned to bouldering; people who rated themselves less experienced were less likely to return.
- [Lum and Park (2019)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31024194/) surveyed 237 recreational climbers about all their climbing injuries, not only ankles: those treated without surgery returned in 3.9 months on average, those with surgery in 9.1 months.

These are group averages, not a timeline for you.

</details>

**Something you can do tonight**: stand on one leg, then the other, and compare which side wobbles more. The Dutch guideline lists a failed single-leg balance test as a risk factor for ankle sprains and says coordination and balance training prevent repeat sprains (evidence from general sports populations). If the side you have sprained before is clearly wobblier, a physiotherapist can set up balance training for you.

## Practice: four scenarios

Each answer is below the question. Think first, then look.

**1. A friend tops a problem on a vertical wall and lets go from the top. "The mats are thick."**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Both the DAV booklet and its accident report recommend downclimbing whenever possible, and climbing low first if you must jump. Falling upright from the upper part of a vertical wall and landing on both feet is exactly the scenario Beurienne found most likely to injure ankles. Suggest they try downclimbing the whole way next time.

</details>

**2. You land on a seam between mats and roll your ankle. You can limp, but pressing the tip of the outer ankle bone hurts.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Tenderness at the tip of the ankle bone meets one of the Ottawa criteria for an X-ray. Go to the emergency department or an orthopaedic clinic and let a doctor decide; being able to walk does not make it a sprain. Tell the gym where the seam is, too.

</details>

**3. Two months ago you sprained your ankle landing from a boulder. The X-ray showed no fracture, but your ankle still hurts after climbing.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Go back to the orthopaedic clinic. Schöffl and Küpper warn that osteochondral lesions of the talus are often invisible on the first X-ray, and that an MRI is indicated when ankle pain lasts beyond 6 weeks. Whether to scan, and how, is the doctor's call.

</details>

**4. A climbing partner says: "Learn to land properly and you won't get hurt."**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

In Beurienne's survey, 85% of injurious falls were involuntary, with no time to set up a landing; Auer and Josephsen found no statistically significant protective effect from the preventive measures they looked at. Landing technique is worth practising, but more reliable is falling from high up less often: downclimb, lower your jump height, and watch for pad gaps.

</details>
