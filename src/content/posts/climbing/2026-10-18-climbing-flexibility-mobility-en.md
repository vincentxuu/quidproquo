You're on a gym boulder that needs a high step, and your foot just won't go up there. A friend says: "You're too stiff. Go home and stretch, it'll stop you getting injured too."

That sentence makes two claims: flexibility will make you climb better, and stretching will make you get hurt less. Research answers them differently. This post covers how much flexibility affects climbing performance, then what we know about stretching and injury, and ends with a 10-minute hip and shoulder routine.

This is part 12 of the Climbing Injuries and First Aid series. For getting back on the wall after an injury, see the previous post, [Returning to Climbing After Injury](/posts/climbing/2026-10-18-return-to-climbing-en); for the warm-up research, see [Climbing Warm-Up](/posts/climbing/2026-10-18-climbing-warm-up-en).

## Flexibility vs mobility

The two words are often used interchangeably. In this post:

- **Flexibility**: how far a joint can be passively moved. Reaching for your toes while sitting on the floor is flexibility.
- **Mobility**: the range you can actively move through and control. Getting your foot onto a high hold and then shifting your weight over it to stand up is mobility.

Climbing needs the second one more. [Lattice Training's lower-body warm-up and cool-down routine](https://latticetraining.com/workout/9cb29eb7-7bd8-44d8-b0d9-ad8d554c5823/warm-up-and-cool-down) includes an isometric horse stance, described as building "adductor and lower body strength at end range". That is exactly "able to get there, and able to hold it".

**Something you can do tonight**: sit on the floor with your legs as wide as they'll go and note the distance between your heels. Then stand, hold a wall, lift one leg out to the side as high as you can and hold for 5 seconds. A big gap between the two means you can get into the range but can't yet produce force there.

## How much does flexibility matter for climbing?

### It matters, but less than you'd think

[Mermier et al. (2000)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11049146/) tested 44 climbers graded 5.6 to 5.13c, including hip and shoulder flexibility, and looked at which factors best explained their climbing scores. The variables fell into three components:

- **Training-related** (strength, endurance and so on): 58.9% of the variance in performance.
- **Flexibility**: 1.8%.
- **Anthropometry** (height, arm span and so on): 0.3%.

[MacKenzie et al. (2020)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31094249/) tested 77 climbers. Shoulder power and endurance were the main determinants; flexibility, alongside balance and core endurance, was listed as an "important secondary determinant". In men, hip, hamstring and lower-back flexibility correlated with ability; in women, no flexibility measure did.

### Enough is enough

The International Rock Climbing Research Association (IRCRA) published a 10-item sport-specific test battery in [Draper et al. (2021)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33652414/), with 132 climbers from 7 countries. The flexibility and strength tests only partly separated climbers of different ability; the finger hang and powerslap tests did that best.

The authors suggest climbing may require a threshold level of flexibility, beyond which further improvement may not help. That is the authors' interpretation of their data, not something they measured directly.

### General tests miss it; test in climbing positions

The sit-and-reach is the classic school fitness test. [Draper et al. (2009)](https://doi.org/10.1080/24748668.2009.11868465) compared several tests:

- Existing tests such as the sit-and-reach correlated poorly with climbing ability.
- A foot-loading test on a purpose-built climbing board correlated most strongly.

[Draga et al. (2020)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32272571/) tested 60 elite men climbing 7b–9a and found something different: the sitting and standing straddle tests (hip abduction) correlated with ability, while the authors' climbing-specific tests did not. What the two studies share: **the joint that matters is the hip**, measured in positions close to climbing.

<details><summary>More numbers</summary>

- Draper 2009: foot-loading test r = 0.65; lateral foot reach r = 0.30; adapted Grant foot raise r = 0.34.
- Draga 2020: straddle stand r = −0.48, straddle sit r = −0.41 (the tests measure the distance from the pubic bone to the floor or wall, so a smaller number means a wider straddle, hence the negative sign).
- [Grant et al. (1996)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8887209/): among 30 men, the elite group's leg span, adjusted for body mass and leg length, was about 139 cm, versus about 122–124 cm for recreational climbers and non-climbers.

</details>

**Something you can do tonight**: next time you can't reach a foothold at the gym, try two things first: move your body slightly away from the wall, and turn your hip towards that foot. If changing position gets you there, what you're missing is technique, not flexibility. For turning techniques, see [Turning and Twisting in Climbing](/posts/climbing/2026-10-18-climbing-turning-twisting-en).

## Does stretching prevent injury?

### Climbing research: no association

[Quarmby et al. (2023)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38162697/) reviewed 34 studies on overuse injuries in adult climbers. Stretching, warm-up and cool-down, taping and hydration were not associated with overuse injury risk.

The stretching finding rests on two studies, one of them prospective. That isn't much evidence, but it points the same way as research in other sports. The authors also note that long static stretches (over 60 seconds) may reduce muscle performance, so they consider it problematic to advise climbers to stretch at length before training or competition.

### Other sports: strength training beats stretching

[Lauersen et al. (2014)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24100287/) pooled 25 randomised controlled trials with more than 26,000 participants across many sports, not climbing. Stretching had no protective effect; strength training cut sports injuries to less than a third.

Climbing has evidence pointing the same way: in Quarmby's review, a strength training intervention prevented shoulder and elbow injuries. For a routine, see [Antagonist and Core Training](/posts/climbing/2026-10-18-climbing-antagonist-core-en).

**Something you can do tonight**: next time someone says "stretch more and you won't get hurt", try: "Climbing research hasn't found a link between stretching and injury; if you want to prevent injury, strength training has more evidence." Then give your weekly training time to strength work first.

## The mobility climbing actually uses: hips and shoulders

If it isn't for injury prevention, mobility training has one job: letting your body get into the positions a route asks for.

### Hips: most footwork depends on them

- **High steps**: the foothold is above hip height, so the hip has to flex that far and then let you move your weight over it.
- **Turning in and drop knees**: the hip needs internal and external rotation. [Turning and Twisting in Climbing](/posts/climbing/2026-10-18-climbing-turning-twisting-en) notes that the wider apart the footholds, the more hip flexibility you need.
- **Wide bridging and frog positions**: getting your hips close to a vertical wall takes hip abduction, the direction Draga 2020 measured.
- **Heel hooks**: a high heel hook needs hamstrings that can lengthen. For heel hooks and knee injuries, see [The Knee and the Heel Hook](/posts/climbing/2026-10-18-climbing-knee-heel-hook-en).

### Shoulders: less research

The 2000 study above did measure shoulder flexibility; it's folded into that 1.8%. A [2025 randomised crossover trial from a Kaohsiung Medical University team](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11877290/) had 22 recreational climbers do three 10-minute warm-ups: dynamic stretching with a resistance band, vibration rolling, and a climbing-specific warm-up. All three immediately increased shoulder flexion and internal and external rotation range; vibration rolling increased it most. The study only measured immediate changes, not performance or injury over time.

**Something you can do tonight**: at the gym, pick an easy route you've done before and deliberately place every foot a bit higher. Whichever position feels most stuck goes first on your mobility list.

## A 10-minute mobility routine

The hip exercises come from Lattice's lower-body routine (about 14 minutes in full); the shoulder exercises come from the band warm-up in the Kaohsiung trial. **Evidence level: exercises from a coaching routine and a warm-up study; no research has tested this routine's long-term effect.** The time per exercise is this post's allocation; Lattice doesn't give per-exercise timings.

| Order | Exercise | Time | Key points (per source) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Lying internal rotation | 1 min | On your back, knees bent about 90°, feet wider than hips; let the knees fall gently inward; wider feet, bigger stretch |
| 2 | Pigeon pose | 1 min each side | Front shin angled across your body, back leg straight behind; lean over the front leg; keep hips as square as possible |
| 3 | Seated hamstring stretch | 1 min each side | One leg straight; lead with your chest towards the foot; hinge at the hip rather than rounding the back |
| 4 | Horse stance isometric | 1 min | Feet much wider than shoulders, sink into a deep squat and hold; torso upright, knees not caving in |
| 5 | Frog | 1 min | From kneeling, knees wide and feet together behind you; lower the hips and let gravity deepen the stretch |
| 6 | Band external rotation | 1 min | Upper arm by your side, rotate the forearm outward |
| 7 | Band scapular retraction | 1 min | Pull the band with both hands and squeeze the shoulder blades back |

About 10 minutes in total. Where to put it:

- **Before climbing**: as part of the warm-up, with shorter, more dynamic holds. Quarmby's review cautions that long static stretches may affect performance.
- **After climbing or on rest days**: hold longer. Lattice describes this routine as suitable for either warm-up or cool-down.

Mobility work shouldn't hurt. If you get sharp pain, numbness or a catching feeling in a joint while stretching, stop and get it assessed by a doctor (orthopaedics, rehabilitation medicine) or a physiotherapist.

**Something you can do tonight**: do the routine once and note your two tightest exercises. In a month, try the same high step on the same route and see whether anything has changed.

## Practice: four scenarios

Answers are below each question. Think first, then check.

**1. You can't touch your toes in a sit-and-reach, and a friend says that's why you're stuck at V3.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The sit-and-reach correlates poorly with climbing ability (Draper 2009). What matters is hip mobility in climbing positions. In the research, strength and endurance mainly determine ability; flexibility is secondary. Check whether technique, finger strength or shoulder endurance is what's holding you back; see [Plateaued in Climbing, Should You Start Training?](/posts/climbing/2026-10-18-climbing-training-diagnosis-en).

</details>

**2. You do 15 minutes of static stretching before every session because you heard it prevents injury.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Climbing research hasn't found a link between stretching and overuse injury (Quarmby 2023), and long static stretches may reduce performance. Before climbing, swap that time for a dynamic warm-up and progressive easy climbing; move static stretching to after the session or rest days. See [Climbing Warm-Up](/posts/climbing/2026-10-18-climbing-warm-up-en).

</details>

**3. On a vertical wall you can never reach a big foothold out to the side, but on the floor your straddle is wide.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

You can get into the range but may not be able to produce force there, or your position puts your hips too far from the wall. Try turning your hips into the wall and getting your body closer, and add end-range strength work such as the isometric horse stance.

</details>

**4. You feel a sharp pain at the front of your hip during pigeon pose, and for two weeks high steps have hurt too.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Mobility work shouldn't hurt. Pain that has lasted two weeks and is also triggered by climbing should be assessed by an orthopaedic or rehabilitation doctor or a physiotherapist; don't try to fix it by stretching harder. Avoid the movements that provoke it in the meantime.

</details>
