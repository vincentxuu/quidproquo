It's Wednesday, you worked until nine, and the gym is out. You look at your doorframe and wonder: if I put up a pull-up bar and buy a hangboard, can I train at home?

You can, but the rules at home are different from the gym. The most common mistake is replacing a two-hour bouldering session with a two-hour hangboard session. This post covers what to buy, what each piece of kit trains, and how to lay out a 30-minute session.

First, the level of evidence. **There is no dedicated research on home training.** Everything below is coaching consensus, mainly from [Lattice Training](https://ukclimbing.com/articles/skills/series/lattice_home_training), [Power Company Climbing](https://powercompanyclimbing.com/blog/covid-19-climbing-training-fingers), the [BMC](https://thebmc.co.uk/en/how-to-fingerboard) and physical therapist [Jason Hooper](https://hoopersbeta.com/library/one-routine-once-a-week-climbing-training). Hangboarding itself does have research behind it; that's in the [hangboard post](/posts/climbing/2026-10-18-hangboard-training-en).

If you're not sure you should be training at all yet, start with [the first post in the series](/posts/climbing/2026-10-18-climbing-training-diagnosis-en), on whether a plateau calls for training.

## Before you start: one evening of climbing is not one evening of hangboarding

Lattice's Tom Randall puts it bluntly in his [home training article on UKClimbing](https://ukclimbing.com/articles/skills/series/lattice_home_training): two hours of bouldering cannot be swapped for two hours on a fingerboard. The hangboard concentrates load on your fingers far more than climbing does, so volume has to come down. His rule of thumb: **until you're experienced, keep a fingerboard session under 20 minutes.**

Power Company's Kris Hampton comes at it from another angle: don't replace all your climbing time with hanging at first, because your shoulders and elbows haven't been prepared for it. Early on, hangboarding can be less than half of your total training time, then build up gradually.

Keep your expectations realistic too. Taiwanese climber JOVA wrote up his own experience on [Vocus (in Chinese)](https://vocus.cc/article/6684a44dfd897800016f7f79): after tearing ankle ligaments he couldn't climb for three months, so he trained his fingers at home with a doorframe bar and a lifting block. Back at the gym, he sent most V3s in one or two goes and just missed a V4. His conclusion: finger training helps you maintain, but finger strength alone is nowhere near enough to improve. That's one person's log, not a study, but it points the same way as the coaches.

**Something you can do tonight**: Open your calendar and circle the evenings next week you won't make it to the gym. Next to each one, write "20 minutes", not "2 hours".

## Equipment: the minimum to buy

The minimum is a pull-up bar plus something to hang from. In [Lattice's training app](http://latticetraining.com/blog/what-equipment-do-i-need-to-do-a-latticeplan), the default home setup is exactly those two: a pull-up bar and a hangboard. Their principle: they would rather prescribe a slightly less "optimal" workout you can actually complete than a perfect one your home can't support.

JOVA bought a doorframe pull-up bar and the simplest lifting block he could find; he says the total was under NT$2,000.

Power Company goes cheaper still: a single hardwood strip (oak is fine) screwed above a doorway is a hangboard. Sand the edge round before you hang on it.

<details><summary>Power Company's suggested edge depths</summary>

Rough edge depth for training, by your current level:

| Level | Workout edge | Warm-up edge |
|---|---|---|
| Up to 5.11 or V3 | 25 mm | Something larger |
| Mid 5.11 to 5.12- or V4–V5 | 20 mm | 25 mm |
| Mid 5.12 to 5.13- or V6–V7 | 15 mm | 20 mm |
| Mid 5.13 and up or V8–V9+ | 10 mm | 20 mm |

The author calls these rough guidelines to get started.

</details>

A resistance band isn't essential, but it's handy: you can stand in it to take weight off a hang, and use it for shoulder external rotation (which the [next post](/posts/climbing/2026-10-18-climbing-antagonist-core-en) uses).

**Something you can do tonight**: Walk around your home and find a doorframe or wall that can take a bar or a wooden strip, with room underneath for a chair. If there isn't one, a lifting block needs no mounting at all.

## Hangboard: how to offload and how to hang at home

At home there's no coach and no jugs to warm up on, so the first skill is **taking weight off**.

- **Feet on a chair**: Lattice's favourite hack is to put a chair, box or stack of pads a meter or less in front of the board and hang with your feet on it. The closer your feet are to directly under you, the lower the intensity.
- **Toes on the floor**: Lattice's advice for beginners is longer, lighter hangs, [for example 20 seconds with a foot left on the floor](https://latticetraining.com/blog/the-beginners-guide), so the tendons adapt slowly.
- **Resistance band**: Hang with your feet in a band. Intensity is harder to judge this way, so go by feel.
- **Lifting block**: [kateberry's blog (in Chinese)](http://kateberrysblog.blogspot.com/2026/03/climbingtraining1.html) notes that many people don't hang at all; they load plates under a lifting block and pick it up with their fingers.

For the warm-up, use Power Company's approach: on a larger edge, feet on the floor, gradually add more bodyweight until you're ready to hang.

For the hang itself, the [BMC's starter exercise](https://thebmc.co.uk/en/how-to-fingerboard) is: four fingers, open hand, hang 7 seconds, rest 3, six times. Keep a slight bend in the arms so they're engaged; hanging purely on your elbow and shoulder joints leads to injury.

Mix your grips. Lattice points out that fingerboard edges are all the same size and shape, so using one grip all the time keeps loading the same spot. Be extra conservative with new grips: most non-advanced climbers should train a new grip no more than once a week in the first month. For grip names, see [Hold Types and Grips](/posts/climbing/2026-10-18-climbing-holds-grips-en).

How to choose between MaxHangs and repeaters, and how long you should climb before using a hangboard, are disputed questions; the [hangboard post](/posts/climbing/2026-10-18-hangboard-training-en) sets the views side by side. When the BMC asked Dave MacLeod, he said "grade is irrelevant; it's the circumstances that count": for busy people who can't get to a wall several times a week, fingerboarding can suit even relative novices. He also says the real question is: **are you fingerboarding when you'd be better off doing more bouldering?**

**Something you can do tonight**: Put a chair in front of your hangboard, feet on it, and do one set of the BMC's 7-on, 3-off × 6 on the biggest edge. Afterwards your fingers should feel worked, not wrecked.

## The bar: pull-ups and lock-offs

[Lattice's upper-body article](https://ukclimbing.com/articles/series/lattice_home_training/training_the_upper_body_at_home+tom_randall_qa-12784) says that if you could do only one exercise, it would probably be the pull-up. It lists three types:

- **Pull-ups**: palms facing away, hands slightly wider than shoulder width. A wider grip puts more load on the lats.
- **Lock-offs**: pull to an angle and hold. If pull-ups are hard for you, two-arm lock-offs are a good first progression. Train at roughly 90° and 125° of elbow flexion; **avoid the fully locked top position**, where the elbow takes the most stress.
- **Chin-ups**: palms facing you, hands at shoulder width. More biceps, and useful for steeper climbing.

Power Company's rule: limit yourself to 10–12 total challenging reps of an exercise per session. If it feels easy, make it harder, for example by pausing at the hardest point and taking a few slow breaths.

**Something you can do tonight**: Try one two-arm lock-off on the bar: bend to about 90°, hold for a count of five, then lower slowly. If you can't hold it, put your feet on a chair to help.

## The floor: press-ups and core

Climbing is mostly pulling. The Lattice article notes that press-ups train the pectorals and triceps, and the triceps are an important antagonist to the biceps. It also flags how often press-ups are done badly:

1. Hands under your shoulders, elbows back; don't flare them out or shrug toward your ears.
2. Brace your abs and squeeze your glutes so your hips don't rise.
3. Don't let your head sag; go down until your chin touches the floor.
4. If it's too hard, put your hands on a chair or bench. The author prefers this to kneeling because you keep tension all the way to your toes.

For core, Lattice recommends variety rather than the same few exercises all season; Hooper's home circuit uses plank shoulder taps, side planks and hollow rocks. Why climbers need pushing and core work, and how to turn it into a once-a-week prevention routine, is the subject of the next post, [Antagonists, Core and General Strength](/posts/climbing/2026-10-18-climbing-antagonist-core-en).

**Something you can do tonight**: With your hands on a chair, do five press-ups while someone films you from the side. Check three things: are your elbows flaring, are your hips rising, is your head sagging?

## A 30-minute session: an example

Below is an example that combines the principles above. It isn't anyone's official program. The ordering rule comes from Lattice: **the hangboard always goes first**; don't train fingers when you're tired.

| Block | Approx. time | Content | Based on |
|---|---|---|---|
| Warm-up | 8 min | Feet on the floor, gradually loading a big edge until you are ready to hang | Power Company |
| Hangboard | 10 min | Feet on a chair, 7 s on / 3 s off × 6, 2–3 sets, rest until your fingers recover | BMC, Lattice |
| Bar | 6 min | Pull-ups or two-arm lock-offs, no more than 10–12 challenging reps each | Lattice, Power Company |
| Floor | 6 min | Press-ups, plank shoulder taps, side plank | Lattice, Hooper's Beta |

For the week, Lattice's home rules include three that matter here:

- **Don't do two strength sessions on consecutive days.** A new session can take 2–3 days to recover from.
- **If you're doing two kinds of session in a day, split them**, e.g. hangboard in the morning and core in the evening. Power Company suggests the same split to break up the monotony of training at home.
- **Take a lighter week every few weeks.** Lattice suggests dropping volume to half or less every 4th week; older climbers may want every 3rd week.

For the year, see [Periodization](/posts/climbing/2026-10-18-climbing-periodization-en).

**Something you can do tonight**: Do the table above once, and at the end of each block write down on your phone how many sets and reps you did and where your feet were. Next time, repeat from that log; that's how you'll know whether you're improving.

## When to stop

Power Company's reminder is simple: when you're new to hanging, monitor your fingers, elbows and shoulders, and don't push extra hard just because you're stuck at home.

If a finger gets a sharp pain during a hang, or the inside of your elbow or the front of your shoulder still hurts the next day, stop that exercise. Injury assessment and management will be covered in the injury prevention series (in progress); for persistent pain or swelling, see a doctor or physical therapist.

**Something you can do tonight**: After each session, rate your fingers, elbows and shoulders from 0 to 10. If one is higher than usual twice in a row, cut the exercises that load it next time.

## Practice: four scenarios

Answers are under each question. Think first, then check.

**1. You normally boulder for two hours on Tuesday and Thursday. This Thursday you can't go, so you plan to make up for it with two hours on the hangboard.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Don't. Hangboarding concentrates far more load on your fingers than climbing; Lattice recommends keeping sessions under 20 minutes until you're experienced. Do one full 30-minute session on Thursday (with only about 10 minutes on the hangboard) and spend the rest of the time resting.

</details>

**2. You just bought a hangboard but can't hang your full weight even on the biggest edge.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Offload instead of forcing it. Put a chair in front of the board and stand on it, or leave your toes on the floor, or use a lifting block and lift weight from a standing position. Adjust intensity with your feet: the closer they are to directly under you, the easier it is.

</details>

**3. A friend does lock-offs every day at home, always at the very top with his chin over the bar, and lately the inside of his elbow feels off.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Lattice recommends lock-offs at about 90° and 125° and avoiding the fully locked top position, because the elbow takes the most stress there and overuse raises injury risk. Change the angle and cut the volume first; if the elbow pain persists, he should see a doctor or physical therapist.

</details>

**4. After a month of home hangboarding, you're back at the gym. Your fingers feel stronger, but you're still stuck on the same V3.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

That's common. JOVA's log says the same: home training maintains, but improvement comes from climbing. Your plateau may not be your fingers. Go back to the [diagnosis post](/posts/climbing/2026-10-18-climbing-training-diagnosis-en) and re-diagnose, or look through the [Climbing Movement series](/posts/climbing/2026-10-18-climbing-movement-overview-en) for a movement problem.

</details>
