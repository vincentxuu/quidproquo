> 🌏 [中文版](/posts/climbing/2026-10-18-climbing-injury-overview)

Halfway up a boulder problem, a finger pops on a small crimp. Or you jump from four metres and your ankle lands on the seam between two mats. You sit on the mat with three questions: how bad is it, do I need a hospital right now, and which doctor do I see?

This post is the entry point to the Climbing Injuries and First Aid series. It answers those three questions and then gives you a map of all 14 posts. If you are new to climbing gyms, start with the [beginner's guide](/posts/climbing/2026-10-08-climbing-beginner-guide-en).

**This series does not replace a doctor.** It summarises what research and clinicians have written; it does not diagnose or prescribe. The [UIAA Medical Commission's sport-climbing factsheet](https://theuiaa.org/documents/safety/UIAA_Medical_SportClimbing.pdf) puts it bluntly: climbers already tend not to see a doctor when injured, and that is very dangerous.

## Where climbers get hurt: it depends on where the data comes from

You may have heard "climbing mostly hurts your fingers" and also "climbing mostly breaks legs". Both have research behind them. The difference is where the patients were collected.

- **Sports-medicine clinic**: Schöffl's group in Germany [followed 911 climbing injuries at one specialist clinic](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25712297/). Fingers made up 52%, with the shoulder second. The most common finger injury was a pulley injury.
- **Emergency department**: an [analysis of US emergency-department data from 2014–2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41550581/) shows the opposite picture. The lower limb made up 50.6%, falls were the main cause, and fractures were the most common type.
- **Why**: a [2026 scoping review of 45 studies](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12911722/) notes that only severe acute injuries reach emergency or surgical care, so hospital data rank fractures first. Minor injuries that don't stop people climbing tend to go unrecorded in any kind of study.

Overall, the most common injuries are ligament sprains and muscle or tendon strains, mainly in the fingers, hands, ankles and shoulders. Finger and shoulder problems are mostly overuse injuries that build up slowly; ankle injuries and fractures are mostly acute injuries from falls. Bouldering has both. A [study that followed 507 indoor boulderers for a year](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33966976/) found more upper-limb injuries, but lower-limb injuries were more often rated as more severe and more often happened when falling or jumping off the wall.

<details><summary>Why injury rates differ tenfold</summary>

- [Five years and over 515,000 visits at a Stuttgart gym](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23877045/): 0.02 acute injuries per 1,000 hours.
- [An online survey of sport climbers](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21913158/): 0.2 acute injuries per 1,000 hours.
- Both count acute injuries only, but one is an on-site gym record and the other is self-reported recall, so they cannot be compared directly. Studies that include overuse injuries report higher numbers again.

</details>

**Something you can do tonight**: write down where you've had pain in the last three months and for how long. If fingers, shoulders and elbows keep appearing, you are closer to the clinic picture. If you often jump off the top of boulders, you are closer to the emergency-department picture. Start the rest of the series from your picture.

## What raises injury risk

[Quarmby and colleagues' 2023 systematic review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38162697/) pooled 34 studies on overuse injuries in adult climbers. Five factors were associated with higher risk:

- Higher climbing intensity
- Bouldering as the main discipline
- Lower grip or finger strength
- Frequent use of the crimp grip
- A previous injury

The same review found that warm-ups, cool-downs, stretching and taping were **not associated** with overuse injury, though the authors note the evidence for these is limited. On the other side, a strength-training programme prevented shoulder and elbow injuries in a randomised trial, although both papers came from the same group of participants.

If research doesn't support "warming up prevents injury", why warm up at all? The next post, [Warm-up](/posts/climbing/2026-10-18-climbing-warm-up-en), covers exactly that. For how to fit strength training in, see [Antagonist and core training](/posts/climbing/2026-10-18-climbing-antagonist-core-en).

**Something you can do tonight**: check how many of the five factors apply to you. If your intensity jumped recently, or you went back to hard climbing before an old injury settled, put those two things into next week's plan.

## In the moment: when to get medical help immediately

The most important part first. In the situations below, don't hesitate: call [119](https://www.nfa.gov.tw/cht/index.php?code=list&flag=detail&ids=66&article_id=795) (Taiwan's fire and ambulance number; in Chinese) or have someone take you to the emergency department. **Don't drive yourself.**

**A fall with a head strike**: the UK National Health Service (NHS) [head-injury page](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/head-injury-and-concussion/) lists situations that need an ambulance, including:

- Falling from more than 1 metre and hitting the head
- Being knocked out and not waking, being unable to stay awake, or having a seizure
- New numbness or weakness anywhere in the body
- Problems with walking, balance, speaking or understanding
- Clear fluid coming from the ears or nose

**Neck or back pain, or tingling limbs, after landing from height**: the [Wilderness Medical Society's 2024 spine guideline summary](https://wms.org/magazine/1476/2024-Spine-Summary) frames the goal as protecting the spinal cord: a conscious casualty should be encouraged not to move in any way that is painful. What to do while waiting for rescue is covered in the last post, [Crag first aid and accident response](/posts/climbing/2026-10-18-crag-first-aid-en).

**A possible limb fracture**: the NHS [sprains and strains page](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/sprains-and-strains/) says to call an ambulance or go to the ED if any of these apply:

- You heard a crack when the injury happened
- The injured part has changed shape or points at an odd angle
- The injured part is numb, tingling or has pins and needles
- The skin around it has turned blue or grey, or is cold to touch

**A finger pop**: the UIAA Medical Commission says you cannot tell for yourself whether you have torn a finger tendon. A pop you heard or felt under sudden load, followed by swelling, warrants examination by a doctor who understands climbing injuries. [Lattice's physiotherapist](https://ukclimbing.com/articles/skills/series/lattice_training_series_with_tom_randall/part_6_finger_injuries-11469) adds that visible bowstringing of the tendon, obvious deformity or major loss of function call for a specialist appointment and an MRI.

**Finger pain in anyone under 18**: the British Mountaineering Council's [growth-plate guide](https://thebmc.co.uk/en/growth-plate-stress-fractures-in-teenage-climbers) says "Pain = STOP!". A teenager with persistent finger pain should **never be allowed to climb through the pain**; a doctor needs to assess it and, where needed, refer to a hand surgeon. Details in [Youth growth plates](/posts/climbing/2026-10-18-youth-growth-plate-en).

Having none of these signs doesn't mean you're fine. [Taipei Veterans General Hospital's sprain leaflet](https://ihealth.vghtpe.gov.tw/media/476) (in Chinese) says to see a doctor promptly if swelling and pain persist after rest; the NHS likewise says to see a GP if it gets worse or doesn't improve with self-care.

**Something you can do tonight**: find the nearest emergency department to your usual gym that is open evenings and weekends, and save it in your phone. When something actually happens, you won't be in the mood to search.

## The first few days: PRICE or PEACE & LOVE

For a sprain or strain that doesn't need immediate care, what do you do for the first few days? Sources disagree here, and you'll see two camps.

**PRICE (Protection, Rest, Ice, Compression, Elevation)**: the NHS still recommends PRICE for the first 2–3 days. Taiwanese hospital leaflets mostly use it too, for example Taipei Veterans General Hospital and the [rehabilitation department at NTUH Hsinchu Branch](https://www.hch.gov.tw/?aid=626&pid=57&page_name=detail&iid=459) (in Chinese) for ankle sprains.

**PEACE & LOVE**: in a [British Journal of Sports Medicine (BJSM) editorial](https://blogs.bmj.com/bjsm/2019/04/26/soft-tissue-injuries-simply-need-peace-love/), Dubois and Esculier argue that RICE and PRICE only cover the acute phase and ignore recovery. Their version differs in three main ways:

- **Protect for only 1–3 days** and minimise rest, because prolonged rest weakens tissue.
- **Question ice**: they say there is no high-quality evidence that ice works for soft-tissue injuries.
- **Avoid anti-inflammatory drugs and modalities**, because inflammation is part of repair. After the first days, resume loading early, within limits that don't make the pain worse.

What both camps share: short-term protection, not prolonged immobility. Be clear about the evidence level: PEACE & LOVE is **expert opinion**, drawn mostly from ankle-sprain and general soft-tissue literature, not climbing-specific research.

**Ask a doctor or pharmacist about medication**; this series does not give drug advice. The UIAA Medical Commission also warns against making a habit of anti-inflammatories, because they can mask an overload situation.

**Something you can do tonight**: create an "injury log" note template on your phone with three lines: when, what move, and whether you heard a sound or it swelled straight away. At the doctor's, those three lines are far more useful than "I think I pulled something that day".

## Which doctor to see in Taiwan

One thing to know first: under the [Regulations on Medical Specialty Categories and Examinations](https://www.rsroc.org.tw/news/news_detail.asp?news_id=2654&NType=9) (in Chinese), Taiwan's 25 official specialties include orthopaedics, rehabilitation medicine (physical medicine and rehabilitation), plastic surgery and emergency medicine, but **not "sports medicine" or "hand surgery"**. Those two are society-level specialist training. Hospital departments called "sports medicine" often sit under orthopaedics, for example the [sports medicine department at Kaohsiung Chang Gung](https://www.cgmh.org.tw/tw/Systems/BranchInfo/8/82700/82740) (in Chinese).

The table below shows common routes, **not a diagnosis**. If unsure, ask the clinic or hospital front desk before booking.

| Situation | Where to start | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Any situation from the "In the moment" section | Emergency department (call 119) | Possible fracture, head or spine injury |
| Finger, wrist or elbow injury, especially a pop followed by swelling | Orthopaedics, or a doctor with hand-surgery training | The [Taiwan Society for Surgery of the Hand](https://handsurgery.com.tw/學會簡介/) (in Chinese) defines hand surgery as covering the shoulder to the fingertips, practised by orthopaedic or plastic surgeons who pass an additional exam; many problems need no surgery |
| A sprain not clearly better after a week, or slowly building aches | Rehabilitation medicine or orthopaedics | NTUH Hsinchu's rehabilitation department advises rehab treatment if an ankle sprain hasn't improved after a week |
| A rehab programme and a graded return to climbing | A physiotherapist, after a doctor's diagnosis | See below |

**Can you go straight to a physiotherapist?** Under Article 12 of Taiwan's [Physical Therapists Act](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawAll.aspx?pcode=L0020044) (in Chinese), physiotherapists work from a doctor's diagnosis, consultation request or order; only services "not for the purpose of treating disease" are exempt. If an injury needs treatment, see a doctor first and take the diagnosis to the physiotherapist. For prevention or a fitness assessment, you can go directly.

**Medical centre or clinic?** The [National Health Insurance co-payment rules](https://www.nhi.gov.tw/ch/cp-5163-9803c-2737-1.html) (in Chinese) make walking into a medical centre without a referral more expensive, so for non-urgent problems you can start at an orthopaedic or rehabilitation clinic and get referred if needed.

<details><summary>Basic outpatient co-payment, Western medicine (NHI, from 15 April 2017)</summary>

| Level | With referral | Without referral |
|---|---|---|
| Medical centre | NT$170 | NT$420 |
| Regional hospital | NT$100 | NT$240 |
| District hospital | NT$50 | NT$80 |
| Clinic | NT$50 | NT$50 |

</details>

**Something you can do tonight**: near your home or gym, find one orthopaedic or rehabilitation clinic and one doctor with hand-surgery or sports-medicine training, and note their name and clinic hours. When a finger goes, you won't be starting from zero.

## Practice: four scenarios

Answers are under each question. Think first, then look.

**1. A friend jumps off the top of a boulder, says the ankle hurts badly, can't put weight on it, and the outside of the ankle is swelling.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Being unable to bear weight is itself a reason to see a doctor. If there was a crack at the moment of injury, the ankle looks deformed, the foot is numb or the skin has changed colour, the NHS advises going straight to the ED, and not letting them drive. Without those signs, protect and elevate it for now and get to orthopaedics or the ED soon so a doctor can decide on an X-ray. This kind of landing injury is the most typical climbing injury in emergency-department data.

</details>

**2. You bear down on a small crimp and a finger pops. It doesn't hurt much at first, but ten minutes later the base of your ring finger is swollen.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The UIAA Medical Commission says a pop followed by swelling warrants examination by a doctor who knows climbing injuries; you cannot judge it yourself. Stop climbing today and book orthopaedics or a doctor with hand-surgery training soon. If the finger is visibly deformed or the tendon lifts away from the bone, Lattice's physiotherapist recommends a specialist appointment and imaging. More in Finger pulley injuries.

</details>

**3. A 15-year-old says the back of the middle finger's second joint has hurt for two weeks, "it stops hurting once I'm warmed up", and wants to keep going to competition training.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Don't continue. The BMC guide says "Pain = STOP!" for teenagers; they should never climb through the pain. Persistent pain on the back of a finger joint is one of the typical presentations of a growth-plate stress fracture. See a doctor and ask for referral to a hand surgeon; a normal X-ray doesn't rule it out, and the guide says a focused MRI may be needed to show it.

</details>

**4. You swing into the wall on a lead fall and feel a bit dazed. Five minutes later you feel fine and want to do another route.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Don't climb again, and have someone stay with you. The NHS head-injury page says vomiting or dizziness after a head knock needs medical advice, and a fall from more than 1 metre with a head strike, confusion, or speech or balance problems needs an ambulance. In Taiwan, if you can't judge it, call 119 or go to the ED, and don't drive yourself.

</details>
