You've started hangboarding three times a week. A friend at the gym says, "Drop two kilos and you'll send that route." You start skipping dinner. The first two weeks feel easier. By week four your fingers never seem to recover and the loads on the board won't go up.

This post answers two questions. Is "lighter means stronger" actually true? And how do you know you're training too much or eating too little? It's the last post in the Climbing Training series. The plans in earlier posts ([hangboard](/posts/climbing/2026-10-18-hangboard-training-en), [periodization](/posts/climbing/2026-10-18-climbing-periodization-en)) all assume you eat and sleep enough. This post is about that assumption.

To be clear about scope: this is a summary of what research and coaches say, not a diagnostic tool. If the warning signs below match your situation, see a doctor or registered dietitian rather than judging yourself by the numbers.

Evidence labels in this post: **research** = published studies; **coaching consensus** = advice from coaches or training organizations, not directly tested in studies.

## "Lighter means stronger": the views side by side

Half of this is true. Climbing works against gravity, so for the same finger strength, less body weight means less load on each finger. But "eat less now and you'll climb better" is a different claim.

| Source | What it says | Type |
|---|---|---|
| [Watts 1993](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8497013/) | World Cup climbers had very low body fat and a high grip-strength-to-mass ratio; these predicted climbing ability, but with a low R² | Research |
| [Ginszt 2023 systematic review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36930882/) | Elite climbers had lower body mass and body fat than less advanced climbers, and higher maximal half-crimp finger strength | Research |
| [Gibson-Smith 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32850940/) | Among 40 experienced climbers, body composition did not differ significantly between ability groups | Research |
| [Lattice × UKC, "Finger Strength Rules"](https://www.ukclimbing.com/articles/skills/series/lattice_training_series_with_tom_randall/part_5_finger_strength_rules-11455) | In their athletes and in 8a.nu data, BMI showed no significant correlation with performance; climbers with long calorie deficits and very low body fat struggle to gain strength | Coach observation |
| [Beth Rodden (Outside)](https://www.outsideonline.com/health/training-performance/beth-rodden-climbing-body-image/) | Losing weight won her podiums and sponsorships in the short term, then broke her body down in the long term | Personal account |

Read the table this way: top climbers are leaner on average. That's a fact. But leanness is one of the outcomes, which doesn't mean cutting weight will make you stronger. Lattice puts it bluntly: long-term calorie deficits will not help strength.

Rodden's story is worth reading in full. Her first ascent of Meltdown was the hardest trad route established by a woman at the time. She started losing weight before competitions as a teenager and didn't get her first period until 19. She writes that losing weight "worked really well for my short-term performance gains but was extremely harmful in the long run": in her late twenties, tendons, ligaments and bones began to fail.

**Something you can do tonight**: Think of the last time you thought "if I were just a bit lighter." Which route, which move? Was it really weight, or footholds, body position or fingers? Go back to the [Climbing Movement overview](/posts/climbing/2026-10-18-climbing-movement-overview-en) and look at that move as a technique problem first.

## What RED-S is

Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport (RED-S) is a term the International Olympic Committee introduced in 2014; the 2023 [consensus statement](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37752011/) updated it as REDs. The cause is low energy availability (LEA): the energy you eat, minus what exercise burns, leaves too little for the body to run normally.

[Sports medicine physician Dr. M's summary](https://www.drmbesuperior.com/post/%E9%81%8B%E5%8B%95%E7%9B%B8%E5%B0%8D%E8%83%BD%E9%87%8F%E4%B8%8D%E8%B6%B3-relative-energy-deficiency-in-sports%EF%BC%88red-s%EF%BC%89) (in Chinese) explains it clearly. RED-S grew out of the Female Athlete Triad; once it became clear that men are affected too, it got a broader name. The systems involved include menstrual function, bone, hormones, metabolism, immunity, digestion and mental health.

Two common misunderstandings:

1. **"My BMI is normal, so I'm fine."** [Joubert 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36032265/) surveyed World Cup-level female competitors and found some with menstrual disturbances despite relatively normal BMIs. [World Climbing (formerly the IFSC)](https://www.worldclimbing.com/news/new-competition-policy-places-ifsc-at-forefront-of-fight-against-reds) also says a BMI test alone does not give an accurate picture of health. (Research)
2. **"This is a women's problem."** The 2023 IOC consensus notes that more data now describe the impact of LEA in males. (Research)

<details><summary>The LEA threshold numbers</summary>

Studies usually express energy availability per kg of fat-free mass per day: 45 kcal or more is adequate, below 30 kcal is LEA, and in between is "suboptimal." Dr. M's summary notes these are values suggested in the 2018 IOC consensus, and that there is no settled answer for how low, or for how long, it takes to cause RED-S. Measuring it properly means logging food, estimating exercise expenditure and measuring fat-free mass. Most people can't calculate it accurately on their own, which is one reason it belongs with a professional.

</details>

**Something you can do tonight**: On one sheet of paper, write down this week's training days and the days you deliberately ate less. If they fall on the same days, drop one of the two.

## Three groups of warning signs

[Lattice's RED-S article](https://latticetraining.com/blog/your-period-is-a-marker-of-health-red-s-energy-availability) splits the warning signs into menstrual and non-menstrual. Grouped into three, they're easier to remember:

1. **Menstrual changes**: cycles become irregular, flow or cycle length changes, or periods stop. With low energy availability, the body turns down sex hormone production. Two caveats: the bleed on the combined oral contraceptive pill isn't a natural cycle and can mask a problem; and some athletes have had multiple stress fractures before their period changed, so a regular period doesn't mean you're healthy. (Coaching consensus)
2. **Repeated injury or illness**: Lattice lists both. [Grønhaug 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42676485/) surveyed 417 climbers and found that a higher LEA symptom burden went with higher odds of reporting a current injury, with gastrointestinal symptoms the most consistent link. It's a cross-sectional study, so it shows an association, not that LEA causes injury. (Research)
3. **Training stops paying off; mood and focus drop**: poor response to strength training, reduced concentration, low mood, low motivation. This group applies to everyone, menstrual cycle or not. (Coaching consensus)

Lattice's suggestion: if you notice these changes, try reducing training, adding more frequent rest weeks, or eating a bit more. A change in your period should always be checked by a doctor first to rule out other medical conditions.

**Something you can do tonight**: Start a note on your phone with one line per week: period (if you have one), whether you were ill or injured, and whether your hangboard load dropped from last week. Keep it for four weeks and bring it to the doctor.

## Do climbers really under-eat?

If you think "but I'm not dieting," the research may surprise you. Many climbers who aren't deliberately restricting still don't eat enough.

- [Monedero 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35836334/): of 25 advanced to elite climbers, 28% had LEA. Average carbohydrate intake in both the male and female groups was below sports nutrition recommendations, and calcium, magnesium and vitamin D intakes were inadequate. (Research)
- [Gibson-Smith 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32850940/): most of 40 experienced climbers didn't eat enough to meet the predicted needs of a "moderate" training program. 30% of the women met the criteria for iron deficiency. (Research)
- [Mora-Fernandez 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39275289/): more than half of 46 Spanish sport climbers had inadequate carbohydrate intake. (Research)
- The [narrative review by Tan and Schöffl 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40875976/) also notes a concerning number of high-level climbers whose nutrition can't sustain even a moderate level of training. (Research)

<details><summary>More numbers</summary>

- Monedero 2023: 88% were below the "adequate" energy availability threshold; only the women's intake was significantly below their calculated needs; the women also ate less protein and iron than recommended.
- Gibson-Smith 2020: 77.5% didn't meet the predicted energy requirement for moderate training; 30% of men and 5% of women didn't even meet predicted resting metabolic rate; vegan/vegetarian women had lower serum ferritin.
- Mora-Fernandez 2024: 57.8% low in carbohydrate, 31.1% low in protein; mean energy availability about 33 kcal per kg fat-free mass, with 35.6% in LEA.

</details>

So how much should you eat? Honestly, there's no climbing-specific answer. [Joubert 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42602506/) reviewed 16 observational studies of climbers' diets and found that no clinical trial has yet manipulated climbers' diets, so evidence-based, climbing-specific nutrition guidance doesn't exist yet.

The most concrete guidance so far comes from [Smith 2017's review of bouldering nutrition](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28387575/): bouldering is high-intensity, so make sure carbohydrate is adequate; spread protein across the day, roughly every 3–4 hours; and when weight loss is the goal, it's even more important to work with a nutrition professional. The same review says every aspect of bouldering nutrition still needs more research. (Research review)

**Something you can do tonight**: Take photos of your three meals tomorrow, a training day. If there's almost no rice, noodles, potatoes or similar starch, you're in the gap the studies find most often (low carbohydrate). Show the photos to a registered dietitian and ask them to work out how much you need for your training load. If you want to change your weight, book the dietitian first.

## Disordered eating: why two studies seem to disagree

How common is disordered eating in climbing? Two studies seem to give different answers:

| Study | Participants | Findings |
|---|---|---|
| [Joubert 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33345077/) | 498 sport lead climbers from an international online survey, no control group | Prevalence in women more than double that in men; among women, linked to higher ability |
| [Nigicser 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39414282/) | 183 Swedish advanced and elite climbers, compared with 180 non-climbers | No difference in eating disorder symptoms between climbers and controls; women reported more symptoms than men |

They don't actually conflict. Joubert had no control group, so it can only say the problem exists among climbers. Nigicser had one and found climbers were no worse than non-climbers. Both found higher risk in women than in men.

[Grønhaug 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37398555/) analysed injuries in the same group of World Cup women: BMI did not predict injury, and those with an eating disorder appeared more likely to be injured, though this did not reach statistical significance. (Research)

If thoughts about food or weight are already affecting your daily life, that is a medical issue. See a psychiatrist or psychologist; it isn't something to push through on willpower.

## How World Climbing handles REDs

[World Climbing introduced a REDs policy in 2024](https://www.worldclimbing.com/news/new-competition-policy-places-ifsc-at-forefront-of-fight-against-reds) and describes itself as the first international federation with comprehensive REDs regulations. The four main parts:

- Athletes fill in two short questionnaires (height, weight, heart rate, blood pressure and so on).
- National federations issue each athlete a health certificate, or request more testing before giving clearance.
- Random and targeted testing of BMI, heart rate and blood pressure during the season.
- Suspected cases go to an external commission of independent experts.

They deliberately don't screen on BMI alone. Their reasons: BMI by itself isn't accurate, it varies a lot between countries, and excluding athletes on BMI alone wouldn't be legally defensible. For everyday climbers the takeaway is simple: if the international federation doesn't rely on a weight number, you don't need to make it your only measure either.

**Something you can do tonight**: If you coach or lead a team, check whether you've commented on anyone's weight in front of athletes this week. The BMC-supported [En:Spire event](https://www.ukclimbing.com/news/press/enspire_raising_awareness_of_relative_energy_deficiency_in_climbing-12429) brought a sports medicine doctor and a dietitian to a climbing wall to talk about RED-S signs and management, partly so coaches and clubs could better support their athletes.

## Recovery: how do you know you've overdone it?

Eating enough is half of it. The other half is giving your body time to recover.

**How long between hangboard sessions**: In her [Fingerboard Training Guide III](https://en-eva-lopez.blogspot.com/2018/07/fingerboard-training-guide-iii-periodization-samples-of-maxhangs-training-programs-.html), Eva López recommends two sessions a week for improvement, with 48–72 hours of recovery between them; even if you do three sessions a week, that gap is still "compulsory." (Coaching consensus)

She also lists when to extend the gap to 72 hours: high stress, a physically demanding job, or when you can't reach the same load from one session to the next without an obvious reason. In that case she says to review the rest of your week's climbing and training, plus your diet and sleep.

**What happens when load exceeds recovery**: Physical therapist Jason Hooper of [Hooper's Beta](https://hoopersbeta.com/library/throw-your-hangboard-in-the-trash-if-you-dont-know-this-save-a-friend) says tissues have a limited capacity for work. Too much workload exceeds your ability to recover and leads to chronic fatigue and eventually the failure of some tissue, often in the fingers or shoulders. He also notes that adults generally need much more recovery time than youth team kids. (Coaching consensus, physical therapist's view)

**How to deload**: [Lattice's deload article](https://latticetraining.com/blog/do_climbers_need_deload_weeks) says they run 3–4 week cycles, each ending with a deload week. A deload isn't a week off; it cuts total training load by 40–60%: fewer sessions, fewer sets, or easier climbing. During a deload, avoid staying up late or eating less well, because both affect recovery. (Coaching consensus)

Putting these together gives three possible signs of overdoing it:

1. Your hangboard load falls short of last session's twice in a row, with no obvious reason.
2. Your second session of the week is regularly worse than the first.
3. Repeated minor injuries, frequent colds, low mood and motivation. These overlap with the RED-S warning signs; Lattice reads them as signs of too much stress and too little food or recovery.

These come from coaching advice, not diagnostic criteria. If pain persists or gets worse, see a physical therapist or orthopaedic doctor. Injury prevention and what to do after an injury are covered in the [Climbing Injuries and First Aid](/posts/climbing/2026-10-18-climbing-injury-overview-en) series.

**Something you can do tonight**: Add a column to your training log: "this session's hangboard load vs last time." Next time it drops, don't add extra training. Check the previous two nights' sleep and food, and push the next session back to 72 hours later.

## Practice: four scenarios

**1. A friend says you'd send that 5.11b if you lost two kilos. You've been climbing for a year and a half and aren't overweight.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Don't start dieting. Elite climbers are leaner on average, but Lattice found no significant correlation between BMI and performance in their data, and they observed that climbers in long-term calorie deficits struggle to gain finger strength. Find out which move stops you and whether it's technique or fingers. If you really want to change your weight, plan it with a dietitian rather than just eating less.

</details>

**2. Your BMI is in the normal range, you've missed two periods, and your hangboard loads aren't going up.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

A normal BMI doesn't rule anything out. Joubert 2022's World Cup study included climbers with relatively normal BMIs and menstrual disturbances. Menstrual change plus training that stops paying off are two of the groups Lattice lists. See a doctor (gynaecology or sports medicine) to rule out other causes; until then, cut back training and eat a bit more.

</details>

**3. You're a man. You've had four colds in three months, your fingers and shoulders take turns aching, and you have no motivation to train.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

RED-S isn't only a women's problem. Repeated illness, repeated injury and low motivation are on Lattice's list of signs that apply to everyone. Write down the past month's training and eating and take it to a doctor or dietitian. Meanwhile, schedule a deload week: cut total load by about half, not to zero.

</details>

**4. You hangboarded three times this week. The third session's load was well below the first, and you slept five hours the night before.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

This is exactly when Eva López says to extend rest: if you can't reach last session's load, check sleep, diet and the rest of your training. Push the next hangboard session to 72 hours later and don't make up for it with extra training. If this happens every week, go back to your [periodization plan](/posts/climbing/2026-10-18-climbing-periodization-en) and see whether it's time for a deload week.

</details>
