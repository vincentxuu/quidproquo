Your back and neck can run into three completely different problems in climbing. The first is the rarest and the most serious: on a rope, you aren't caught, you hit the ground, and you injure your spine. The second is common and mostly mild: an aching lower back. The third every belayer knows: an afternoon of looking up, and a stiff neck.

This post goes in order of severity. First, how to prevent ground-fall spinal injuries and what to do first if one happens; then low back pain; finally, a look at two sayings, "belayer's neck" and the "climber's hunch". By the end you should be able to name ways to reduce neck strain while belaying, and know which claims have no evidence yet.

**This post is not a diagnosis.** If back or neck pain persists or comes with the warning signs below, see an orthopaedic or rehabilitation doctor. If someone hits the ground and has back pain with numbness in the arms or legs, call emergency services (119 in Taiwan).

## The most serious: spinal injuries from ground falls

The German Alpine Club (DAV) compiles climbing-gym accidents every year, counting only those with an ambulance call-out or at least a moderate injury. Its [2025 statistics (in German)](https://www.alpenverein.de/verband/bergsport/sicherheitsforschung/kletterhallen-unfallstatistik/kletterhallenunfallstatistik-2025) cover gyms in Germany and Austria, and one line is worth every belayer remembering: **more than half of serious rope-climbing accidents were spinal injuries from ground falls.**

Bouldering accidents are far more numerous but are mostly arm and leg injuries. Rope-climbing accidents are fewer and more serious.

US emergency department data shows a similar picture. In a ten-year national analysis ([Lin 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12806586/)), vertebral fractures made up about 8% of all climbing fractures. People who fell from higher than about 6 metres had a clearly higher fracture risk.

### Where ground falls happen

DAV 2025 breaks the ground falls down:

- **While lowering.** Nearly half of rope-climbing ground falls happened during lowering, mostly when the rope ran out of control through the belay device. DAV points out that the share of ground falls in situations like lowering, which in theory have no free-fall potential, is "worryingly high".
- **While clipping on lead.** Most were climbers falling as they pulled up rope to clip. DAV recommends setting routes so the section near the ground is relatively easy and offers good clipping positions.
- **Not clipped into the auto-belay.** Every case was a person forgetting to clip in, not a machine failure; the only fatality that year was this kind.
- **An unfinished tie-in knot.** In one case it was missed despite a partner check.

<details><summary>DAV 2025 numbers</summary>

- 252 accidents analysed: 174 bouldering, 68 rope climbing (including auto-belays), 10 other.
- Of 15 serious rope-climbing accidents, 9 were spinal injuries from ground falls.
- 31 rope-climbing ground falls in total, 15 while lowering; in 12 of those 15 the rope ran uncontrolled through the belay device.
- 5 ground falls at auto-belays, all from not clipping in; 1 fatal, the other 4 serious, from 4.5–10 m.
- Among serious bouldering accidents, 1 was a vertebral fracture after landing on the tailbone.

</details>

DAV's conclusion is blunt: too many serious rope-climbing accidents happen because the partner check, or the self-check at an auto-belay, isn't done properly. Another common cause is misusing the belay device, such as letting go with the brake hand or not controlling rope speed while lowering.

All of this is taught in belay courses. Review the partner check and lowering in [Learning to Belay Top Rope in the Gym](/posts/climbing/2026-10-17-gym-top-rope-belay-en), and the stance and rope handling for the first five clips in [Learning to Lead in the Gym](/posts/climbing/2026-10-17-gym-lead-climbing-en).

### What to do first after a fall

If someone hits the ground and has back or neck pain, or numbness or weakness in the arms or legs, call emergency services.

While you wait, the goal is to protect the spinal cord, not to strap the person down so they can't move. That's the core of the [official summary of the Wilderness Medical Society's (WMS) 2024 spine guideline](https://wms.org/magazine/1476/2024-Spine-Summary). Encourage a conscious casualty not to make any movement that hurts; padding with soft materials and limiting movement is enough. The guideline no longer treats rigid cervical collars and long backboards as immobilisation tools, because evidence suggests they are not necessarily safe.

How to move someone and how to call for help is in [Crag First Aid and Accident Response](/posts/climbing/2026-10-18-crag-first-aid-en) and [Self-Rescue Basics](/posts/climbing/2026-10-17-self-rescue-basics-en).

**Something you can do tonight**: Next time you tie in, look at your tie-in knot once more after the partner check: is the figure-eight fully retraced, is the tail long enough? When you lower your partner, keep your brake hand on the rope the whole way, slowly enough that you could stop at any moment.

## Low back pain: common, but mostly mild

The numbers vary widely here because each study asks a different question.

**Ask "any low back trouble in the past year?" and the answer is high.** An Italian study ([Carraro 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10770867/)) asked teenage competitive climbers, and about three in four reported low back complaints in the past year. But most were low-intensity and low-disability, and the authors concluded they **did not affect regular training**.

**Ask "which chronic injuries do you have?" and the low back ranks far down.** In a Norwegian online survey ([Grønhaug 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6059297/)), only about 3% of chronic injuries were in the lower back, far fewer than fingers, shoulders and elbows.

Schöffl's group responded to Carraro directly in their [2025 study of adolescent climbing injuries](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11848689/): no other study has seen such a high figure, and Carraro asked only about low back pain without recording other injuries.

### What MRI shows

Swedish researchers scanned the spines of national-team-level climbers and an equal number of controls, 15 in each group. The [2023 analysis](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9999554/) used visual grading and found no difference between groups, and no one had spondylolisthesis (a slipped vertebra). A [2026 re-analysis of the same images with quantitative methods](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13005541/) found lower disc signal in the climbers around the junction of the thoracic and lumbar spine, which the authors read as early signs of degeneration.

Whether these changes cause pain is unknown. The authors' own recommendation: **reserve spinal MRI in climbers for those with pain or other spinal symptoms, not routine screening.**

### Warning signs for back pain

Climbing research hasn't produced climbing-specific warning signs for back pain, so here is the UK National Health Service's triage from its [back pain page](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/back-pain/):

- **Call emergency services or go to the emergency department**: pain, tingling, weakness or numbness in both legs; loss of feeling around the genitals or anus; changes in bladder or bowel control; chest pain; back pain that started after a serious accident.
- **Get urgent medical help**: you feel hot, cold, shivery or generally unwell; severe pain that starts suddenly or gets worse quickly.
- **Book an appointment**: no improvement after a few weeks of self-care; it stops you doing daily activities; unexplained weight loss; it doesn't improve with rest or is worse at night; the pain is between your shoulder blades.

In Taiwan, back pain is usually seen first by orthopaedics or rehabilitation medicine; see the [Climbing Injuries Overview](/posts/climbing/2026-10-18-climbing-injury-overview-en).

**Something you can do tonight**: If your lower back has been aching lately, go through the three tiers above. If none apply, note which movement brings the pain on; if any apply, act at that tier.

## "Belayer's neck": it really aches, but it isn't a diagnosis

**The common saying: "Belay long enough and you'll get belayer's neck. You need belay glasses."**

The sore neck is real. But chiropractor Carla Cupido, who wrote [Climbing.com's article on belayer's neck](https://www.climbing.com/skills/belayers-neck/), opens by saying "belayer's neck" is **not an official orthopaedic diagnosis**. No study has yet measured how common neck pain is among belayers, and none has tested whether belay glasses prevent it. In Grønhaug's survey, the neck accounted for 0.5% of chronic injuries, and the survey didn't separate belaying from climbing as the cause.

Another [Climbing.com article](https://www.climbing.com/gym-climbing/prevent-belayers-neck/), by physiotherapist Adam Luu, is titled "8 science-backed methods". It says tilting your head back 15 degrees raises the pressure in the discs by almost 25%, but those figures come from a spine biomechanics model and a study of fighter pilots' necks under high G, not from belayers. Applying model numbers directly to belaying is an **extrapolation**.

That doesn't make the advice useless; it means it is **practical experience**, not a research finding. Here is what the two articles suggest:

- **Change position often.** Cupido considers this probably the single most important thing for avoiding neck pain: when the climber has clipped or is resting, look down, turn your head side to side, tilt it.
- **Change where you stand** and the angle you look up at changes. But when belaying a leader, stance is about catching safely first; see [Learning to Lead in the Gym](/posts/climbing/2026-10-17-gym-lead-climbing-en).
- **Take a few minutes between belays** to get your neck out of the looking-up position.
- **Practise chin tucks at home**: sit or stand tall, draw your head back to make a double chin, hold about 3 seconds.
- **Belay glasses** let you see the climber without looking up. Cupido says most people adapt quickly.
- **A stopping rule**: Luu keeps soreness at or below 2–3 out of 10; above 3, take a break or stop belaying for the day. Cupido advises anyone who already has chronic pain to ask their doctor.

**Something you can do tonight**: Next time you belay, every time the climber clips and pauses, look down once and turn your head to each side once. At the end of the route, count how many times you did it.

## The "climber's hunch": a visible difference, unknown whether it hurts

**The common saying: "Climb long enough and you'll get hunched, and then your neck, shoulders and back will hurt."**

One study supports this saying, but only the first half. [Förster 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18651371/) measured the spinal shape of 80 male climbers, split into performance-oriented sport climbers and recreational climbers:

- The performance group had a **larger thoracic kyphosis angle** when standing, more so at the top level.
- Lumbar lordosis (the curve of the lower back) was also larger, but not statistically significant.
- Range of motion bending forward and backward showed **no difference** between groups.

The limitations are clear: the control group were also climbers; only men were included; all participants **had no symptoms** and pain wasn't measured; and a cross-sectional design can't show that climbing caused the difference.

Secondary articles often make it sound worse. [The Climbing Doctor's hunchback article](https://theclimbingdoctor.com/hunchback) says "high grade climbers usually suffer from back pain pathology" and that excessive kyphosis causes headaches, numb fingers and shoulder problems. But Förster's participants had no symptoms, and the study didn't measure those outcomes. By contrast, [Climbing.com's article on climber's back](https://www.climbing.com/skills/how-to-treat-climbers-back/) is more cautious: these postural traits are **not inherently harmful**.

The more accurate version: high-level male climbers have a more curved upper back, and whether that difference hurts or turns into injury is unknown. If you want to train shoulder-blade and back stability, the routine in [Antagonist and Core Training](/posts/climbing/2026-10-18-climbing-antagonist-core-en) covers it; the reason is that strength training protects shoulders and elbows, not that it fixes a hunch.

**Something you can do tonight**: Next time someone says "climbing has made you hunched", you can reply: "One study found high-level climbers have a more curved upper back, but none of them had symptoms and pain wasn't measured." If your back actually hurts, use the warning signs in the previous section.

## Practice: five scenarios

Answers are under each question. Think first, then look.

**1. While lowering you in the gym, your friend lets go of the brake strand to brush their hair back. You're 8 metres up.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Shout "stop" right away. In DAV's 2025 data, nearly half of rope-climbing ground falls happened while lowering, typically from the brake hand leaving the rope and the rope running out of control. Keeping the brake hand on the rope throughout the lower is a basic belay rule.

</details>

**2. Your partner falls to the ground from around the third clip on a lead route. They're conscious and say their back hurts badly and their legs feel a bit numb.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Call emergency services. Back pain with numbness in the legs is a warning sign of spinal injury. While you wait, encourage them not to make any movement that hurts, pad around them with soft materials, and don't pull them up or move them yourself. The WMS 2024 guideline aims to protect the spinal cord by limiting movement, not by forcing immobilisation.

</details>

**3. You're 16 and train on the school climbing team. Your lower back has often ached lately, but it doesn't affect training. Online you read: "75% of teenage climbers have low back pain, it's normal."**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

That number comes from a study that asked only about low back pain, and other studies haven't seen anything that high. Even if it's common, rule out the warning signs: numbness or weakness in both legs, bladder or bowel changes, worse at night, no better with rest, pain between the shoulder blades. If any apply, see a doctor; if none apply but it hasn't improved after a few weeks, get assessed by orthopaedics or rehabilitation medicine anyway. Overuse injuries in young climbers are the subject of the next post.

</details>

**4. You've belayed all afternoon and your neck is stiff, about 5 out of 10. Your partner wants two more routes.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

By physiotherapist Luu's stopping rule, above 3 means take a break or stop belaying for the day. Swap belayers, or you climb and they belay. If the soreness persists or keeps coming back, see a doctor.

</details>

**5. Someone says: "Research proves climbing gives you a hunch and causes headaches and numb hands."**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The research (Förster 2009) only found a larger thoracic kyphosis angle in high-level male climbers. None had symptoms, pain wasn't measured, and the study can't show climbing caused it. "Causes headaches and numb hands" is an extension made by secondary articles, not a research finding.

</details>
