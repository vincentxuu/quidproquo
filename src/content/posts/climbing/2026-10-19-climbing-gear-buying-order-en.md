On your first gym visit you rented shoes, and the front desk handed you a chalk bag. A few months later you start wondering: is it time to buy my own shoes? What about a harness? A friend offers to sell you a rope he's used for a few years, cheap. Should you take it?

This is post 0 of the Climbing Gear series. The series covers selection criteria, standards and manufacturers' retirement rules only, **with no brand or model recommendations**; any model mentioned is an example of how a standard or a manual is written. This post answers two questions: what to buy first and what to keep renting, and how to read the CE, UIAA, EN numbers and manufacture year on a label. How to choose and inspect each item is left to the next four posts.

If you haven't been to a gym yet, start with the [climbing beginner's guide](/posts/climbing/2026-10-08-climbing-beginner-guide-en).

## What to buy first, what to keep renting

The series follows the order in which you'll spend money. The rule: **buy things when you'll use them, and once you own them, be able to judge whether they're still usable.**

| Stage | What to buy | Why now | Post in this series |
|---|---|---|---|
| Going to the gym regularly | Climbing shoes, chalk bag | You use them in the gym; a good fit only comes with your own shoes | [Post 1: Climbing shoes](/posts/climbing/2026-10-19-climbing-shoes-en) |
| Top-roping regularly, taking a belay test | Harness, belay device, locking carabiner | You put them on and operate them every session; knowing your own gear is safer | [Post 2: Harness and helmet](/posts/climbing/2026-10-19-climbing-harness-helmet-en), [Post 3: Rope, belay device and carabiners](/posts/climbing/2026-10-19-climbing-rope-belay-device-en) |
| About to climb outdoors | Helmet | Crags have rockfall, and belayers wear helmets too | [Post 2](/posts/climbing/2026-10-19-climbing-harness-helmet-en) |
| Leading your own outdoor sport trips | Dynamic rope, quickdraws | The gym supplies ropes; outdoors you bring your own | [Post 3](/posts/climbing/2026-10-19-climbing-rope-belay-device-en) |

### Why shoes come first

Two reasons: fit and hygiene.

[Climbing Ninjette](https://climbingninjette.com/2026/08/29/climbing-shoes-size/) (in Chinese) notes that rental shoes usually come in looser sizing, and that your own pair gives you a consistent feel underfoot. On hygiene, take [the price list at Tup Climbing's Wanhua gym](https://tupclimbing.tw/pricing/84) (in Chinese, checked 2026-10-19): shoe rental is NT$100 per visit and a chalk bag NT$80, and you must wear socks with rental shoes; you can buy a pair on site if you forgot.

**When to buy?** The same post suggests renting until you've climbed about five times, to find out whether you enjoy it, and buying once you're climbing at least once a week. That's one climbing blogger's experience, not a research finding, but it's a reasonable starting point.

### Why harness and belay device come second

Once you top-rope regularly, you put on a harness and operate a belay device every session. With your own gear, you know its history and its feel. How to operate a belay device is covered in [gym top-rope belaying](/posts/climbing/2026-10-17-gym-top-rope-belay-en); this series only covers how to choose one.

### Helmet, rope and quickdraws come last

A helmet is for rockfall outdoors, and [outdoor belaying](/posts/climbing/2026-10-17-outdoor-belay-en) explains why belayers wear one too. A rope and quickdraws are what you need when you start leading your own outdoor trips; until then, climb with experienced people and use theirs.

In the video below, North Wales instructor Jez lays out what to bring when a friend takes you out to second: one harness, a helmet, a chalk bag, beginner shoes, and a belay device with a locking carabiner, while the friend brings the rope and the rack. That matches this post's advice to borrow ropes and quickdraws at first. The difference is the helmet: his audience starts on UK crags, so it's in the first batch; if you're still in the gym, the order above stands. It's one instructor talking from experience with UK prices and no testing, and the personal-kit part ends around 7:30, after which he moves on to trad gear.

## Shoes aren't PPE; harnesses are

Climbing gear falls into two groups. An [article by the German Alpine Club (DAV) safety research team](https://www.alpenverein.de/artikel/nutzungsdauer-karabiner-seile-helme-klettersteigsets_a1acb469-ec21-4718-a487-9facc2f821d7) (in German) draws the line: climbing shoes are sports equipment, used mainly to do the sport, with no regulations on strength and the like; gear whose main job is protection is personal protective equipment (PPE), which EU law regulates.

[Petzl's equipment-care notes](https://products.papertrail.io/downloads/petzl/d08231b_entretien-assureurs-descendeurs_EN.pdf) list three PPE categories:

- **Category 1**: minor hazards, such as gloves and glasses.
- **Category 2**: serious hazards, such as ventilated helmets and crampons.
- **Category 3**: major or deadly hazards, such as harnesses, carabiners and ice axes.

So for shoes you can only rely on trying them on; for harnesses, helmets, ropes, carabiners and belay devices you also read the label.

## CE marking vs the UIAA Safety Label

You'll see two kinds of marking on gear.

- **CE marking**: required for PPE sold in the EU. The EN standard it was tested to is usually printed next to it.
- **UIAA Safety Label**: issued by the International Climbing and Mountaineering Federation (UIAA). [The UIAA's safety standards page](https://www.theuiaa.org/safety/safety-standards/) says it began writing standards in 1960 with ropes and now covers more than 25 types of equipment; it works with the European Committee for Standardization (CEN) to harmonise them, and in some cases asks for additional tests, making its standard stricter than CEN's.

[The UIAA's buying advice, updated in February 2026](https://theuiaa.org/uiaa/uiaa-advice-ten-things-to-consider-when-buying-climbing-gear), says to buy only gear bearing the UIAA Safety Label and/or the CE mark. It's usually engraved or stitched on the gear, or printed in the instructions. If you doubt a model's label, check the [UIAA Certified Equipment Database](https://www.theuiaa.org/safety-standards/certified-equipment/).

While breaking a batch of carabiners bought on Amazon, HowNOT2's Ryan ran into a real case: one product page claimed UIAA certification, but the UIAA site showed it had expired. Start at 10:40; the description walks through ticking the extended search for old products to find the lapsed listing. The video is from 2020 and uses a home-built slow-pull rig with only a few samples per model, not a standard test. The knockoffs did beat their labels in those few pulls, but a sample that small says nothing about whether knockoffs are safe to climb on. Ryan also admits he doesn't know the difference between CE and UIAA, and the definitions in the description come from viewer comments, so go by the UIAA sources in this section for how to read a label.

### Standard numbers for common gear

| Gear | EN standard | UIAA standard | Post in this series |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dynamic rope | EN 892 | UIAA 101 | Post 3 |
| Harness | EN 12277 | UIAA 105 | Post 2 |
| Helmet | EN 12492 | UIAA 106 | Post 2 |
| Carabiner | EN 12275 | UIAA 121 | Post 3 |
| Belay (braking) device | EN 15151 | UIAA 129 | Post 3 |

For example, [Petzl's GRIGRI technical notice](https://www.petzl.com/sfc/servlet.shepherd/version/download/068Tx000009YCjEIAW) (November 2024 edition) classifies it as "EN 15151-1 type 6", a belay and rappel device without an anti-panic function, and states it meets the EU PPE Regulation (EU) 2016/425.

**What does passing a standard mean?** That samples of this model passed the standard's lab tests. It doesn't mean the one in your hands is still good: it may have sat too long, met acid or been dropped. That's the subject of post 4.

## Where the manufacture year is printed

Why look for it? Because textiles and plastics in harnesses, ropes and helmets age, and a manufacturer's maximum lifetime runs from the **date of manufacture**, not the day you bought it.

[DAV's 2010 article on gear lifetime](https://sac-hoherrohn.ch/wp-content/uploads/2019/02/dav_lebensdauer-von-alpinausruestung.pdf) (in German) says that since 2007 textile products such as harnesses and slings have had to carry their year of manufacture. DAV's newer article adds that under current standards, harnesses, dynamic ropes, helmets and sewn slings must carry the year of manufacture. Where to look:

- **Rope**: the band at each end. [The UIAA 101/EN 892 pictorial](https://www.theuiaa.org/documents/safety-standards/Pictorial_UIAA101%20Dynamic%20Ropes.pdf) lists what goes on it: the rope-type symbol, length and diameter, year of manufacture and manufacturer.
- **Harness**: the sewn label. [The UIAA 105/EN 12277 pictorial](https://www.theuiaa.org/documents/safety-standards/Pictorial_UIAA105%20Harnesses.pdf) states that all harnesses shall be labelled with the year of manufacture.
- **Helmet**: check the label or marking inside the shell; if you can't find it, check the manual.
- **Metal gear**: not always marked. DAV notes that metal doesn't age with time, so metal items aren't required to show a manufacture year; when they do, it's usually encoded in the engraved serial number.

Some manufacturers encode the year in the serial number. Petzl's care notes give the example "09 365 AB 1234": 09 is the year, 365 the day of that year. Older harnesses may read "99 289 A", again with the year first. Other brands encode differently, so check the manual.

Ropes carry a date in a second place you normally can't see. At the start of his rope guide, Hard Is Easy's Ben cuts a rope open: some cores hold a plastic tape printed with the maker and the production quarter, while others have only a thin coloured strip whose colour the manufacturer changes each year, so you'd have to ask them which year it means. It's about 45 seconds from the one-minute mark. You only see that tape once the rope is cut, so for buying and routine checks, the end label is still what you read.

## Why harnesses, helmets and ropes shouldn't be bought second-hand

Back to your friend's rope.

[The UIAA's buying advice](https://theuiaa.org/uiaa/uiaa-advice-ten-things-to-consider-when-buying-climbing-gear) is blunt: the UIAA does not endorse second-hand climbing gear because its integrity can't be guaranteed, and **harnesses, helmets and ropes in particular should not be acquired second-hand**, because their compromised integrity is especially hard to detect.

[The retirement conditions in Petzl's GRIGRI technical notice](https://www.petzl.com/sfc/servlet.shepherd/version/download/068Tx000009YCjEIAW) include one more: retire it if **you don't know its full usage history**. With second-hand soft goods, you almost never know how many falls it took or whether it sat next to a car battery.

So the answer to your friend's rope is no. Even a good friend may not remember everything that rope has been through.

## Buying online and counterfeits

Three more points in the UIAA advice are about where you buy:

1. **Watch for counterfeits.** Seemingly certified products at very low prices are a warning sign.
2. **Buy online only from trusted sources**: known brands, the manufacturer, or a reputable retailer.
3. **Check the database when in doubt.** The UIAA Certified Equipment Database above lists models.

Two HowNOT2 videos are counterexamples showing that these three points lower the risk rather than remove it. In the first, Emily tells her own story: she ordered a rope online from an authorized Black Diamond dealer, and it arrived in what looked like genuine packaging, batch number and manufacture date included. Inside was a rope Black Diamond confirmed wasn't theirs; Ryan suspects it was a static rope. The title says "allegedly", and Ryan is relaying her account rather than something he saw firsthand.

## The first thing after buying: inspect and record

The UIAA's final group, "Record and Review", is the one most people skip. Two of its points:

- **Inspect on arrival.** Look for damage, deformation, incomplete stitching or corrosion; make sure it's new, clearly labelled and **not close to the end of its recommended service life**. If anything looks questionable, don't use it and contact the retailer. Maximum lifetime runs from the manufacture date, so even a brand-new harness needs a look at its year.
- **Keep a record.** Note brand, model, serial number, purchase date, inspection dates and usage to track wear and replacement.

A record-sheet template is in [post 4](/posts/climbing/2026-10-19-climbing-gear-inspection-retirement-en).

**Something you can do tonight**: start a spreadsheet with those six columns and add a row for every piece of PPE you own.

## Practice: four scenarios

Answers are under each question. Think first, then look.

**1. You've climbed three times, love it, and want to buy shoes, a harness and a belay device all at once.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Just buy shoes. Under five sessions you're still finding out whether you like it (Climbing Ninjette's experience, not a hard rule). Buy a harness and belay device once you top-rope regularly and have passed a belay test; by then you'll know better what you want.

</details>

**2. An online listing offers a "brand new, never used" helmet at a third of retail, with no markings visible in the photos.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Don't buy it. The UIAA warns that seemingly certified gear at very low prices may be counterfeit, and without a visible CE or UIAA marking you can't confirm which standard it meets. Even if genuine, you don't know how many years it sat, or where, before it was "never used".

</details>

**3. You buy a harness in a shop, get home, and find the label says it was made six years ago.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The UIAA says to check on purchase that gear isn't close to the end of its recommended service life. At Petzl, for example, [plastics and textiles have a maximum lifetime of 10 years from manufacture](https://products.papertrail.io/downloads/petzl/d08231b_entretien-assureurs-descendeurs_EN.pdf), so six are already gone. Take it back and ask for one made more recently.

</details>

**4. A senior club member wants to sell you a rope: "Only climbed on a few times, basically new."**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The UIAA says ropes shouldn't be bought second-hand, and Petzl's retirement conditions include not knowing the full usage history. You can't verify "a few times", or whether it ever met acid or a big fall. Decline politely and buy new.

</details>

## Further viewing: gear-testing and instruction channels

The videos embedded in this post come from the channels below. They're a good place to keep digging; keep each channel's limits in mind as you watch.

- **[HowNOT2](https://www.youtube.com/@HowNOT2)**: run by Ryan Jenks. It started in 2016 as highline safety instruction and grew into breaking climbing, caving and canyoneering gear, plus the Bolt Busters series that pulls bolts out of real rock. Its value is in the cases beyond the rated number: knots, wear, old gear, bad placements. Limits: most tests are slow pulls on a home-built rig with one to three samples, and Ryan himself calls it entertainment, not science; the channel is backed by an online gear shop, and some tested items are sold there.
- **[Hard Is Easy](https://www.youtube.com/@HardIsEasy)**: run solo by a climber who signs as Ben, a former software developer. After watching a hard catch slam a climber into the rock in Tonsai, Thailand, he designed his first experiment; the channel now focuses on experiments and physics explanations about belaying, ropes, carabiners and belay devices, with several recent episodes filmed in Mammut's lab. Limits: most experiments are his own design, not EN/UIAA standard tests.
- **[JB Mountain Skills](https://www.youtube.com/@JBMountainSkills)**: run by Jez, a climbing and mountaineering instructor in North Wales; lots of hands-on anchor and top-rope setup lessons. Limits: one instructor's experience and UK practice, no test data.
