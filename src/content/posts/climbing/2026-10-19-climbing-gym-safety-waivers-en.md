> 🌏 [中文版](/posts/climbing/2026-10-19-climbing-gym-safety-waivers)

On your first visit to a climbing gym, the front desk asks you to sign something on a tablet or on paper. Most people sign without reading it. What does it say? If you get hurt inside, does it stop you from being compensated? And when the gym says "we're insured", is that insurance for you?

This post answers three questions from the climber's side: does the form you sign hold up in Taiwan, who is liable when something goes wrong, and what the gym's insurance covers. It ends with how to judge a gym's safety management and what to ask before you climb. How gyms run as businesses is out of scope.

The previous post, [The Route Setter's Job](/posts/climbing/2026-10-19-route-setter-career-en), noted that setters themselves get injured a lot. A gym is a place where risk has to be managed; this post looks at who carries that risk.

> This post summarises public information on laws and court rulings. It is not legal advice. For an actual dispute, talk to a lawyer or your local consumer service centre.

## What the form you sign says

Take [T-UP Climbing's Zhonghe branch](https://tupclimbing.tw/terms-and-conditions/78) (in Mandarin). Its form is called "Safety Rules and Personal Responsibility Statement" in Chinese and "Liability Waiver" in English. It has two parts:

- **Safety rules**: house rules, such as no sitting on the bouldering mats, no belaying by anyone under 16, and no private coaching.
- **Personal responsibility statement**: you say you know climbing can cause injury or death, you understand and will follow the safety rules, you have assessed your own health, and you take responsibility for your own safety and conduct.

Notice what it doesn't say: "the gym accepts no responsibility for any accident." The only exclusion-like sentence is in the usage rules, "the gym is not responsible for any loss of property", and that is about the belongings you bring, not injuries you suffer inside.

## "At your own risk" doesn't hold in Taiwan

[Article 10-1](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=J0170001&flno=10-1) of the Consumer Protection Act reads: "The liability of business operators for damages to consumers or third parties under this Section shall not be limited or waived by prior agreement." "This Section" means Articles 7 to 10: goods and services must meet a reasonably expected level of safety, and the business must compensate when they don't. An injury at a gym caused by an unsafe wall, floor or service falls in that category.

A gym provides a service and you are the consumer. So if a waiver says "the gym bears no liability for any accident" or "all consequences are my own responsibility", that clause is void for this kind of liability. The authorities have said so repeatedly:

- In 2019 the Executive Yuan's Department of Consumer Protection [inspected 20 climbing and trampoline gyms](https://www.anntw.com/articles/20190917-sT5Z) (in Mandarin). Twelve asked customers to sign liability waivers. The department's position: "Exclusion clauses conflict with the Consumer Protection Act and are void."
- In 2023 New Taipei City [jointly inspected six indoor climbing gyms](https://www.ntpc.gov.tw/ch/home.jsp?id=e8ca970cde5c00e1&dataserno=e47c5c455d0250e61868788488bf5d1a) (in Mandarin). All six failed; four had exclusion clauses that violated the Act.
- [Taipei's Department of Sports 2026 inspection items for climbing gyms](https://industry.sports.gov.taipei/announcement/?K=286) (in Mandarin) list, as a separate item, that the operator must not limit or waive its liability in advance in writing, verbally or by any other means.

### So what's the point of signing?

Signing doesn't take away your right to claim, but it isn't meaningless either. In the statement you acknowledge the risks and agree to follow the rules. If an accident is linked to you breaking those rules, your own negligence may reduce the compensation. The Neihu case below is an example.

**Something you can do tonight**: next time you're at the gym, spend two minutes reading the entry form. Is it telling you the rules, or asking you to give up your rights?

## Who is liable: the Neihu auto-belay case

In 2020 a climber who held top-rope and lead belay cards was using an auto belay alone on the outdoor top-rope wall at Taipei's Neihu Sports Center. On her third climb she forgot to clip in and fell to her death. Her family sued under the Consumer Protection Act. The two rulings:

| Court | Outcome | Reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| Shilin District Court (first instance, October 2023) | Operator 70% liable, [ordered to pay NT$1.05 million](https://news.pts.org.tw/article/663770) (in Mandarin) | The warning signs did not provide adequate safety; the operator bears no-fault liability under the Act |
| Taiwan High Court (second instance, October 2024, still appealable when reported) | The climber's own share cut to 5%; [operator to pay a further NT$375,000](https://cnews.com.tw/224241015a04/) (in Mandarin) | No staff were supervising at the time and there was no warning reminding users to clip in, so the service fell short of the safety that could reasonably be expected |

The case shows two things:

1. **Forgetting to clip in doesn't make it all your fault.** The courts looked at whether the operator did its part: warnings, someone on the floor. The appeal court even said the climber's slip was exactly what the gym's safety service should have guarded against.
2. **Your own negligence still counts.** The difference between the two rulings was how much the climber herself was responsible for. The appeal court noted that she had long training and a belay card, and a little attention could have prevented it.

Not clipping in is the most common auto-belay accident. In the [DAV's 2025 gym accident statistics](https://www.alpenverein.de/verband/bergsport/sicherheitsforschung/kletterhallen-unfallstatistik/kletterhallenunfallstatistik-2025) (in German), all five ground-fall accidents on auto belays happened because the climber wasn't clipped in, and three of those climbers had more than ten years' experience.

Abroad there's another kind of case: in 2019 at Vertical World in Seattle, a climber on an auto belay fell about 30 feet. The two sides disputed what happened, and in 2023 the case [settled for US$6 million](https://www.climbing.com/news/gym-and-auto-belay-manufacturer-to-pay-6m-in-settlement-for-auto-belay-accident/), with the device maker paying US$5 million and the gym US$1 million.

**Something you can do tonight**: build one habit. Every time you use an auto belay, clip in, then tug the carabiner by hand to confirm it's on your harness belay loop before you leave the ground.

### The US is different

Waivers work very differently in the US. Most states, in principle, accept that a waiver can release a business from ordinary negligence but usually not gross negligence, and the details vary by state. In 2014 the Oregon Supreme Court held a waiver unenforceable in a ski-resort case (Bagley v. Mt. Bachelor), after which climbing gym waivers there were also on shaky ground. [SB 1517](https://cwapro.org/blog/a-win-for-climbing-gyms-in-oregon), signed into law in early 2026, restored their effect while listing liabilities that can't be waived, such as gross negligence and improper maintenance or inspection of safety equipment.

So when you read online that "once you sign a waiver you can't sue", that's usually about the US and doesn't apply in Taiwan.

## The gym's insurance: who does it cover?

When a gym says "we're insured", it usually means **public liability insurance**. The logic: when the business is legally liable, the insurer pays the injured person on its behalf.

A lawyer's 2026 [column](https://www.legis-pedia.com/columns/7/articles/1458) (in Mandarin) puts it plainly: the insurer pays only once the business is found legally liable. In other words, getting hurt doesn't guarantee the gym's insurer will pay. If you want cover whenever you're injured, buy your own, such as an activity-specific policy. [T-UP's entry rules](https://tupclimbing.tw/terms-and-conditions/78) also state that the entry fee doesn't include travel or personal accident insurance and you should buy your own.

### Does the law require gyms to be insured?

We found no national law requiring private climbing gyms to carry insurance. [Article 44](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawAll.aspx?pcode=H0120001) of the Sports Act only authorises the central government to set safety and insurance rules for "public sports facilities". In 2019 the Ministry of Education's Sports Administration (whose work passed to the Ministry of Sports in September 2025) said it had drafted Articles 44-1 and 44-2 to bring private sports venues under regulation, but the latest amended text, from July 2026, contains neither article.

The insurance requirement sits at the local level. Taipei is one example:

- The [Taipei City Regulations on Mandatory Public Liability Insurance for Consumer Venues](https://laws.gov.taipei/Law/LawSearch/LawArticleContent/FL025415) (in Mandarin; amended 7 January 2025) require the venues listed in the annex to be insured. Minimum cover per venue: NT$6 million per person for bodily injury or death, NT$30 million per incident for bodily injury or death, NT$3 million per incident for property damage, and NT$66 million in total per year.
- Insured businesses must post the policy and proof of payment somewhere prominent in the venue so customers can see them.

**Does the annex list climbing gyms?** Going through the [annex](https://laws.gov.taipei/Law/LawSearch/LawFileList/FL025415) (in Mandarin) line by line, there is no entry for "climbing gym" or "bouldering gym". The closest is category six, "sports and leisure venues with low-density use", which lists ice rinks, swimming pools, ball-sports grounds, sports venues and fitness centres, among others, with the Department of Sports as the enforcing agency. Article 3 says that when it's unclear whether a venue not in the annex must be insured, the city government decides based on the venue category definitions.

So whether a Taipei climbing gym counts as a "sports venue" or "fitness centre" and must be insured isn't answered by the text; it depends on the city's decision. In practice, the Department of Sports' 2026 notice requires climbing gym operators to comply with "public liability insurance and related rules", and the 2019 central inspection also checked insurance (4 of the 20 venues were uninsured or under-insured). Other cities and counties have different rules; the insurance industry association keeps a [table of minimum mandatory public liability cover by local government](https://www.nlia.org.tw/wp-content/uploads/doc/casualty01/%E7%8F%BE%E6%9C%89%E6%B3%95%E8%A6%8F%E5%BC%B7%E5%88%B6%E6%A5%AD%E8%80%85%E6%87%89%E6%8A%95%E4%BF%9D%E5%85%AC%E5%85%B1%E6%84%8F%E5%A4%96%E9%9A%AA-115.08.pdf) (in Mandarin).

**Something you can do tonight**: next time you're at the gym, look near the front desk for a posted public liability policy. For Taipei venues covered by the mandatory rules, posting it is required.

## How to judge whether a gym is safe

Taiwan has no law specifically on climbing gym facility specifications. The same [lawyer's observation](https://www.legis-pedia.com/columns/7/articles/1458) is that courts mostly refer to the Chinese Taipei Alpine Association's *Sport Climbing Safety Manual* to decide whether a facility meets the standard.

The most concrete, usable checklist is the [climbing gym inspection items Taipei's Department of Sports published on 11 February 2026](https://industry.sports.gov.taipei/announcement/?K=286). The most useful ones for climbers:

- **Staff on the floor**: enough staff during opening hours to watch every area, at least one with a coaching licence and a first-aid certificate, and an AED on site.
- **Induction**: before bouldering or leading, the operator must give users training or a briefing.
- **Equipment**: belay devices, harnesses and climbing gear must be UIAA-approved or meet CE or other international standards.
- **Maintenance records**: a plan for maintaining, repairing, servicing and regularly inspecting equipment, with records kept at least five years.
- **Bouldering mats**: 30–50 cm thick, level and without gaps; extending at least 1 m beyond the sides of the wall and at least 3 m out from it.
- **Staff qualifications**: coaches, judges and route setters on site must hold a Level C (or equivalent) or higher certificate, valid within four years.

International standards give a comparison. According to [a summary by French wall maker Pyramide](https://pyramide.eu/en/standards-information/specific-standards), Europe's EN 12572-1 requires an artificial wall to be marked with the manufacturer, the standard number, the installation date and the date of the next major inspection. The Climbing Wall Association's [structural inspection specification](https://cwapro.org/file/secure/cwainspectionspecfinal2022.pdf) says that without manufacturer guidance, indoor walls should be inspected at least every 48 months.

Inspection results are a reminder that none of this is a given. In the [2019 central inspection](https://www.anntw.com/articles/20190917-sT5Z), 18 of the 20 climbing and trampoline gyms failed on facility and equipment safety management. In [New Taipei in 2023](https://www.ntpc.gov.tw/ch/home.jsp?id=e8ca970cde5c00e1&dataserno=e47c5c455d0250e61868788488bf5d1a), all six climbing gyms failed, with problems including fire safety, building safety declarations and missing warnings in the mat area.

### Questions to ask before you climb

1. On a first visit, will you show me how to fall in the bouldering area and how to use the auto belays?
2. How many staff are on the floor during opening hours? Who has first aid? Where's the AED?
3. How often are the auto belays inspected? When was the last inspection?
4. Is the wall marked with its next inspection date?
5. Do you carry public liability insurance? Where is the policy posted?
6. What certificates do your coaches and route setters hold?

Good gyms can usually answer. A gym that can't isn't necessarily dangerous, but it's worth keeping an eye out.

**Something you can do tonight**: pick two of these questions and ask the front desk on your next visit.

## Practice: four scenarios

The answer is under each one. Think first, then look.

**1. The waiver you signed says "Any accident I have in the gym is entirely my own responsibility and has nothing to do with the gym." You sprain your ankle in the bouldering area because of a gap between mats. The gym says you signed, so you can't claim.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Under Article 10-1 of the Consumer Protection Act, the business's liability for damages can't be limited or waived in advance, so that clause is void. A gap between mats is a facility safety problem; both the CTAA's *Sport Climbing Safety Manual* and Taipei's inspection items require level mats without gaps. You can still assert your rights. This is not legal advice; for an actual dispute, talk to a lawyer or a consumer service centre.

</details>

**2. A friend says: "The gym has public liability insurance, so if I get hurt I'll definitely get paid."**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Not necessarily. Public liability insurance pays only after the business is found legally liable. If the accident was entirely your own doing, you may get nothing. If you want cover whenever you're hurt, buy your own personal accident or activity insurance.

</details>

**3. Using an auto belay, you notice its carabiner lying on the floor. The person before you seems to have climbed without clipping in.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Not clipping in is the most common auto-belay accident; in the DAV's 2025 statistics, all five auto-belay ground falls had this cause. If the person on the wall hasn't climbed high yet, call out right away for them to stop and come back down. Then tell the staff and ask the gym to consider stronger reminders. And every time you clip in yourself, tug to check.

</details>

**4. You read online: "Once you sign a waiver, you can't sue the gym."**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

That's mostly about the US. Most US states accept that waivers can release ordinary negligence, but Taiwan's Consumer Protection Act says business liability can't be waived in advance. Your own negligence may still reduce the award: in the Neihu auto-belay case, the appeal court held the climber 5% responsible.

</details>
