In the shop you pick up a harness, step in, pull the waistbelt to where it "seems about right" and tighten it. The staff ask if you'd like to hang in it to check. You say no; the gym's rental harness goes on the same way.

This is post 2 of the Climbing Gear series. [Post 0](/posts/climbing/2026-10-19-climbing-gear-buying-order-en) explained that harnesses and helmets are personal protective equipment, with standards and labels. But [the UIAA's 2026 buying advice](https://theuiaa.org/uiaa/uiaa-advice-ten-things-to-consider-when-buying-climbing-gear) singles them out: for harnesses and helmets, **fit affects their ability to protect you**. Try before you buy where possible, and since sizing varies a lot between brands, use the manufacturer's size chart rather than S, M or L.

This post covers the parts of a harness, choosing a type, fitting it, and choosing and fitting a helmet. When to retire them is in [post 4](/posts/climbing/2026-10-19-climbing-gear-inspection-retirement-en).

## The five parts of a harness

Names as used in [Petzl Taiwan's harness guide](https://www.petzltw.com/pages/舒適的盪在空中指南_如何挑選攀岩吊帶) (in Chinese, published 2026-07-16):

- **Waistbelt**: goes around your waist, adjusted with a metal buckle.
- **Leg loops**: go around your thighs; adjustable or fixed.
- **Belay loop**: the loop at the front joining waistbelt and leg loops. [REI's harness guide](https://www.rei.com/learn/expert-advice/climbing-harness.html) calls it the strongest point on the harness; your locking carabiner goes here when belaying or rappelling.
- **Tie-in points**: two small loops on the waistbelt and leg loops that the rope passes through. Petzl Taiwan stresses that **both must be threaded**, never just one.
- **Gear loops**: loops on the sides for carrying gear. Petzl Taiwan says **gear loops must never bear body weight**; they only carry equipment.

There are also **elastic leg-loop straps** at the back, which hold the leg loops in position on your thighs.

## Harness types: choose by what you'll climb

[REI](https://www.rei.com/learn/expert-advice/climbing-harness.html) sorts harnesses into several types:

| Type | Features | Suits |
|---|---|---|
| Sport/indoor | Self-locking buckle, 2–4 gear loops (usually 2 is enough), light | Gym, outdoor sport |
| Trad | Adjustable leg loops, 4 or more gear loops, thicker padding | Placing your own gear, long hours on a route |
| Alpine | Fully adjustable waist and legs, 4 or fewer gear loops, thin belay loop | Mountains, easy on and off |

For gear loops, Petzl Taiwan's rule of thumb is easy to remember:

- **Indoor top-roping**: you only carry a belay device, so 2 are enough.
- **Outdoor sport, single pitch**: you only carry quickdraws, so about 4.
- **Trad and multi-pitch**: more gear, so 6 is more comfortable.

Adjustable or fixed leg loops? Petzl Taiwan says adjustable loops let you fit over thicker winter layers outdoors; fixed loops are lighter and skip the buckles.

**Something you can do tonight**: write down what you're most likely to climb in the next year: only the gym, outdoor sport, or starting trad. Use that to decide how many gear loops you need, so a "do-everything" harness doesn't tempt you into buying something you won't use.

## Fitting: waistbelt above the hips, tight enough not to slip out upside down

Two Taiwanese sources seem to put the waistbelt in different places:

- [Petzl Taiwan](https://www.petzltw.com/pages/舒適的盪在空中指南_如何挑選攀岩吊帶) (in Chinese): wear the waistbelt **above the hip bones**, tightened fully so it sits firmly on them and you won't slip out the top if you end up upside down.
- [Adventure Taiwan](https://www.adventuretaiwan.com/how-to-wear-climbing-harness/) (in Chinese): pull it up to roughly **belly-button height**, tight enough that you can slide a flat hand between body and belt but can't easily turn it.

[REI](https://www.rei.com/learn/expert-advice/climbing-harness.html) connects the two: put the waistbelt slightly above your iliac crest, which is **near belly-button level for most people**. REI's tightness check is no more than a two-finger gap between waist and belt. So the two descriptions mostly agree; where your belly button sits varies, so go by your hip bones, and your harness's manual takes priority.

Leg loops matter less. Petzl Taiwan says comfortable and snug is enough. REI says tighter leg loops are more comfortable when hanging but restrict movement, looser ones the opposite; both are safe, so it's personal preference.

### Hang in it in the shop

REI says you can't know whether a harness is comfortable without hanging in it or weighting it. Some shops have a test station or a wall where you can hang on a rope. Once you're hanging, check:

- **Can you sit upright?** Like sitting in a chair, without using your core. If you have to work to stay upright, shorten the elastic straps at the back of the leg loops; if that doesn't help, try another harness.
- **Does the waistbelt shift?** If so, tighten until it stops. Before weighting it, also try pulling the waistbelt down over your hips; you shouldn't be able to. If the shop allows it, slowly invert to test for slippage.
- **Any pressure points?** If so, consider another model.

### Buckles: does yours need doubling back?

Adventure Taiwan says newer harnesses usually don't need doubling back, but some do: the webbing is threaded through the buckle and then back again. To tell, look at the buckle's design, or for a word like **DANGER** printed on it; if it's there, double it back. REI's fitting steps also say to make sure every buckle is doubled back (not needed on self-locking buckles).

A rental harness and your own may have different buckles, so the first thing with any new harness is to check how its buckle works. The partner-check routine is in [gym top-rope belaying](/posts/climbing/2026-10-17-gym-top-rope-belay-en).

**Something you can do tonight**: put on your harness (or your next rental), check the two-finger gap at the waistbelt, then try to pull the belt down over your hips. If it goes, it's too loose.

<details><summary>What EN 12277/UIAA 105 tests</summary>

[The UIAA 105/EN 12277 pictorial](https://www.theuiaa.org/documents/safety-standards/Pictorial_UIAA105%20Harnesses.pdf) lists the key numbers:

- Sit-harness strength test: a dummy in the harness is pulled to 15 kN.
- Every loop provided for abseiling (rappelling) must withstand at least 15 kN (10 kN for small-body versions).
- Slippage at every buckle: 20 mm maximum.
- All harnesses must be labelled with the year of manufacture.
- Load-bearing stitching must contrast with the webbing in colour or surface appearance; the UIAA adds that at least 50% of visible stitching in load-bearing parts must contrast in colour.

Petzl Taiwan's guide gives the certifications for climbing harnesses as "CE EN 12277 Type C (sit harness), and UIAA 105".

**What these numbers do and don't mean**: a new harness of this model passed these lab tests. It doesn't mean a harness worn wrongly, buckled wrongly, worn out or past its lifetime can take the same load. The contrasting stitching is there so you can see when it's worn through, which post 4 uses.

</details>

## Helmets: foam shell or hardshell

There are two constructions. [REI's helmet guide](https://www.rei.com/learn/expert-advice/climbing-helmet.html) warns that brands use different names, so judge by construction:

| Type | Construction | Pros | Cons |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hardshell | Very hard outer shell (usually ABS plastic), strap suspension, thin foam liner | Cheap, durable, long-lasting | Not as light or ventilated as foam |
| Foam shell (often called EPS) | Thick foam under a thin shell; absorbs impact by deforming | Light, well ventilated | More expensive |

Which to choose? The two sources don't quite agree:

- [DAV's 2010 article on gear lifetime](https://sac-hoherrohn.ch/wp-content/uploads/2019/02/dav_lebensdauer-von-alpinausruestung.pdf) (in German): hardshells generally cope better with rockfall and are clearly tougher under multiple impacts (tumbling down broken ground); light foam helmets give the best energy absorption in a single impact. So DAV suggests **foam rather for alpine sport climbing, hardshell for classic alpine terrain**.
- REI: for **multi-pitch trad and mountaineering**, where you carry a lot and wear the helmet for hours, low weight and ventilation matter, so consider foam; for cool-weather single-pitch sport, where you can take the helmet off between climbs, you can trade comfort for a cheaper, durable hardshell.

They weigh different things: DAV the kind of impact, REI the burden of wearing it all day. DAV is writing about alpine settings; REI singles out single-pitch sport climbing on its own. Both are laid out here so you can match each to the terrain you're heading for.

### Fitting a helmet

REI's steps:

1. Put the helmet on squarely, with the front rim straight across your forehead, not tipped back.
2. Adjust it, then shake your head side to side and tilt it back slightly. The helmet should stay snug and not move.
3. Buckle the chin strap with **no slack**. The front and rear straps should form a "Y" around each ear.

REI also suggests trying the adjustment system to see how intuitive it is. In the cold you may wear a hat under it and need to readjust.

Why belayers outdoors wear helmets too is covered, with accident reports, in [outdoor belaying](/posts/climbing/2026-10-17-outdoor-belay-en); it isn't repeated here.

**Something you can do tonight**: if you own a helmet, run through REI's three steps, especially the head shake and the chin strap. Plenty of chin straps are loose.

<details><summary>What EN 12492/UIAA 106 tests</summary>

[The UIAA 106/EN 12492 pictorial](https://www.theuiaa.org/documents/safety-standards/Pictorial_UIAA106%20Helmets.pdf):

- **Top energy absorption**: a 5 kg mass dropped 2 m onto the helmet; force transmitted to the headform must be no more than 10 kN for EN, 8 kN for UIAA.
- **Front, side and rear absorption**: 5 kg from 0.5 m at 60 degrees, same limits.
- **Penetration**: a 3 kg cone dropped 1 m must not touch the headform.
- **Chin strap**: 500 N with no breakage and no more than 25 mm extension.
- **Slippage**: pulled from the front and back, the helmet must not come off the headform.

The table also shows the UIAA being stricter than EN on some items: 8 kN versus 10 kN for top absorption.

**What it does and doesn't mean**: passing shows a new helmet can absorb these impacts. It doesn't mean a helmet that's been hit, warped in the sun or covered in stickers still can. Post 4 covers those.

</details>

## Practice: four scenarios

Answers are under each question. Think first, then look.

**1. You try a harness with the waistbelt on your belly, but you can pull it down past your hips by hand.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Too loose, or too low. Petzl Taiwan says the belt goes above the hip bones, tightened fully so you can't slip out upside down; REI's check is no more than two fingers of gap and no pulling it past your hips. Tighten and try again; if it still comes down at its tightest, go down a size or try another model.

</details>

**2. You only top-rope in the gym. The staff recommend a trad harness with 6 gear loops and adjustable legs, "so you can use it outside later."**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Petzl Taiwan's guidance is 2 gear loops for indoor top-roping and about 4 for outdoor sport. A trad harness is heavier and bulkier than you need now. Buy for what you'll climb in the next year.

</details>

**3. A friend lends you an old harness with "DANGER" printed on the buckle and says, "Just thread it through."**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Adventure Taiwan says buckles with a word like DANGER usually need doubling back: thread the webbing through, then back again. Without that, the buckle may not hold. Do a partner check once it's on. Also, you don't know a borrowed harness's history or manufacture year; as post 0 noted, Petzl lists "you do not know its full usage history" as a reason to retire gear.

</details>

**4. Your helmet sits high on your forehead, the chin strap is slack, and it wobbles when you shake your head.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Follow REI's steps: wear it squarely with the rim across your forehead, adjust until it doesn't move when you shake your head, and buckle the chin strap with no slack, the straps forming a Y around your ears. If you can't stop it moving, try another model. The UIAA also notes that fit affects how well a helmet protects you.

</details>
