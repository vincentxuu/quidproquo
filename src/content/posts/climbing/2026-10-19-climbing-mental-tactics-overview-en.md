You've just got your gym lead card. There's a 5.10a you've top-roped several times. On lead, one move above the third quickdraw, your hands clamp down, your legs start shaking, and you call take. Back on the ground you're confused: you know you can climb it, so what happened?

This is the entry point to the Climbing Mental Game and Tactics series. It gives no methods yet. It answers three questions: what research says about lead feeling scarier than top-rope, how anxiety makes you climb worse, and whether you fear falling or failing. The methods start in post 1.

If you haven't learned to lead yet, start with [gym lead climbing](/posts/climbing/2026-10-17-gym-lead-climbing-en). If you're not sure whether you're stuck on technique, mindset or fitness, run the three-step check in [should you start training when you plateau](/posts/climbing/2026-10-18-climbing-training-diagnosis-en). This series picks up that post's "mindset" branch.

Every recommendation in this series carries an evidence label:

- **Research**: backed by an experiment or measurement.
- **Coaching consensus**: what coaches do in practice, with no controlled study behind it.
- **Inference**: borrowed from research in other fields and not tested directly in climbing.

## Fine on top-rope, frozen on lead: what the research says

The most direct evidence comes from climbing the same route twice. Twenty-six intermediate sport climbers each climbed it once on top-rope and once on lead, and took about the same time both ways. On lead, both cognitive and somatic anxiety were higher, confidence was lower, and oxygen uptake was higher ([Turkey, Aras and Akalan 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24445539/)). Same moves, but fear of falling made them spend more energy. (Research)

It also shows up onsight. Thirty-two intermediate climbers each onsighted three routes of the same grade, in random order: one on top-rope, one on lead, and one on lead with a run-out (bolts deliberately spaced further apart). In both lead conditions movement was less fluent, cognitive anxiety rose and confidence dropped. Only the run-out condition also raised somatic anxiety clearly ([Giles et al. 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40484106/)). (Research)

The largest sample comes from Poland: 100 climbers at gyms in three cities. Each climbed on top-rope first and then on lead, always in that order, and filled in an anxiety questionnaire afterwards. Anxiety was clearly higher on lead (a mean of 40.1 against 33.1), and 46% scored high for anxiety on lead ([Swinarska et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12859972/)). Read the limits alongside the result. It's a cross-sectional study, so it shows association only. It used a convenience sample and didn't control for climbing experience or grade. And because lead was always the second climb, fatigue and order effects are mixed into the difference. (Research)

### Not every study finds a difference

Twenty-one advanced climbers onsighted routes near their limit. Anxiety, confidence and cortisol were the same on lead and top-rope; only heart rate in the last part of the route was higher on lead ([Fryer et al. 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22299663/)). The authors read this as climbers who lead often, and are used to lead falls, not finding lead more stressful.

Intermediate climbers have exceptions too. Nineteen intermediates each onsighted the same 6a (about 5.10b), either on lead or on top-rope, and anxiety, confidence and cortisol didn't differ by style ([Draper et al. 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21984397/)). A route can only be onsighted once, so this was probably a lead group compared with a top-rope group. That design is weaker than the studies above, where the same person climbed both ways. (Inference: the abstract doesn't describe the grouping.)

So the accurate summary is: most studies find intermediate climbers more anxious on lead, but not every time. Samples range from 9 to 100 people, most between 20 and 30, and every study compares people at a single point in time. "You'll get used to it if you lead more" is plausible, but nobody has followed the same climbers over years to show it. (Inference)

### The more likely and longer the fall, the bigger the stress

Nine advanced female climbers each climbed twice: once clipping every quickdraw, once clipping only half. With half clipped, total catecholamines in the blood (the adrenaline family of stress hormones) rose to 890% of baseline, against 568% with everything clipped ([Baláš et al. 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27400414/)). The longer the potential fall, the louder the body's alarm. (Research, very small sample)

Difficulty matters too. Sixty-one intermediate to advanced climbers each onsighted one lead route, split into three groups: above, at, or below their onsight grade. The above-grade group had clearly higher anxiety and lower confidence, and movement quality improved as climbers' ability rose relative to the route ([Giles et al. 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42304823/)). (Research)

**Try this next session**: at the gym, pick a route you can top-rope cleanly. Before you start, note in your phone how nervous you are on a 0–10 scale. Then lead it once and note the score again before you start. If the lead score is clearly higher, this series is written for you. (Series suggestion)

## How anxiety makes you climb worse

A Dutch team built two identical traverses on a climbing wall, one low and one high, and had novices climb each. On the high one, climbing took longer, there were more exploratory movements, climbers gripped holds for longer, and their movements slowed ([Pijpers et al. 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16025756/)). They explain this with the conscious processing hypothesis: pressure turns your attention to your own body, and you start consciously controlling moves that were automatic, which makes them less smooth. (Research)

The same team later added eye tracking. When anxious, climbers fixated more often and for longer, mostly on the holds used for climbing, and their search rate dropped ([Nieuwenhuys et al. 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18490789/)). (Research) The abstract doesn't separate handholds from footholds. The idea that a scared climber's gaze locks onto the hold in front of them and misses the feet and the line ahead is inferred from the slower search rate. (Inference)

That's what happened above your third quickdraw: you gripped harder than you needed, hung on holds too long, and your feet scrabbled. You didn't suddenly get weaker. Anxiety changed how you move.

**Try this next session**: ask a friend to film you on lead. When you watch it back, look for only two things: where you paused longest, and where you placed a foot more than once. That's where anxiety started taking over. (Series suggestion)

## Anxiety isn't always the enemy

The studies above make it tempting to conclude that less anxiety is always better. Three British studies point another way: when anxiety was higher, climbers usually put in more effort and performed correspondingly better ([Hardy and Hutchinson 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17999221/)). The authors add that cognitive anxiety may not be the main driver of those changes in effort and performance. Individuals also varied a lot: some could turn anxiety-driven effort into performance, others couldn't. (Research)

In 2025 a New Zealand team reviewed 83 climbing-psychology studies. One finding: climbing ability is associated with how far climbers interpret anxiety as helpful ([Mangan et al. 2025, systematic review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39389518/)). That's a correlation. It doesn't mean learning to reinterpret anxiety will make you climb harder. (Research)

Researchers and coaches don't give quite the same advice. The conclusion of Giles 2025 recommends lowering cognitive anxiety and building confidence. Hazel Findlay, in her [mental training FAQ](https://www.hazel-findlay.com/blog/faqs-about-building-a-strong-mind-for-climbing), says she's often scared herself. To her, the scary parts of climbing are the interesting parts. She wouldn't want to remove fear completely; the point is to set the challenge at the right level (coaching consensus). This series presents both views side by side, and [post 2](/posts/climbing/2026-10-19-climbing-performance-anxiety-en) covers how to choose.

**Try this next session**: think back to the best route you've climbed recently. Before you started, were you completely calm, or a bit nervous? Write it down. It will help you decide later whether to turn anxiety down or learn to use it. (Series suggestion)

## Two fears: falling and failing

In the same FAQ, Findlay says the climbers who come to her most often report three fears: fear of falling (including fear of heights, exposure and getting hurt), fear of failure, and performance anxiety. She treats them differently. Fear of falling is handled by setting the mental challenge at the right level and progressing fall practice step by step. Fear of failure is partly handled by redefining what "failure" means to you (coaching consensus).

She also says something every new leader should hear:

> "A 7a can feel 10 times harder on lead compared to top-rope."

In other words, a route's difficulty isn't only its grade; it also has a mental challenge level. Top-roping 7a doesn't mean you're ready to lead 7a (coaching consensus).

Having both is common. In one small qualitative study, five sport climbers attended Rational Emotive Behaviour Therapy (REBT) workshops and three were then interviewed ([Lumby et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1123/tsp.2025-0179)). The "fear" they described was mostly about pressure to perform, not physical safety. It's a tiny, tentative study, but it's a reminder that being able to take falls doesn't mean you've cleared the mental hurdle.

**Try this next session**: write down the last three times you called take or lowered off on lead. For each, ask: in that moment, was I afraid of "what happens if I fall" or "what happens if I don't finish"? Whichever came up more often, read the matching post in the table first. ## When not to go it alone

This series is about the ordinary nerves and fear of everyday climbers. If you panic whenever you get near the situation where you were hurt in a fall, if you have an anxiety disorder, or if you're under heavy stress in the rest of your life, fall practice may not help and could make things worse. The section "When fall practice backfires" in [post 1](/posts/climbing/2026-10-19-climbing-fear-of-falling-en) describes the warning signs. If they apply to you, talk to a psychologist or psychiatrist.

**Try this next session**: check yourself against the three situations above. If any of them fits, hold off on fall practice, write down what's going on, and take it to a psychologist. (Series suggestion)

## Practice: four scenarios

**1. You top-rope 5.10c, but on lead you often freeze halfway up a 5.10a. A friend says: "Your fingers are just weak. Get on a fingerboard."**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Don't rush into strength training. Fine on top-rope but stuck on lead is exactly the mindset problem in step two of the [training diagnosis](/posts/climbing/2026-10-18-climbing-training-diagnosis-en). Aras and Akalan 2014 and Giles 2025 both found intermediate climbers more anxious on lead, with stiffer movement and higher energy cost. Use the self-check table in the "Two fears" section to work out whether you fear falling or failing.

</details>

**2. On lead you notice you keep staring at the next handhold, and you often place a foot, then readjust it.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

This fits what Nieuwenhuys 2008 saw: when anxious, climbers fixate mostly on the holds they're using and scan less. That this pulls your eyes away from the footholds is this post's inference. It's a symptom of anxiety, not a sudden loss of footwork. Film yourself to find where it starts; that's usually the height or distance where the fear kicks in.

</details>

**3. You're not very afraid of falling and fall practice goes smoothly, but when the gym is busy and people are watching from below, you climb especially badly.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

This looks more like fear of failure or performance anxiety than fear of falling. Findlay treats the two separately, and most of the climbers interviewed in Lumby 2026 were afraid because of pressure to perform. Read [post 2](/posts/climbing/2026-10-19-climbing-performance-anxiety-en) first.

</details>

**4. You believe you have to be "completely calm" to climb well, so whenever you feel a bit nervous before starting, you decide the day is ruined.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Anxiety isn't necessarily bad. Hardy and Hutchinson 2007 found that with higher anxiety, climbers usually tried harder and performed correspondingly better. The Mangan 2025 review also found ability linked to seeing anxiety as helpful. Some nerves are normal; the problem is nerves strong enough to distort your movement. Post 2 covers how to live with them.

</details>
