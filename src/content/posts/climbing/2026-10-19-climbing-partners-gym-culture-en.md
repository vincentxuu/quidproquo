The sentence you hear most often in a climbing gym is probably "Can anyone give me a belay?" Bouldering works alone; roped climbing needs a second person holding the rope. You trust that person with your life, and they trust you with theirs, so choosing who to climb with is a safety decision as well as a social one.

This post starts with finding a partner: where to look, how to set up a first session with a stranger, and whether to say something when you see someone else's belay error. It ends with the gym rules nobody writes on the wall. Belaying itself is covered in [Learning to Top-Rope Belay in the Gym](/posts/climbing/2026-10-17-gym-top-rope-belay-en) and [Learning to Lead in the Gym](/posts/climbing/2026-10-17-gym-lead-climbing-en). If you have never been to a gym, start with the [Climbing Beginner's Guide](/posts/climbing/2026-10-08-climbing-beginner-guide-en).

This is also the first post in The Climbing Community and Industry series. A gym holds more than you and your partner: kids, coaches, route setters and the people running the place. The series map at the end matches each of them to a post.

## Why partners matter

The British Mountaineering Council's [Your Climbing Counts survey](https://thebmc.co.uk/your-climbing-counts), launched in late 2023, collected about 5,500 responses. Nearly half said they would climb more if they had a regular partner. In the same survey, the most common way into climbing, for men and women alike, was being brought along by a friend.

Time is still the biggest limit: more than two-thirds said they would climb more with more time. A missing partner comes further down the list, but it's easier to fix than time.

**Something to do tonight:** think about the last time you skipped the gym. If the reason was "nobody to belay me", the next section is for you. If you just want to climb, bouldering and auto-belays don't need a partner.

## Where to find a partner

Climbing.com's 2025 list of [43 ways to find a climbing partner](https://www.climbing.com/culture-climbing/43-ways-find-a-climbing-partner/) includes an Instagram poll of more than 1,000 people: 86% met their partners in real life and only 14% online. It is not a scientific sample, but the direction is clear. The best places are the ones where people are physically present.

Common routes in Taiwan:

- **Gym meetups and classes.** Many gyms have regular group sessions, and your classmates on a belay or technique course are natural candidates. They climb at a similar level and have just learned the same belay routine.
- **LINE groups.** Searching LINE for 攀岩 (climbing) or 找繩伴 (looking for a partner) turns up public open chats such as [攀岩抱石找繩伴&岩友](https://openchat.line.me/tw/cover/j71r_XNpPxJIUt4CWK9lTui2J5FaLeLUAbVw9pDuM0sjyZ2x-RpVAZug_t8) (in Chinese), which had about 459 members in October 2026.
- **University mountaineering and climbing clubs.** Some universities have their own walls. The [NCKU wall](https://news-secr.ncku.edu.tw/p/404-1037-98066.php) (in Chinese) is run jointly by the mountaineering club and the student affairs office, and people from outside the university climb there on open evenings too.
- **Facebook groups.** Most regions have one, but you usually need to log in to see anything.
- **Online partner finders abroad.** When you travel or live overseas, [Mountain Project's Partner Finder](https://www.mountainproject.com/partner-finder) lets you filter by distance, age and the grades someone can lead or follow. In October 2026 the page showed about 38,000 climbers listed.

REI's [six ways to find your climbing gym community](https://www.rei.com/blog/climb/6-ways-to-find-your-climbing-gym-community) adds two routes people forget: volunteering with your local climbing advocacy group, and the gym notice board, where you can post a note looking for a partner or start a group of your own. REI also advises asking plenty of questions before you tie in with someone you met online.

**Something to do tonight:** ask your gym whether it runs group sessions, or join a local LINE group and lurk for a while to see how people arrange to climb.

## Your first session with a new partner

Say you met a new partner on Facebook and you're climbing together this weekend. The goal of the first session is to see their belay habits clearly while taking as little risk as possible.

1. **Meet at the gym, not the crag.** The gym has staff, a belay-check system and flat ground. Climb together indoors a few times before you talk about going outside.
2. **Top-rope first, not lead.** Top-rope belaying produces fewer errors than lead belaying (numbers in the next section), and you can see their basics more easily.
3. **Ask about their credentials.** Ask directly: "Do you have this gym's belay card?" "When did you last belay?" In Taiwan, gyms usually require you to pass their own belay check or hold a CBT (the national belay test) before you can top-rope unsupervised; details are in the top-rope belay post.
4. **Do a partner check.** Check each other's harness, knot, belay device and carabiner before leaving the ground. Whether they let you check them, and whether they check you without being asked, tells you something.
5. **Belay them first.** Watch how they climb and how they use calls, then swap. For your first climb, pick a route you're confident on and unlikely to fall off.
6. **Talk afterwards.** Ask how they like to be belayed, whether they want beta, and what kind of falls they're comfortable with.

### What to watch: five belay habits

When they are belaying you, you can watch for these from the ground and from the wall:

- **Does the brake hand ever leave the rope?** This matters most. A brake hand off the rope can turn a fall into a ground fall.
- **Do they keep up with taking in?** On top-rope the rope should stay tight, especially near the ground.
- **Where do they stand?** Close to the wall, but not directly under you.
- **Are they watching you?** When you look down, they should be looking up, not chatting to the next pair.
- **Is the lower smooth?** An even speed, no sudden drops.

Climbing.com's [eight signs you need a new partner](https://www.climbing.com/skills/red-flags-in-a-belaytionship-8-signs-you-need-a-new-partner/) reads like the reverse of that list:

- Dodgy gear, or refusing a partner check
- Ignoring your calls
- Not paying attention while belaying
- Sandbagging you: downplaying or misstating a route's difficulty and danger
- Spraying beta after you've asked them not to
- Doing something that broke your trust, such as taking the brake hand off the rope
- Making decisions on their own
- Constant mockery

Its advice is practical too. If their gear or belaying genuinely makes you uneasy, end the session. If they take the feedback well, treat it as a chance to get safer together. If nothing has changed next time, find someone else.

**Something to do tonight:** next time you climb with someone new, say "Let's do a partner check" before you start. How they react will tell you a lot.

## Belay errors are more common than you think

The German Alpine Club (DAV) is one of the few organisations that has studied gym accidents for years. A 2014 article in the DAV magazine, [Kampf dem Eisberg](https://bergsteigerbund.de/wp-content/uploads/2017/05/Panorama-6-2014-Sicherheitsforschung-Sicherungsfehler-Hallenklettern.pdf) (in German), summarised a 2012 observation study in gyms: lead belayers made an average of 1.4 errors per belay, top-rope belayers 0.7. Most of those errors never become accidents, because nobody happens to fall, or be lowered, at that moment. The DAV uses an iceberg as the image: accidents are the tip above the water, and below it sits a mass of errors that didn't hurt anyone.

The [DAV's 2025 gym accident statistics](https://www.alpenverein.de/verband/bergsport/sicherheitsforschung/kletterhallen-unfallstatistik/kletterhallenunfallstatistik-2025) (in German, covering Germany and Austria) show that the most dangerous moment is not always the climbing itself:

- Of 31 roped ground falls, 15 happened while lowering.
- All five auto-belay ground falls happened because the climber hadn't clipped in, and one was fatal.
- In one case, the pair did a partner check and still missed an unfinished knot.

So a partner check means actually looking, not reciting a checklist.

## Should you say something when you see an error?

When you notice a stranger belaying badly, it's easy to hesitate: will it be awkward? In [Sicherungsfehler in Kletterhallen](https://www.alpenverein.de/artikel/sicherungsfehler-in-kletterhallen_3fca5a95-06c8-48de-bcfb-d80d31059b89) (in German), the DAV's Schwiersch and Janotte offer a rule of thumb: sort the error into one of three levels by its consequence.

| Level | Consequence | Examples | What to do |
|---|---|---|---|
| Must (MUSS) | Immediate danger; a fall or a lower will cause an accident | Climber's knot not finished; belayer lets go of the brake rope for a long time; clearly too much slack near the ground, so a fall would hit the floor; lower out of control | Stop them at once, ideally before they leave the ground; step in if needed |
| Should (SOLL) | Fine for now, but an accident is likely eventually or at a bad moment | Brake hand repeatedly drifting upward; occasional slack near the ground; poor belay position | Wait until both are back on the ground, then talk as equals |
| Can (KANN) | Dangerous only in particular situations | Belaying or climbing in someone else's fall zone | A friendly tip when things are relaxed |

The "must" level is about speed. The script the article gives is short. If the climber has just left the ground: "Stop. You're not tied in properly."

The "should" level is harder, because these errors usually haven't caused problems before and the belayer doesn't think anything is wrong. The DAV suggests:

- **When:** soon after the error, but only once the climb is over and both are standing on the ground.
- **How to approach:** walk towards the pair, stop at a respectful distance, and wait until one of them makes eye contact before you speak. That leaves them in control of whether the conversation starts.
- **What to say:** describe the specific action you saw, without judging. "When you lowered just now, your brake hand came off the rope twice" works far better than "Your belaying looks a bit shaky."

The same article notes that people belay better when they feel they are being watched. Partner checks were first seen in Germany as an intrusion on personal autonomy and are now routine. Speaking up works the same way: the more people do it, the less awkward it gets.

**Something to do tonight:** memorise the three "must" examples. If you see any of them, don't think about it; call out.

## The unwritten rules of the gym

Every gym's rules differ. What follows uses the [safety code and personal responsibility statement at T-UP Zhonghe](https://tupclimbing.tw/terms-and-conditions/78) (in Chinese, with an English section) as an example, plus etiquette common elsewhere.

**Bouldering area**

- Don't sit on the mats, and don't leave things on them. T-UP recommends staying at least 2 metres from the wall.
- Once you've finished or jumped down, get off the mat.
- Before starting, check that your line won't cross anyone already on the wall. Whoever is on the wall first has priority.
- On your first visit to T-UP, staff teach you how to fall safely in the bouldering area and how to use the auto-belays. If you can't do it, they can refuse to let you climb.

**Roped area**

- Don't hang around in other people's belay space; walk through it quickly.
- Don't belay anyone if you don't know how. T-UP's rules say nobody under 16 may belay others.
- T-UP also bans unofficial technique teaching and belay practice in the gym. If you want to teach a friend, send them on a course.

**Beta**

- Most etiquette guides say: ask before you give beta. Some people want to work it out themselves; others want the full solution.
- Say it when asked, then stop. You don't need to keep adding tips after every failed attempt.

**Everything else**

- Use headphones, keep the volume low, and make sure you can still hear people's calls.
- Don't wear climbing shoes into the toilet.
- Don't leave your chalk bag on the mats or in walkways.

**Something to do tonight:** next time you're at the gym, read the rules posted at the entrance or desk. This list covers common rules; your gym's own rules come first.

## Who's in the gym: community and inclusion

A 2026 [opinion piece](https://cwapro.org/blog/what-makes-a-climbing-gym-a-third-place) on the Climbing Wall Association blog describes the gym as a "third place" outside work and home: people chat between attempts, and relationships grow out of that chat. Not everyone finds it equally easy to fit in.

In the US, several communities started by women and people of colour aim to lower the barrier to entry. [Flash Foxy](https://www.outsideonline.com/outdoor-adventure/climbing/shelma-jun-women-climb/) started in New York in 2014 and went on to run a women's climbing festival. [Brown Girls Climb](https://voyagedenver.com/interview/daily-inspiration-meet-brown-girls-climb-colorado) began as an Instagram account in autumn 2016. Black Girls Boulder was founded in 2020. A 2024 [Guardian report](https://www.theguardian.com/sport/2024/jan/25/brown-girls-climb-black-poc-rock-climbing) named concrete barriers: gyms tend to open in wealthier neighbourhoods, climbing is expensive, and competitions involve microaggressions.

Taiwan has similar events. The Little Rock gym in Neihu held a women-focused [climbing festival](https://sportz.im/the-little-rock-202203/) (in Chinese) in April 2022.

**Something to do tonight:** if you see someone standing in front of the wall unsure where to start, ask "Want to climb together?" Plenty of partnerships start that way.

## Some background: where Taiwan's gyms came from

Older climbers in Taiwan often mention Lanzhou Street. In 1997 the Chinese Taipei Alpine Association converted its Lanzhou Street building into an [indoor climbing training centre](https://atc0317.pixnet.net/blog/posts/10031696816) (in Chinese). It later ran commercially and [closed on 27 April 2012](http://kateberrysblog.blogspot.com/2018/12/stone.html) (in Chinese). Accounts of the switch differ: the timeline says it became the Taipei Climbing Club and opened to the public in 1999, while the [panel write-up](http://kateberrysblog.blogspot.com/2021/02/rock-climb-generation-4.html) says the STONE team took it over and ran it commercially from 2004.

In August 2015, [T-UP](https://tupclimbing.tw/) opened its first gym in Yangmei and later added several branches around Taipei. A veteran climber's [write-up of a 2021 panel](http://kateberrysblog.blogspot.com/2021/02/rock-climb-generation-4.html) (in Chinese) describes the atmosphere shifting from "grim and shouty" to "warm and social". That is how most people meet climbing today.

## Practice: four scenarios

Answers are under each question. Think first, then look.

**1. At the gym you notice the pair next to you. The climber is a metre off the ground, and you see their figure-eight only passes through the upper tie-in point of the harness, not the lower one.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

This is a "must" error in the DAV scheme: a fall or a lower will cause an accident. Speak up immediately: "Stop, you're not tied in properly." Ask them to come down and retie. Don't worry about awkwardness, and don't wait for eye contact.

</details>

**2. You notice a belayer who keeps the rope slack for the last few metres as the climber gets close to the ground. The climber doesn't fall and gets down fine.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

This is a "should" error: nothing happened this time, but a low fall could hit the ground. If the slack is bad enough that a fall would clearly reach the ground, treat it as a 'must' and tell the belayer to take in right away. Otherwise, wait until the climb is over and both are on the ground, walk over, stop at a polite distance, and wait for one of them to look at you. Describe what you saw: "In the last three metres the rope was a bit slack." Avoid judgements like "Your belaying isn't good."

</details>

**3. You meet a new partner in a LINE group. They say they're a strong climber and suggest going straight to Long Dong to lead on your first meeting.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Meet at the gym first. Top-rope only on the first session. Ask whether they hold a belay card and when they last belayed, do a partner check, belay them first, then let them belay you, watching whether their brake hand leaves the rope and whether they keep up with taking in. Discuss going outdoors after a few sessions, once you know each other's habits. REI's advice: ask plenty of questions before tying in with someone you met online.

</details>

**4. In the bouldering area, a friend falls at the same move three times on a problem you've just sent. You really want to tell them how to do it.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Ask first: "Want to hear how I did it?" Some people want to work it out, others want the beta. Only tell them if they say yes, and stop when you're done. Also, watch from outside the mats, not sitting on them.

</details>
