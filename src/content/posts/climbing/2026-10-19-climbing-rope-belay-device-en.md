The shop has two ropes, 9.8 mm and 8.9 mm, at about the same price. Thinner is lighter, you think, so the thin one. The staff ask: "What belay device will you use with it?" You don't know.

This is post 3 of the Climbing Gear series, and the most abstract one. You can try on the shoes and harness from the previous posts; the gear here has to work as a set, and you can't see inside it. So we start with what you can see: the label on the rope end.

This post covers choosing a rope, belay device and carabiners that work together. **It doesn't cover how to belay**; that's in [gym top-rope belaying](/posts/climbing/2026-10-17-gym-top-rope-belay-en) and [gym lead climbing](/posts/climbing/2026-10-17-gym-lead-climbing-en).

## Read the rope end: 1, ½, the twin symbol

[The UIAA 101/EN 892 pictorial](https://www.theuiaa.org/documents/safety-standards/Pictorial_UIAA101%20Dynamic%20Ropes.pdf) requires a dynamic rope to be marked with its type (EN 892 requires durable bands; UIAA 101 also allows marking printed directly on the rope):

- A **1** in a circle: single rope.
- A **½** in a circle: half rope.
- Two linked circles (often written ∞; the label may also say TWIN): twin rope.

The label also gives length, diameter, year of manufacture and manufacturer.

The three types are three different systems. From [Black Diamond's rope guide](https://blackdiamondequipment.com/blogs/stories/how-to-choose-a-climbing-rope):

- **Single**: the most common; one rope is enough, used in gyms, at sport crags and on multi-pitch. BD says singles run from about 8.8 to 10.2 mm.
- **Half and twin**: used two at a time, usually thinner (BD says mostly under 8 mm), mostly in the mountains. Twins are clipped together through every piece, functioning more like a single rope.

**Diameters overlap, so millimetres alone won't tell you.** [Mabu Valley's guide to dynamic ropes](https://mabuvalley.com/blogs/戶外豆知識/認識動力繩) (in Chinese) is clear: single, half and twin are different systems whose diameters can overlap, so check the end marking before the diameter. Some thin ropes are certified as both half and twin, like the 7.4 mm rope in BD's guide; either use is fine, but always as a pair.

If you climb only in the gym and at single-pitch sport crags, you want a rope marked **1**.

**Something you can do tonight**: at the gym, look at the end of the rope you're using. Find the circled symbol, the diameter and the year of manufacture.

## Dynamic and static rope

[Mabu Valley](https://mabuvalley.com/blogs/戶外豆知識/認識動力繩) explains that in a lead fall, dynamic rope absorbs energy by stretching in a controlled way, lowering the instantaneous load on the whole belay system. Low-stretch rope (commonly called static rope) is for fixing, ascending, descending and rope access, not for catching lead falls. BD agrees: you're not going to lead on a static rope; it's for jugging and hauling.

### Impact force: the more the rope stretches, the less it hurts

Picture falling from above your last quickdraw. The force on you, your belayer and the quickdraw at the instant the rope catches you is the impact force. The more the rope can stretch in that instant, the longer you take to slow down, and the lower the impact force. That's why lead climbing always uses dynamic rope.

Think of it in reverse, too: [DAV's 2010 article on gear lifetime](https://sac-hoherrohn.ch/wp-content/uploads/2019/02/dav_lebensdauer-von-alpinausruestung.pdf) (in German) says a rope's dynamic properties decline as it ages. [Mammut's rope-care page](https://help.mammut.com/en/article/a65324) says a fall above fall factor 1.0 compromises a rope. What fall factor is, and how much force an anchor takes in a fall, is in [building anchors](/posts/climbing/2026-10-17-building-anchors-en).

<details><summary>What EN 892/UIAA 101 tests</summary>

[The UIAA 101/EN 892 pictorial](https://www.theuiaa.org/documents/safety-standards/Pictorial_UIAA101%20Dynamic%20Ropes.pdf):

| Item | Single | Half | Twin (tested as a pair) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Falling mass | 80 kg | 55 kg | 80 kg |
| Peak force, first drop | ≤ 12 kN | ≤ 8 kN | ≤ 12 kN |
| Minimum drops without breaking | 5 | 5 | 12 |
| Static elongation (80 kg) | ≤ 10% | ≤ 12% | ≤ 10% |
| Dynamic elongation, first drop | ≤ 40% | ≤ 40% | ≤ 40% |

Sheath slippage must also be no more than 20 mm. In the drop test the mass falls freely from 2.3 m above the anchor point, with 2.5 m of rope before the first drop, running over an edge of 5 mm radius that simulates a carabiner. Mammut says ropes are built and tested to withstand up to five falls of factor 1.7, but an older or worn rope may not.

**What it does and doesn't mean**: this is a new rope under severe conditions. Real falls are mostly milder, but your rope isn't new either.

</details>

## Length: 60, 70 or 80 m

[BD](https://blackdiamondequipment.com/blogs/stories/how-to-choose-a-climbing-rope):

- **60 m**: mountain routes with long approaches and frequent unroping, or multi-pitch with a tag line.
- **70 m**: BD calls this the standard for cragging and multi-pitch today; many rappels and pitches are set up for it.
- **80 m**: sport crags with long routes; and if you take a lot of falls, you can cut off worn ends and still have plenty of rope.

The most direct consequence of a short rope is the end slipping through the belay device while lowering. [Outdoor belaying](/posts/climbing/2026-10-17-outdoor-belay-en) cites American Alpine Club figures showing over half of lowering accidents in a decade had this cause; [first outdoor sport lead](/posts/climbing/2026-10-17-first-outdoor-sport-lead-en) covers checking route length before you go. Whatever the length, tie a stopper knot in the end.

## Diameter: durable or light

BD splits single ropes into three bands:

- **9.5 mm and up**: durable workhorses; BD says they hold up to seasons of use at the crag.
- **Around 9.4 mm**: all-rounders, between light and durable.
- **8.8 mm and under**: high-performance, lightweight ropes for redpoints or when weight counts.

By BD's split, thicker ropes buy durability and thinner ones save weight. Thin ropes have another cost, which comes up with belay devices: the GRIGRI manual, for one, warns that thin ropes are harder to grip and harder to control.

## "Dry" isn't always water repellent

Many ropes are labelled "dry", meaning water-repellent treatment. BD says dry treatment adds some durability and allows use in icy conditions. But "dry" on the label doesn't mean the rope passed a water-repellency standard.

[The UIAA standard for water-repellent ropes, published in 2014](https://theuiaa.org/home/new-uiaa-standard-developed-for-water-repellant-ropes), works like this: the whole sample is lightly abraded, equivalent to a few days' use, then soaked for 15 minutes. To be labelled "UIAA Water Repellent", it must absorb no more than 5% of its weight in water. For comparison, an untreated rope absorbs around 50% in this test, and ropes labelled "dry" with inadequate treatment absorb 20–40%. The UIAA's announcement quotes rope maker Beal: ropes with surface treatment only can't pass.

So if you need genuine water repellency (ice, alpine), look for "UIAA Water Repellent", not just "dry".

## Belay devices: two families, compatibility in the manual

Belay devices fall into two broad families:

- **Manual**: friction comes from the rope bending through the device; braking depends entirely on your brake hand.
- **Assisted braking**: pinches the rope by itself in a fall. As [gym top-rope belaying](/posts/climbing/2026-10-17-gym-top-rope-belay-en) explains, it assists rather than brakes automatically, and your brake hand stays on the rope.

The UIAA's [first braking-device standard (UIAA 129), published in 2009](https://www.theuiaa.org/safety-standard-for-braking-devices-published/), covers four kinds: manual, locking-assisted, abseil devices, and abseil devices with a panic function. The European standard is EN 15151. [Petzl's GRIGRI manual](https://www.petzl.com/sfc/servlet.shepherd/version/download/068Tx000009YCjEIAW), for example, says "EN 15151-1 type 6: devices for belaying and rappelling without a panic blocking element." Which type a given device is, its manual will tell you.

### Compatibility: rope diameter must fall within the device's range

Compatibility is item two in [the UIAA's 2026 buying advice](https://theuiaa.org/uiaa/uiaa-advice-ten-things-to-consider-when-buying-climbing-gear), with this example: a belay device designed for 9–10 mm rope may not function properly with a thicker 11 mm rope.

The compatible diameters are in the device's manual. The GRIGRI manual says:

- Compatible with 8.5 to 11 mm EN 892 single ropes.
- Works **optimally from 8.9 to 10.5 mm**.
- 10.5 to 11 mm: with thick or well-used ropes, giving slack takes more effort.
- 8.5 to 8.9 mm: thin ropes are harder to grip and harder to control when lowering, rappelling or holding a fall, and need more vigilance and experience.

The manual adds two warnings:

- **Diameters have a tolerance.** Commercial nominal diameters can vary by up to ±0.2 mm, so combinations near the limits need extra care.
- **Some ropes are slippery.** Sheath-treated ropes, new ropes, and icy, wet or muddy ropes can reduce the GRIGRI's blocking. Whenever you use an unfamiliar rope, get used to the device's feel with it again.

Back to the two ropes in the shop: find out what diameters your belay device suits first, then choose between 9.8 and 8.9.

**Something you can do tonight**: find your belay device's manual (paper or on the maker's site) and note its compatible and optimal diameter ranges. Then check that the rope you use most falls inside them.

## Carabiners: Type H for belaying, Type T for quickdraws

[The UIAA 121/EN 12275 pictorial](https://www.theuiaa.org/documents/safety-standards/Pictorial_UIAA121%20Connectors.pdf) classifies connectors into types. The relevant ones:

| Type | Use |
|---|---|
| H (HMS) | Belaying |
| T (directional) | Quickdraws |
| B (basic) | General use |
| K (Klettersteig) | Via ferrata; must have an automatic lock |
| X (oval) | Aid climbing |
| Q (quick link) | Screw-gate quick link |

So the carabiner that clips your belay device to your belay loop should be a **locking Type H**. Before buying, check the marking on the carabiner or its manual to confirm it's Type H.

The pictorial sets two more rules: gate opening at least 21 mm for Type K and 15 mm for all other types, and for all types the gate must stay closed under an opening force of 5 N or less.

### Quickdraws

A quickdraw is two carabiners joined by a sewn sling. [DAV's 2010 article](https://sac-hoherrohn.ch/wp-content/uploads/2019/02/dav_lebensdauer-von-alpinausruestung.pdf) noted that keylock gates without a nose had become standard on the bolt end and wiregates on the rope end, and that top-standard carabiners of the time had gate-open strengths above 9 kN.

How many to carry depends on how many bolts your route has. How to clip and orient them is in [gym lead climbing](/posts/climbing/2026-10-17-gym-lead-climbing-en); preparing for your first outdoor lead is in [first outdoor sport lead](/posts/climbing/2026-10-17-first-outdoor-sport-lead-en).

## Practice: four scenarios

Answers are under each question. Think first, then look.

**1. You find an 8.9 mm rope marked ½ and with the twin symbol, cheaper than single ropes, and want to lead on it at the crag.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

No. Those markings mean it's a half and twin rope, used as a pair, not as a single rope for leading. For a single rope, look for 1 on the end. Diameters overlap, so check the symbol, not just the millimetres.

</details>

**2. Your belay device's manual says "for 9–10 mm ropes", and a friend lends you an 11 mm rope.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

This is exactly the UIAA's example: a device designed for 9–10 mm may not work properly with an 11 mm rope. Choose a rope within the manual's range, or a device compatible with the rope.

</details>

**3. You've just bought a brand-new, sheath-treated 8.7 mm rope and plan to belay a friend on lead with a GRIGRI.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

8.7 mm is within the GRIGRI's compatible range but outside its optimal range (8.9–10.5 mm). The manual says thin ropes are harder to grip and control, and new or sheath-treated ropes can reduce blocking. Stacked together, this setup demands real experience and vigilance. If you're still building belay experience, make your first rope one in the optimal range.

</details>

**4. A rope in the shop says "dry", and the staff say it's "fully waterproof, fine for ice climbing."**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

"Dry" doesn't mean it passed the UIAA water-repellent standard. The UIAA says ropes labelled dry but inadequately treated absorb 20–40% water in the test. For genuine water repellency, look for "UIAA Water Repellent" (no more than 5% uptake).

</details>
