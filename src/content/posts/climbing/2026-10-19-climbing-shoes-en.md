You've tried on three pairs in the shop. The first feels comfortable, and the staff say "too loose." The second hurts too much to stand in, and they say "it'll loosen up." The third is somewhere between, and you can't tell whether it fits.

This is post 1 of the Climbing Gear series. [Post 0](/posts/climbing/2026-10-19-climbing-gear-buying-order-en) explained that climbing shoes aren't personal protective equipment, and the [German Alpine Club (DAV)](https://www.alpenverein.de/artikel/nutzungsdauer-karabiner-seile-helme-klettersteigsets_a1acb469-ec21-4718-a487-9facc2f821d7) (in German) notes there are no strength regulations for them. So there's no CE label to read; **fit is all you have**. This post covers choosing a shape, what to check when trying shoes on, how much materials stretch, and when to resole.

Two things are not here. How to stand on holds is in [climbing footwork](/posts/climbing/2026-10-18-climbing-footwork-en). What overly tight shoes do to your feet, and when to see a doctor, is in the injury series post [feet and climbing shoes](/posts/climbing/2026-10-18-climbing-feet-shoes-en).

## Three shapes: neutral, moderate, aggressive

[REI's guide to climbing shoes](https://www.rei.com/learn/expert-advice/rock-shoes.html) sorts shoes into three types:

| Shape | Looks like | Suits | Trade-off |
|---|---|---|---|
| Neutral | Flat sole, toes lie flat | Beginner to intermediate climbers, long multi-pitch days; the flat profile slots into cracks | Thicker, stiffer soles feel less |
| Moderate | Slightly downturned | Gym, sport, some bouldering and trad; handles slab, cracks and slightly overhanging routes | Less comfortable than neutral; stickier, thinner rubber wears faster |
| Aggressive | Strongly downturned, usually asymmetric, high heel tension | Hard overhanging routes and boulders | Least comfortable; worse in cracks and on smears |

REI says neutral shoes, being more comfortable, are a popular choice for novice to intermediate climbers. The goal of a first pair is to last a whole session and let you concentrate on footwork, not to send a grade harder the moment you put them on.

**Something you can do tonight**: think about what walls you climbed in the gym last month. If it was mostly vertical walls and low-angle slabs, start with neutral or moderate shoes; don't buy aggressive shoes for the occasional session on the overhang.

## Check three things when trying them on

[Climbing Ninjette's sizing guide](https://climbingninjette.com/2026/08/29/climbing-shoes-size/) (in Chinese) boils fit down to three checkpoints, which line up with REI's advice:

1. **Toes**: slightly bent and touching the front, but not folded up with your toenails pressed white against the shoe. REI says a shoe performs better with toes slightly bent at the knuckles, but toes should be flat or comfortably curved, with knuckles not painfully bunched against the upper.
2. **Heel**: no slipping or obvious gap when you stand, rise onto your toes or bend your knees. Ninjette says heel lift means the shoe's shape doesn't suit your foot, and switching models helps more than switching sizes. REI adds that on your toes, the back of the shoe shouldn't pinch the bottom of your Achilles tendon.
3. **Upper and sides**: press with your fingers; there should be no empty spots. REI says dead space between your toes and the shoe means it won't stay rigid on a foothold; Ninjette notes this is common for people with low insteps or narrow feet.

Two more details:

- **Try shoes in the afternoon or after exercise.** Ninjette points out that feet swell slightly by evening, closer to how they are when climbing.
- **Fully loosen lace-ups before putting them on.** REI suggests undoing the laces completely, then tightening from toe to ankle.

REI also says the only sure way is to try shoes on. If you can only buy online, Ninjette suggests shops that offer size exchanges.

**Something you can do tonight**: at your next gym session, put on your rental or current shoes and run the three checks. Whichever one fails is what to solve first when you buy.

## "How many sizes down?" has no formula

This is the question beginners ask most, and the answers vary most:

- Taiwanese outdoor outlet [OUTSiDERS](https://www.outsiders.com.tw/post/17455) (in Chinese) says to choose a size that feels a bit tight but not painful, usually half a size to one size below your street shoe.
- Ninjette says not to use a "two sizes down" formula. Lasts vary a lot between brands and models, so one person can wear different sizes in two brands; go by how the shoe fits.
- Studies measured bigger gaps. In the UK, [McHenry 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26261058/) measured 56 climbers with a foot ruler and found climbing shoes averaged almost 4 UK sizes smaller than street shoes; in Italy, [Buda 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23536501/) found an average of 2.3 sizes among 144 climbers.

Those study numbers describe how tight people actually wear shoes, not how tight they should. In McHenry's study, 55 of 56 climbers wore ill-fitting, excessively tight shoes, 91% had foot pain while climbing, and higher-ability climbers tended to choose tighter fits. These are cross-sectional studies and can't show that tight shoes cause injury; the details, and which foot problems show up, are in [feet and climbing shoes](/posts/climbing/2026-10-18-climbing-feet-shoes-en).

So this post gives no formula. Run the three checks and pick a pair you can climb in for a whole session.

## Materials: how much they stretch

The same tightness can feel very different a month later depending on the upper. From REI and Ninjette:

| Upper | How much it stretches | How to size on the day |
|---|---|---|
| Unlined leather | Up to about a full size (REI) | A bit tight at first is acceptable, but you must be able to stand in it |
| Lined leather | Half a size or less (REI) | How it feels now is close to how it'll feel long term |
| Synthetic | Barely; softens slightly with use (REI) | Must fit on the day (Ninjette) |

REI adds two notes: unlined leather can bleed colour onto your feet, and some makers line only the toe to limit stretch where it happens most.

Ninjette names the most common beginner mistake: hearing "climbing shoes stretch," buying a tight synthetic pair, and three months later it still hurts. Ask the staff what the upper is made of before you buy.

## Laces, straps or slippers

From [REI](https://www.rei.com/learn/expert-advice/rock-shoes.html):

- **Lace-up**: the most adjustable fit. Loosen when your feet are hot and swollen or for a walk-off; crank them down for a hard pitch.
- **Strap** (hook-and-loop): easy on and off, great for bouldering and the gym when you slip shoes off between climbs.
- **Slipper**: the most sensitive and lowest-profile; REI says they're good for training foot strength and slotting into very thin cracks.

OUTSiDERS adds an observation from Taiwan: straps are the most common style for indoor bouldering, while lace-ups are common outdoors and on roped routes, where you climb longer and don't take them off as often.

## Socks or not

From Ninjette:

- **Always wear socks in rental shoes**, for hygiene; many gyms require it.
- **In your own shoes** most people go barefoot, but a thin sports sock is fine if your heels rub or you dislike the feel, at a small cost in sensitivity.
- **Don't fill a shoe that's too big with thick socks.** Your foot slides inside and you're less stable. The problem is the size, not the sock.

## When to resole

Most climbers don't wear shoes out and buy new; they send them for a resole. [This Climbing.com resoling guide](https://climbing.com/gear/resole-rock-climbing-shoes) interviews Eric Pauwels of the US resoler Rock & Resole:

- **Look at the big-toe area.** He says 90% of sole wear happens there. The sole starts at about 4 mm there and wears down until it's paper-thin, which is when to resole. Many climbers judge by the whole sole and look in the wrong place.
- **A hole in the rand needs a toe cap.** The rand is the roughly 2 mm band of rubber around the upper. A hole means a toe cap, and a toe cap means a new half sole too, because rand rubber wraps under the outsole.
- **Checking a rand without visible holes**: start at the pinkie toe and pinch the rand with your thumb, working toward the big toe, feeling for soft spots, anywhere from pinhead to pencil-eraser size.
- **How many times?** A well-looked-after pair of performance shoes can typically be resoled three or four times.

For Taiwanese prices, take [Boulder Bug's repair price list](https://boulderbug.com/pages/climbing-shoe-repair-pricing-process) (in Chinese, checked 2026-10-19):

| Job | Price | When |
|---|---|---|
| Half sole | NT$1,500 per pair | Sole clearly thin, rand not yet worn through |
| Full sole | NT$2,000 per pair | Widespread sole wear or aged rubber; the workshop decides based on shoe construction and extent of wear |
| Toe reinforcement | NT$450 per spot | Rand thin or worn through, or upper starting to show; usually paired with a half or full sole |

Boulder Bug and Pauwels agree: **resole when the sole is thin but before you wear through to the rand**. It costs less and better preserves the shoe's original shape. On how many resoles a shoe can take they differ: Pauwels says three or four is typical, while Boulder Bug says there is no fixed number and it depends on the upper, midsole and rand.

How long until you need one? In [his shoe-care article on HANCHOR](https://tw.hanchor.com/pages/climb13) (in Chinese), coach and resoler Chu Wei shares his own experience: climbing two or three days a week, a pair lasts him about eight months to a year. Precise footwork and how often you climb dynamically both affect wear. He also retires shoes when they lose their shape, because a shoe that no longer holds the foot loses its support.

**Something you can do tonight**: turn your shoes over and look at the rubber under the big toe, then pinch the rand from pinkie to big toe. Thin sole and intact rand means it's time to resole.

## Everyday care

Chu Wei's routine is simple:

- Wipe the whole shoe with a damp cloth after climbing. He says chalk dries shoes out and the upper can harden.
- Keep them away from heat, and out of the car, where sun can make it very hot.
- After washing, air-dry them in the shade, not in the sun.

Ninjette adds: if you climb without socks, take your shoes out of your bag afterwards to air, rather than leaving them in a closed bag.

## Practice: four scenarios

Answers are under each question. Think first, then look.

**1. You try on a synthetic shoe that hurts too much to stand in. The staff say "it'll loosen up."**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

REI says synthetics barely stretch, and Ninjette says they must fit on the day. Three months from now it will very likely still hurt. Try a larger size, or a lined or unlined leather model.

</details>

**2. A shoe is right in the toes and length, but your heel slips out when you rise onto your toes.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Heel lift means the shape doesn't suit your foot; it isn't a size problem. Ninjette says switching models works better than switching sizes. Going a size down only makes your toes hurt more, and the heel still won't fit.

</details>

**3. After a year, most of the sole still looks thick, but the area under the big toe is almost see-through.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Pauwels says 90% of wear is in the big-toe area, so judge there, not by the whole sole. Send them for a half sole now; if you wear through the rand you'll need a toe cap as well, which costs more.

</details>

**4. A friend says he bought his shoes two sizes down and tells you to do the same.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

There's no formula. OUTSiDERS says half a size to one size, and Ninjette explicitly says not to use a "two sizes down" rule; the average gaps in studies describe how tight people actually wear shoes, and many of those people have foot pain. Use the three checks and pick a pair you can climb a whole session in.

</details>
