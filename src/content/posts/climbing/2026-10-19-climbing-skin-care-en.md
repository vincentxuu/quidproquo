Saturday at Long Dong, Taiwan's sea-cliff crag, third go on your project. You pull on the small edge before the crux and feel a sting in your right index finger: a callus has torn clean off, and there is pink new skin underneath. Your forearms still have power and you remember every move, but the day is over.

This is post 7 of the Climbing Mental Game and Tactics series, and the last one. [Post 5 on redpointing and projecting](/posts/climbing/2026-10-19-redpoint-projecting-tactics-en) planned your attempts, and [post 6 on conditions and friction](/posts/climbing/2026-10-19-climbing-conditions-friction-en) covered chalk, weather and send days. This post covers the other half of friction: your skin.

The short version:

- **There is almost no clinical research on climbers' hand skin.** We found no clinical studies of it, so most of the advice below is coaching consensus, and it is labelled that way.
- **Severe hand sweating is a medical problem. See a dermatologist first.** Medical treatment usually starts with aluminium chloride antiperspirant, often with tap water iontophoresis added for the palms. It does not start with the cream climbers pass around.
- **Skin sets how many attempts you get in a day.** Save skin while warming up, make fewer, better attempts, and stop when you see the warning signs.

## Know your skin: sweaty or dry

The first step is knowing which end you are on. Climbing magazine's [skin care guide (Clarke, 2024)](https://www.climbing.com/skills/training/a-climbers-guide-to-skin-care-dealing-with-sweat-tape-splits-and-more/) puts climbers' skin on a spectrum. At one end it is sweaty and soft; at the other it is dry, hard and prone to splitting. The goal is to move toward the middle. Sweaty skin needs drying; dry skin needs moisture. Advice meant for the other end makes your problem worse.

[Lattice Training's skin article (2023)](https://latticetraining.com/blog/skin-care-for-rock-climbers) also starts with knowing your skin type. (Lattice sells skin products, so keep that in mind.) A quick self-check (**coaching consensus**):

| What you usually notice | Which end | Main risk |
|---|---|---|
| Hands are wet a few moves after chalking, holds feel slick, finger pads go soft | Sweaty | Soft skin wears fast and fingertips thin out |
| Finger pads look shiny like glass, you skate off small holds, knuckles or tips crack | Dry | Split tips; thick calluses torn into flappers |

Dermatologist [Shun Omori, interviewed by the Japanese climbing outlet CLIMBERS](https://www.climbers-web.jp/feature/article-93/) (in Japanese), says the ideal fingertip has a stratum corneum with some thickness, neither too hard nor too soft. Skin that is too thin actually weeps more.

**Something to do tonight**: next time you climb, check your fingertips after your third go and after your last. Note whether they are "wet and soft" or "shiny, hard, cracked". After three sessions you will know which end you are on.

## Sweaty hands: go the medical route first

### How common it is

We found no study of hand-sweating prevalence in Taiwan. The nearest data: among 33,000 high school and college students in three cities in southeast China, about 4% had primary palmar hyperhidrosis ([Li et al., 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18167205/)). In a national survey of about 70,000 Chinese university students, it was more common in coastal areas than inland ([Lai et al., 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25381140/)). Taiwan is also hot, humid and coastal, so the rate may be similar, but that is **inference**, not a Taiwanese figure.

### First-line treatment

If sweating gets in the way of daily life (holding a pen, using your phone, holding hands), it is a treatable condition. See a dermatologist. Three medical reviews order the options in much the same way:

- Topical aluminium chloride is the starting treatment for most primary hyperhidrosis, and iontophoresis is an option for palms and soles. Botulinum toxin is first or second line. Sympathectomy is kept for severe cases where nothing else worked ([American Family Physician, McConaghy 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30215934/)).
- Antiperspirants are first line, and iontophoresis is the main treatment for palm and sole sweating. Sympathectomy is a last resort because it can cause compensatory sweating, where other parts of the body sweat more ([Journal of the American Academy of Dermatology, Nawrocki & Cha 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30710603/)).
- A 2026 review in the German medical journal Deutsches Ärzteblatt orders things a little differently: aluminium chloride and anticholinergic drugs first, then botulinum toxin, with tap water iontophoresis listed among further options. It also notes that high-quality studies are few ([Schreiner et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41572865/)).

In iontophoresis you put your hands in water carrying a weak electric current, for a set time per session, and you have to keep it up. A randomised trial of 32 people ([Hosseini et al., 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37436009/)) compared tap water iontophoresis with aluminium chloride gel iontophoresis. After 7 sessions, sweating dropped clearly in both groups, with no significant difference between them. But only the dominant hand was treated, the untreated hand improved just as much, and there was no untreated control group. So the trial cannot show that iontophoresis caused the drop (**research**, small sample). The authors note it is not suitable for people who are pregnant, have a pacemaker or have epilepsy.

What this means for climbers (**inference**): these treatments have research behind them and a doctor following up, and their side effects are better understood. Whether to use one, and which, is a conversation with your dermatologist. Don't piece it together yourself.

### Antihydral: the cream climbers ask about most

Antihydral is an antiperspirant cream made by the German company Robugen. According to its [official German product information (Pflichttext)](https://www.antihydral.de/pflichttext) (in German):

- Each 100 g contains 13 g of methenamine. Other ingredients include talc, zinc oxide, white clay, glycerol and eucalyptus oil.
- In Germany it is a licensed medicine (Zul.-Nr. 6421167.00.00), not a cosmetic.
- **Contraindications**: do not use it if you are hypersensitive to formaldehyde or any other ingredient; do not apply it to weeping or blistered skin; do not apply it to the breast while breastfeeding. The text also states that in Germany it is sold only in pharmacies ("nur in Apotheken").
- If you are not sure whether you are allergic to formaldehyde, a dermatologist can patch test you ([Goossens & Aerts, 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35229319/) describe patch testing with 2% aqueous formaldehyde).

This section lists only the contraindications from the product information. It gives no instructions for use. Whether to use it, and how, is for a dermatologist to decide.

The UK's [Sheffield Climbing Clinic (2019)](https://www.sheffieldclimbingclinic.com/post/skin-care-for-climbers) explains how it works: methenamine releases formaldehyde on slightly acidic skin, which temporarily shuts down the sweat glands. Formaldehyde and formaldehyde-releasing ingredients are still common contact allergens and still cause hand dermatitis ([Goossens & Aerts, 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35229319/)).

**How much evidence is there?** We found one controlled trial: India, 1995, 60 people, 4 weeks ([Phadke et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20953017/)). Patients with sweaty hands and feet were randomised into three groups. A 10% methenamine solution worked better than tap water iontophoresis, faster than glutaraldehyde, and with fewer side effects. The sample is small, the study is old, and it did not test a 13% cream like Antihydral.

Climbers split on it. Both sides are listed here without picking one:

| Position | Who says so | Their experience |
|---|---|---|
| Helps people who really sweat a lot | 5.15 climber Cameron Hörst ([Climbing, Clarke 2024](https://www.climbing.com/skills/training/a-climbers-guide-to-skin-care-dealing-with-sweat-tape-splits-and-more/)); Climbing writer [Buhay (2026)](https://www.climbing.com/gear/how-to-prevent-sweaty-hands-for-climbing/), who tested it firsthand | Hörst says fingertips that are too dry split on small holds; Buhay found that using it too often (more than once a week) left the hands rough and yellow |
| Not recommended for most people | Sheffield Climbing Clinic: little information on safe use, "difficult to recommend"; [ClimberNews](https://climbernews.com/climbing-skin-care-guide/): a good care routine is usually enough | — |
| Reactions vary a lot | [99Boulders (2021)](https://www.99boulders.com/skin-care-for-climbers) reports someone who applied it for 15 minutes and had splits that took two months to heal | — |

The side effect users mention again and again is **too much drying**: skin turns shiny and hard and cracks at the knuckles and creases. [Trailandcrag's skin guide](https://web.archive.org/web/20250821144522/https://trailandcrag.com/rock-climbing/climbers-guide-to-skincare) notes that people who get a strong drying product into the crease of a finger joint are especially likely to split there.

**Status in Taiwan**: we downloaded the Taiwan Food and Drug Administration's full drug licence dataset and searched all of it. For Antihydral and for any topical methenamine, **no licence record was found (searched 2026-10-19)**. Every methenamine entry in the dataset is an oral or injectable urinary antiseptic. This post gives no purchase channels. The German product page and some of the English articles cited here carry shopping links; those channels do not apply to Taiwan, and this post does not recommend them. If sweating bothers you, take your situation to a dermatologist and ask which options are legally available and suitable for you.

**Something to do tonight**: if sweating bothers you outside climbing too, book a dermatologist appointment and describe the concrete situation: "my hands are wet a few moves after chalking."

## Liquid chalk: less dust, but drying

Liquid chalk (Taiwanese climbers also call it 「白手套」, "white gloves", as in a [2009 PTT X-game thread](https://www.pttapp.cc/bbs/X-game/M.1235149385.A.1A4.html), in Mandarin) is chalk mixed with alcohol. You rub it on, the alcohol evaporates and leaves a layer of chalk. How chalk itself affects friction is covered in [post 6](/posts/climbing/2026-10-19-climbing-conditions-friction-en). Here we only look at what it does to skin (**coaching and manufacturer consensus**; we found no skin research):

- **Upside**: sweaty climbers can use a coat of liquid chalk as a base layer and add regular chalk on top ([Rock+Run's skin care buying guide](https://rockrun.com/blogs/the-flash-rock-run-blog/climbing-skincare-buying-guide)).
- **Downside one: drying.** Alcohol pulls moisture out of skin, and too much leaves it dry ([The Climbing Hangar](https://www.theclimbinghangar.com/blog/handcare-for-climbers-our-recipe-for-strong-healthy-skin-and-more-climbing)). Rock+Run suggests climbers with dry skin avoid chalk with added drying agents.
- **Downside two: allergy.** Some liquid chalks contain rosin. Rosin, alcohol and the latex in tape are all possible allergens ([KletterRetter](https://www.kletterretter.com/en/common-climbing-skin-problems-part-3-3/)). If your hands itch or break out in a red rash after using it, stop. If it doesn't clear up, see a dermatologist.

**Something to do tonight**: if your skin runs dry, next session use liquid chalk only before your send attempt, use regular chalk for warming up and working sections, and see whether you get fewer splits.

## Dry skin and splits

A split tip is a thin crack along the skin lines of a fingertip or knuckle. Climbing's [Schulte (2022)](https://www.climbing.com/skills/skin-care-for-rock-climbing/) puts it bluntly: a split can take a week to heal, and the bigger the split, the fewer goes you get.

A common coaching sequence (**coaching consensus**):

1. **Prevent**: Lattice suggests climbers with dry skin moisturise 3–4 nights a week and dampen their hands slightly with a wet cloth before trying. Trailandcrag warns against putting strong drying products in the creases of finger joints, where skin splits most easily.
2. **After it splits**: 99Boulders files down the hard, raised edges of the split until the skin around it is intact, then moisturises heavily.
3. **While climbing**: if a split hasn't healed, tape over it. Tape makes you grip harder and climb less efficiently, though (Schulte 2022).

Dr Omori's daily routine goes a different way: after climbing, wash your hands and uses a potassium glycerin solution to "dissolve" the frayed skin rather than file it, rinse, then apply lotion and cream. Do this on climbing days and twice a day on rest days too. Sweaty climbers can skip either the lotion or the cream step. The Taiwanese sports medicine blog [gazispace (2022)](https://gazispace.com/notes/hand-skin-care-for-rock-climbers.html) (in Mandarin) gives similar advice: wash after climbing, apply lotion before bed, avoid soaking in hot water.

Moisturiser needs time. The Climbing Hangar suggests applying it at least 2 hours before climbing so it soaks in, not when you walk into the gym.

**Something to do tonight**: put a tube of hand cream by your bed and rub it into your fingertips before you sleep. On your next climbing day, apply it at least 2 hours before you leave, and none once you are at the gym.

## Should you file calluses? Two views

Calluses protect you. The trouble is their edges. The UK site [UKC's hand and foot care article](https://www.ukclimbing.com/articles/skills/hand_and_foot_care-2089) says heavily callused skin is more prone to flappers, usually when you pull hard on a sharp hold, often in the cold. The [Wilderness Medical Society (WMS) skin article](https://wms.org/magazine/magazine/1198/Climbing-Skin-Care/default.aspx) explains why: when a callus doesn't taper, its edge forms a ridge that catches easily on a sharp rock edge.

| Position | Who says so | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| File calluses flat | UKC, Climbing (Clarke 2024), WMS, gazispace | Sand or file the callus edge until it is level with the surrounding skin, leaving no "step"; trailandcrag suggests 80–120 grit sandpaper |
| Don't overdo it | Dermatologist Omori, KletterRetter | Filing fingertips too much frays the skin and makes it tear more easily; Omori dissolves skin on the fingertips and files thick calluses elsewhere; KletterRetter says never file calluses off completely |

The two views overlap. Both agree callus edges shouldn't be left, and neither wants calluses filed off entirely. They differ on whether to file fingertips. No study has compared the two (**coach and doctor opinion**).

**Something to do tonight**: run your fingers over your palm and finger bases and find any thick callus with an edge you can feel. File only that edge, stop once it feels like a smooth slope, and never file down to pink.

## Flappers: cut or tape back down?

[KletterRetter's skin injury grading](https://www.kletterretter.com/en/common-climbing-skin-problems-part-1-3/) goes by skin layer. An abrasion only damages the outer stratum corneum. A flapper lifts the whole epidermis. Bleeding means the dermis is involved. The deeper it goes, the more you should treat it as a wound.

### On the spot

1. **Stop** and clean your hand. Outdoors, rinse with clean water or saline (see [crag first aid and emergency response](/posts/climbing/2026-10-18-crag-first-aid-en)). UKC warns that deep flappers can get infected, so disinfect and cover with gauze.
2. **Decide what to do with the flap**. Two views:

| Approach | Who says so | When it fits |
|---|---|---|
| Trim off the dead flap and let the wound breathe | 99Boulders (the author says taping it back "never worked"), The Climbing Hangar's first option, Schulte 2022 (trim it, then apply antibiotic ointment) | You're done for the day and want it to heal faster |
| Tape the flap back down and keep climbing | The Climbing Hangar's second option, Schulte 2022 (he gives both) | Last day of a trip, and you really want one more go |

3. **What about glue?** UKC is against superglue. WMS says that if you glue it, use medical-grade tissue adhesive, not superglue from the hardware store.

There is also an old crag trick: pack chalk into the wound to stop bleeding. [Raymond & Solberg (2025)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40208048/) tested this in vitro, and magnesium carbonate chalk did shorten one clotting time (aPTT). But it was a test-tube experiment with no data on infection or healing. All it shows is early support for the bleeding part. It is not a way to treat a wound.

### Aftercare

Keep the wound moist or let it dry? Again two views. Trailandcrag favours ointment and a covering to keep it moist. WMS suggests larger wounds get an extra dressing with antibiotic ointment. 99Boulders lets it breathe and dry out. [REI (2018)](https://www.rei.com/blog/climb/how-to-build-climbing-skin) alternates, switching between covered and open. No study has compared which heals climbers' flappers faster (**coaching consensus**).

**See a doctor if**: the wound gets redder, more swollen, hotter or more painful, leaks pus, has redness spreading up the finger or into the palm, or you have a fever. Those are signs of infection. The same goes for a deep wound, one that keeps bleeding, or one where you're not sure something is still inside. Also see a doctor if the wound got dirt in it and you don't know when your last tetanus shot was. The US CDC says dirty or major wounds carry a higher tetanus risk, and whether you need a booster depends on the wound type and your vaccination history ([CDC wound management to prevent tetanus](https://www.cdc.gov/tetanus/hcp/clinical-guidance/index.html)). If the pain is in a joint or tendon rather than the skin, that is a different problem: see [finger pulley injuries](/posts/climbing/2026-10-18-finger-pulley-injury-en) and [finger pain that isn't a pulley](/posts/climbing/2026-10-18-finger-injuries-other-en).

**Something to do tonight**: put a small pouch in your climbing bag with small scissors, a nail file, tape, a few gauze pads and a small pack of saline. Next time you flap a tip, you won't have to borrow at the crag.

## Planning a day's attempts when skin is the limit

Back to the opening question: how many goes do you get in a day? [Post 5](/posts/climbing/2026-10-19-redpoint-projecting-tactics-en) covered recovery for your forearms and head, but often your fingertips hit the limit first. Coaches agree on the basics (**coaching consensus**, untested by research):

- **Save skin while warming up.** Lattice suggests climbers with thin skin tape up for warm-ups and easy routes, and take the tape off only for the attempts that count.
- **Make fewer, better attempts.** One topic of Eric Hörst's [Training for Climbing podcast episode on chalk and skin](https://trainingforclimbing.libsyn.com/93-getting-a-grip-all-about-chalk-use-and-skin-performance) is saving skin by making a few quality attempts. (The guest is the CEO of Friction Labs. We read only the show notes and did not listen to the audio.)
- **Stop when you see the warning signs.** Schulte 2022 says fingertips worn pink and starting to weep only get better with rest. [Climb On's guide](https://climbonequipment.com/en-us/blogs/knowledge/skin-care-guide) points out that the fluid is plasma, not sweat. Another go at that point usually costs you more days off.
- **If you run sweaty, don't soften your skin before a send.** Cameron Hörst, who has sweaty hands, cuts down on showers and dishwashing on climbing trips (Clarke 2024). If you run dry, follow the moisturising advice above.

Put together, a sample day (**inference**; adjust to your skin):

| When | What | Skin consideration |
|---|---|---|
| 2+ hours before leaving | Dry skin: moisturise. Sweaty skin: skip it | Give the skin time to absorb it |
| Warm-up | Easy routes, taped if your tips are thin | Save skin for later |
| Goes 1–2 | Work sections, check beta | Look at your tips after each go |
| Go 3 | Send attempt: tape off, brush holds, light chalk | Skin and forearms are both at their best |
| After that | Tips pink or weeping → stop; still OK → one more go or switch to routes with bigger holds | Don't gamble the next few days on one go |

In Taiwan, hot and humid weather softens skin so it wears faster (see [post 6](/posts/climbing/2026-10-19-climbing-conditions-friction-en)). In a summer gym or on a damp day at Long Dong, expect fewer goes than on a dry day.

**Something to do tonight**: open the project plan you wrote in post 5 and add a "skin" column next to each session: how many goes, which go started to hurt, what colour your tips were at the end. After a few sessions you'll know roughly how many goes your skin lasts.

## Practice questions: four scenarios

**1. Every time you climb, your hands are wet three or four moves after chalking. Someone online says "just use Antihydral" and posts a link to a buying agent who will import it for you.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

See a dermatologist first. Medical treatment for sweaty hands usually starts with aluminium chloride antiperspirant, often with tap water iontophoresis added for the palms; these have research behind them and a doctor following up. Antihydral has one controlled trial, a 1995 study of 60 people, and must not be used by anyone allergic to formaldehyde or any of its ingredients. For it and for topical methenamine, no licence record was found in the TFDA's full drug licence dataset (searched 2026-10-19). Don't click the buying-agent link: a drug imported that way has no licence record in Taiwan, so nobody checks its quality or how it is used. Don't buy it and apply it yourself; have a doctor assess you first. For what to use and what you can get, ask a doctor and a pharmacist.

</details>

**2. It's winter. Your fingertips are shiny and hard, and yesterday your index fingertip split slightly. You're going to Long Dong on Saturday.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

You run dry. Moisturise before bed for the next few nights, file the raised hard edges of the split until the skin around it is intact, and stay off drying products and heavy liquid chalk. On Saturday, tape over the split for the warm-up and take it off only for the hard goes. If the split gets bigger, stop: Schulte says a split can take a week to heal.

</details>

**3. Bouldering, you tear a flapper at the base of your ring finger. Underneath it's pink and oozing a little blood. Your friend says, "Pack some chalk in it, tape it, keep going."**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Bleeding means the dermis is involved (KletterRetter's grading), so clean it and cover it with gauze first. Chalk for stopping bleeding has only test-tube support, with no testing on infection. Unless it's the last day of a trip, trimming the dead skin and letting it heal beats taping it back and forcing it. If over the next few days the wound gets redder, more swollen, hot, or leaks pus, see a doctor.

</details>

**4. You're working a project and only start to feel it on your fifth go, but by then your fingertips are worn pink.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Your skin is running out before your forearms. Tape up for the warm-up and the first few section-working goes, and move the send attempt up to go 3. With fewer goes, rest fully before each one, brush the holds and confirm your beta so every go is a quality one. Stop when your tips turn pink or weep, and save skin for the next session.

</details>
