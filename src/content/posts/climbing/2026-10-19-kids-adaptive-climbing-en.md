Weekend afternoons at the gym are busy: a row of kids in full-body harnesses on the wall, a row of parents filming on their phones. A rarer scene: a climber who can't see, moving up as someone on the ground calls out "two o'clock, a big hold".

This post is for anyone who wants to bring a child, or a disabled friend, to climb. The previous post, [Climbing Partners and Gym Culture](/posts/climbing/2026-10-19-climbing-partners-gym-culture-en), was about partners checking each other. Belaying someone much lighter than you, or someone who needs you to guide them, adds another layer of responsibility.

The first half is about kids: when they can start, how to choose a class, which harness, and what to watch in their fingers. The second half is about adaptive climbing: how it's done abroad, what para climbing is, and what Taiwan has now.

## How young can kids start?

We couldn't find any climbing organisation that sets a minimum starting age. In practice the lines come from each gym's rules and class design. At [T-UP Climbing](https://tupclimbing.tw/) (in Mandarin) in Taiwan, for example, the Zhonghe branch splits kids' classes in two:

| Class | Age | Content | Price per term (October 2026) |
|---|---|---|---|
| [Toddler climbing movement class](https://tupclimbing.tw/course/zhonghe/367) (in Mandarin) | 4–6 | Games for climbing, running, jumping and coordination, about 1 hour per session | NT$3,000 for 6 sessions |
| [Kids' top-rope class](https://tupclimbing.tw/course/zhonghe/370) (in Mandarin) | 7–12 | Footwork, grips, routes set to each child's level, about 1.5 hours per session | NT$4,000 for 6 sessions |

Both classes have at most four kids per group, provide shoes, harness and helmet, and offer a trial session before you sign up.

T-UP's [entry rules](https://tupclimbing.tw/terms-and-conditions/78) and [price list](https://tupclimbing.tw/pricing/83) (both in Mandarin) add some limits for minors:

- Under-18s need an accompanying adult, and a legal guardian must co-sign the entry statement.
- Under-12s must wear a helmet on top-rope, and the accompanying adult must watch them at all times.
- Belaying others: the entry rules say under-16s may not belay and 16–17-year-olds may belay under a guardian's supervision; the price list says under-18s may not belay without permission from on-site coaching staff. The two documents differ in strictness, so go by what staff tell you on the day.

Other gyms' age bands and rules may differ; ask before you book.

### Three things to ask when your 8-year-old first tries top-rope

1. **Who belays?** On a first top-rope session, a coach usually belays. If you want to belay yourself, you'll need that gym's belay card first.
2. **Which harness?** Ask whether your child will wear a full-body or a sit harness; the next section explains why it matters.
3. **How many kids per coach?** T-UP's kids' classes have at most four per group. The fewer kids, the more closely each one is watched on the wall.

**Something you can do tonight**: check your nearest gym's website for its kids' class age bands and its rules on minors entering and belaying.

## Harnesses: full-body or sit?

The European harness standard [EN 12277](https://avs.edelrid.com/images/attribut/EN_12277.pdf) defines four types:

- **Type A**: adult full-body harness.
- **Type B**: children's full-body harness, tested for users up to 40 kg.
- **Type C**: sit harness, the kind most adults wear at the gym.
- **Type D**: chest harness, only for use together with Type C.

The British Mountaineering Council's [harness guide](https://thebmc.co.uk/media/files/Gear/104%20BMC%20Harnesses%20-%20A%20Guide%20For%20Climbers%20And%20Mountaineers%20V5%20%28SINGLE%20PAGES%29.pdf) explains why young children wear full-body harnesses: their centre of gravity is higher than an adult's, and they often don't yet have a defined waist, so a sit harness waistbelt won't hold. The guide gives no age for switching to a sit harness; it says it depends on many factors, including the child's climbing ability.

The same guide warns that how well children's bodies tolerate lead falls hasn't been studied well. Whether lead falls on standard equipment cause immediate injury or later problems isn't known. So be cautious both about starting kids on lead and about moving them into sit harnesses.

**Something you can do tonight**: if you're buying your child a harness, check the label for the standard number and type (B or C), and compare it with your child's weight.

## Belaying someone much lighter than you

When adults belay kids, the usual issue is a large weight difference. Belaying itself, and the limits when the climber is heavier than the belayer, are covered in [Learning to Top-Rope Belay in the Gym](/posts/climbing/2026-10-17-gym-top-rope-belay-en). Two things to add for kids:

- **The other way round is more dangerous.** A child belaying an adult can be pulled right off the ground by a fall. T-UP and other gyms also restrict minors from belaying others.
- **Talk about it beforehand.** The [DAV's 2025 gym accident statistics](https://www.alpenverein.de/verband/bergsport/sicherheitsforschung/kletterhallen-unfallstatistik/kletterhallenunfallstatistik-2025) (in German) include a case where an 80 kg climber doing fall practice hit a 69 kg belayer, who broke a collarbone; the causes were poor communication and no equipment to offset the weight difference. The DAV's advice: before climbing, check your setup, talk about the weight difference, and for pairs with a big gap, add a friction device at the first quickdraw to offset it (the DAV calls it a Bremsassistent).

**Something you can do tonight**: next time you climb with someone much heavier or lighter, say the weight difference out loud before you start, and ask staff whether they have equipment to offset it.

## Teenage fingers: the warning sign

The injury parents of teenage climbers most need to know about isn't the pulley injury common in adults; it's the finger growth plate.

A [2025 prospective study by Schöffl and colleagues](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11848689/) followed under-18 climbers seen at a German climbing-injury referral clinic from 2017 to 2020. Stress injuries around the finger growth plates made up 45.3% of all injuries. Because the clinic specialises in climbing injuries, the authors acknowledge the proportion is inflated. In the literature these injuries cluster at ages 13 to 15, during the growth spurt. Studies count differently (some only fractures, some stress reactions too), so their numbers can't be compared directly.

What parents should remember is this situation: **pain and swelling on the back of a teenager's middle finger joint, with no obvious injury**. Stop climbing and see a doctor; don't decide it's "just tiredness".

What a growth plate is, which finger is most often affected, which specialist to see and how long until they can climb again are all in [Young Climbers and Finger Growth Plates](/posts/climbing/2026-10-18-youth-growth-plate-en). The training consensus is that teenagers shouldn't campus; the reasons are in [Strength, Shoulders and the Campus Board](/posts/climbing/2026-10-18-climbing-strength-power-en).

**Something you can do tonight**: if your child is 13 to 15 and climbs several times a week, save the warning-signs section of Young Climbers and Finger Growth Plates.

## Adaptive climbing: how it's done abroad

Adaptive climbing means adjusting equipment, routes, guidance or teaching so that disabled people can climb. A few common approaches abroad:

- **Training gym staff**: [Paradox Sports](https://www.paradoxsports.org/adaptive-climbing-initiative/) in the US runs a two-day course teaching gym staff, universities and guides how to run adaptive climbing programmes. The aim is for host gyms to go on to set up their own adaptive climbing clubs with regular sessions.
- **Community-run**: [Adaptive Climbing Group](https://adaptiveclimbinggroup.org) was founded by a climber who lost a leg to cancer; its first event, in Brooklyn, New York, in March 2012, drew 38 people. It now runs indoor, outdoor and youth programmes that are free, discounted or pay-what-you-can.
- **Coach CPD**: the UK's [Climbing for All](https://nicas.co.uk/climbing-for-all) is a one-day course for coaches and instructors that has run for over 20 years, half theory and half practice. It starts from the "social model" of disability: disability comes from environments that haven't been adapted, not from a personal defect.

### Para climbing: the competitive end

Competitive climbing for disabled athletes is called para climbing. [World Climbing](https://www.worldclimbing.com/news/get-to-know-para-climbing) (formerly the IFSC) puts athletes into three main categories with four groups of class codes (physical impairment is split into lower limb, AL, and upper limb, AU):

- **B1–B3**: visual impairment. B1 athletes wear a blindfold and a guide calls holds over radio.
- **AL1–AL2**: lower-limb impairment.
- **AU2–AU3**: upper-limb impairment.
- **RP1–RP3**: impaired muscle power, range of motion or neurological impairment.

Lower numbers mean greater impairment. Competitions use lead routes with a top rope, so athletes don't clip quickdraws. Para climbing debuts at the [LA28 Paralympics](https://www.paralympic.org/news/la28-paralympic-medal-event-programme-athlete-quotas) with eight gold medals. How classification is decided and how competitions are scored is left to the climbing competitions series.

## What Taiwan has now

Taiwan currently has no established adaptive climbing organisation or regular classes. Activity is mostly taster sessions for visually impaired climbers:

- In late November 2023, National Taiwan Sport University invited Japanese visually impaired climber Koichiro Kobayashi (founder of the NPO Monkey Magic) to Taiwan. On 1 December, together with a tandem-cycling association for visually impaired people, they ran a ["Climbing Vision" event](https://twnewshub.com/archives/60776) (in Mandarin) at a gym in Beitou. Volunteers first tried climbing blindfolded; the conclusion was that calling the next hold by clock direction, or by its position relative to a part of the body, worked best.
- The [Taiwan Foundation for the Blind](https://www.tfb.org.tw/contents/news_ct?id=71) and the [Taiwan Blind Rehabilitation Institute](https://www.ibt.org.tw/RWD01/OnePage.aspx?tid=120&id=945) (both in Mandarin) have also run indoor climbing and outdoor abseiling sessions.
- The Chinese Taipei Alpine Association's 2026 [national bouldering championships](https://ctaa.j91.me/race/502) (in Mandarin) have only men's and women's open categories, with no para category.

Change at the policy level is in the legislature. The [draft amendment to the Sports Act](https://www.ey.gov.tw/Page/9277F759E41CCD91/d0eb8192-299e-4522-a74d-23435408aed2) (in Mandarin) approved by the Executive Yuan on 3 September 2026 (renaming it the National Sports Act) adds a chapter on "adaptive sport", aiming to guarantee equal participation for disabled people and others with special needs. It's still under review; what it will require of gyms won't be known until the text passes.

If you want to bring a disabled friend to climb, for now you can:

1. Call the gym first, explain your friend's situation, and ask whether any coach has taught someone similar.
2. Start with top-rope, with a coach belaying.
3. For a visually impaired friend, try calling holds by clock direction: "Three o'clock, an arm's length away, a big hold."

**Something you can do tonight**: on a top-rope route you know well, close your eyes and have your partner guide you up by calling holds by clock direction. You'll learn how much detail the guide needs to give.

## Practice: four scenarios

The answer is under each one. Think first, then look.

**1. You want to take your 5-year-old daughter to the gym. A friend says, "Kids have to be at least 7 to climb."**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

We couldn't find any organisation that sets a minimum starting age. A common pattern at Taiwanese gyms is a play-based movement class for 4–6-year-olds and top-rope classes from age 7. Ask the gym directly whether it has a toddler class and what its rules for minors are. T-UP requires under-12s to wear a helmet on top-rope and under-18s to be accompanied by an adult.

</details>

**2. Your 10-year-old son weighs 32 kg. You've found an adult sit harness online that comes in a very small size and want to buy it for him.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Hold on. The BMC guide says young children should use a full-body harness, because their high centre of gravity and lack of a defined waist mean a sit harness waistbelt won't hold. Children's full-body harnesses (EN 12277 Type B) are tested up to 40 kg, so 32 kg is within range. There's no fixed age for switching to a sit harness; it depends on your child's build and ability, so ask a coach. If a coach thinks a sit harness is fine, there are small sit harnesses made for children, so you don't need to squeeze him into an adult model.

</details>

**3. Your 14-year-old climbs four times a week and has recently said the back of the middle joint of their right middle finger hurts and is a bit swollen, but they didn't bang or twist it.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Stop climbing and see a doctor. Pain on the back of a teenager's finger with no injury may be a stress injury to the growth plate; in a study at a climbing-injury referral clinic, these made up 45.3% of under-18 cases, clustering at ages 13 to 15. Don't put it down to tiredness; see Young Climbers and Finger Growth Plates for details.

</details>

**4. Your friend is visually impaired and wants to try climbing. You plan to belay and call holds from the ground yourself.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

For the first session, have a coach belay so you can focus on calling holds, or let the coach guide while you watch and learn. Call the gym first and ask whether any coach has worked with visually impaired climbers. Call holds with clock direction plus distance, such as "Two o'clock, arm's length, a big hold"; that was one of the conclusions of Taiwan's 2023 "Climbing Vision" event.

</details>
