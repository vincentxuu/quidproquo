> 🌏 [中文版](/posts/climbing/2026-10-19-why-japan-climbing-strong)

Watch climbing competitions for a while and you notice something: boulder and lead finals often have several Japanese names. At the 2026 Asian Games in Aichi–Nagoya, Japan won 2 golds, 3 silvers and 1 bronze and topped the climbing medal table. Taiwan's national-team coach Yang Dong-wei says Taiwan's training model "leans towards the Japanese system" ([OUTSiDERS](https://www.outsiders.com.tw/post/47417), in Mandarin). So why is Japan so strong?

This is post 6 of the Climbing Competitions and Pro Climbing series. You hear plenty of explanations: Asian climbers are light, Japan has lots of gyms, kids start young. This post splits them into two layers. First the facts you can check, then the explanations commentators give, each one attributed to whoever said it. By the end you should be able to tell which "why Japan is strong" claims rest on data and which are just opinion.

The conclusion first: there is no public academic study that directly analyses why Japan is strong at climbing. The explanations below come from coaches, federation officials and journalists. They are worth hearing, but they are not settled.

## The facts: how strong is Japan?

### Olympics and World Championships

- **Tokyo 2020**: in the women's combined, Miho Nonaka won silver and Akiyo Noguchi bronze ([olympics.com results](https://www.olympics.com/en/olympic-games/tokyo-2020/results/sport-climbing)).
- **Paris 2024**: in the men's boulder & lead, 17-year-old Sorato Anraku won silver with 145.4 points ([olympics.com report](https://www.olympics.com/en/news/paris-2024-sport-climbing-results-anraku-sorato-mens-boulder-lead)).
- **2025 World Championships, Seoul**: Anraku won men's boulder gold; in lead, Satone Yoshida took silver and Taisei Homma bronze ([Wikipedia medal table](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2025_IFSC_Climbing_World_Championships)).

Japan has not yet won an Olympic gold in climbing. Where Japan really stands out is not the Olympics but the season-long series.

### World Cup overall titles

Counting the overall winners listed in [Wikipedia's World Climbing Series article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Climbing_Series) (every year links to official results), Japanese men have won 14 overall titles and Japanese women 8:

- **Men**: lead — Yuji Hirayama (1998, 2000), Sachi Amma (2012, 2013), Sorato Anraku (2023); boulder — Tomoa Narasaki (2016, 2019), Yoshiyuki Ogata (2021, 2022), Sorato Anraku (2023, 2024, 2025); combined — Tomoa Narasaki (2017, 2019).
- **Women**: Akiyo Noguchi won boulder (2009, 2010, 2014, 2015) and combined (2008, 2009, 2014); Miho Nonaka won boulder in 2018.

In 2023, his first senior season, Anraku won both the lead and the boulder overall titles. [Olympedia](https://www.olympedia.org/results/2005350) says he was the first climber to win both in his debut senior season.

### Depth: how many Japanese climbers make a final

[Inside Climbing's analysis](https://inside-climbing.com/2025/01/29/why-japan-has-11-fewer-spots-at-2025-ifsc-world-cups) notes that the men's lead final at Briançon, France had seven Japanese climbers in 2023 and six in 2024. A final has only eight places.

Part of this comes down to the rules, which we return to below.

### National competitions and selection: the Japan Cup

Japan's national championships are run by JMSCA (Japan Mountaineering and Sport Climbing Association), with one Japan Cup each for boulder, lead and speed. According to [Wikipedia's Climbing Japan Cup article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climbing_Japan_Cup), the lead Japan Cup started in 1987 (2026 was the 39th edition; the 31st was in June 2017 and the 32nd in March 2019, with none in 2018, so editions run one behind years), the boulder Japan Cup in 2005 and speed in 2019.

The Japan Cup is also the national-team selection event. [JMSCA's 2026 selection criteria](https://www.jma-climbing.org/article/2026/01/08/selection-criteria-for-international-2026-competitions/) (in Japanese) set the priority: first, athletes ranked in the top 10 of the previous year's world ranking; then athletes already named for the Asian Games; then, for boulder and lead, the top finishers at the national selection event (the Japan Cup), up to 8 men and 8 women. Japanese climbing outlet [CLIMBERS](https://www.climbers-web.jp/news/20260129-1/) (in Japanese) describes the Japan Cup as "the domestic season opener, the Japanese championship, and the national-team selection event."

Compare Taiwan: [post 5](/posts/climbing/2026-10-19-climbing-competitions-asia-taiwan-en) explained that Taiwan's national championships (open division) also count towards national-team points, but the full points rules are not public. Japan publishes its selection priorities on the federation website.

### Youth development

In October 2024 JMSCA announced its "[Next-Generation Athlete Development Project](https://www.jma-sangaku.or.jp/sports/nextgenpro/)" (in Japanese). It partners with prefectural federations and commercial gyms to set up boulder development hubs and boulder/lead training hubs.

The project document also names three problems JMSCA sees for itself:

1. It needs a sustainable athlete development system, with gyms across the country working with the federation;
2. There are not enough climbing coaches;
3. Most Japanese gyms are bouldering gyms, and there are few facilities that could put Japan at the front of the world in lead and speed.

The background section adds that the current team is built mainly around climbers who have been active since 2016, and that winning medals at the Los Angeles Olympics will require developing the next generation. In other words, even the Japanese federation does not assume today's advantage will carry on by itself.

Young climbers also have their own national competitions. One is the JOC Junior Olympic Cup run by JMSCA; according to [the Fujisawa climbing association](https://www.fujisawa-climbing.jp/2018/08/21joc.html) (in Japanese), more than 200 youth climbers from across Japan take part each year.

### National training centre

In 2022 the Higashi-Kanamachi sports ground in Katsushika, Tokyo was designated as a sport-specific National Training Center (NTC) hub for climbing, the first such designation for the sport ([Tokyo Shimbun](https://www.tokyo-np.co.jp/article/181640), in Japanese).

Compare Taiwan: in [an interview with OUTSiDERS](https://www.outsiders.com.tw/post/47417) (in Mandarin), national-team coach Yang Dong-wei said Taiwan still has no venue built to international competition standards that can be used for dedicated training, and the national team mostly borrows commercial gyms.

### How many gyms

Japan has a lot of climbing gyms, but the sources count them differently:

| When | Number | Source | How it was counted |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2019 | More than 500, up from fewer than 100 in 2008 | [Number Web (Kenichi Moriyama)](https://number.bunshun.jp/articles/-/839683?page=2) (in Japanese) | Journalist's report |
| May 2020 | 666 | [Mickipedia](https://micki-pedia.com/number-of-climbing-gym-2020.html) (in Japanese) | One person counting on a map; the author says the figure is not guaranteed |
| 2021 | About 800 | [CINC Capital](https://cinc-capital.co.jp/column/industry/climbing-gym-ma) (in Japanese), citing JMSCA | Second-hand |

What is certain is that the number of Japanese gyms rose sharply within a decade. The exact figure depends on who did the counting.

## The commentary: how people explain it

Each point below is someone's view, not a research finding.

**1. Dense hold layouts and more movement variety.** Toru Kobinata, then IFSC vice-president, told [Number](https://number.bunshun.jp/articles/-/839683?page=2) that gyms abroad often have fewer holds spaced further apart, which is easy for beginners to read but limits movement variety. Many Japanese gyms cover whole walls in holds, so you can combine them into all kinds of moves, and he believes this has helped Japanese climbers a lot. The same article cautions that gym growth is a worldwide trend, so "lots of gyms" alone is a weak explanation for Japan's strength.

**2. Climb all the time, on problems from many different setters.** In the OUTSiDERS interview Yang Dong-wei said: "Japanese climbers keep it simple: they just climb and climb. Solving problems from lots of different setters builds every part of their ability and their movement library." He also said Japanese teenagers may finish school at three and go straight to the gym, while Taiwanese kids only have Wednesday afternoons free.

**3. Asians are light, so they suit climbing.** This is a common claim, and Yang also said "climbing really suits Asians." But the Number article offers a counter-example: about ten years before 2019, the top of the world was almost entirely European, and Japanese climbers only rose to prominence recently. If body type were the main reason, that time gap would be hard to explain.

**4. The quota system amplified depth.** [Inside Climbing](https://inside-climbing.com/2025/01/29/why-japan-has-11-fewer-spots-at-2025-ifsc-world-cups) explains that before 2025, World Cup quotas gave countries extra spots based on world ranking, and climbers in the world top 10 also got named spots of their own. Japan had many highly ranked climbers, so it could send many athletes, which is why finals often had six or seven Japanese climbers. When the rules changed in 2025, Japan lost more spots than any other country: 11. This explains why finals had so many Japanese climbers, not why Japanese climbers are so strong.

## Comparison: Korea, China and Indonesia

Japan is not the only strong climbing nation in Asia, and each country is strong in different places:

- **Indonesia focuses on speed.** Veddriq Leonardo won men's speed gold at the Paris Olympics in 4.75 seconds ([Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/sports/olympics/climbing-indonesias-leonardo-wins-gold-medal-mens-speed-event-2024-08-08/)). AFP reported that Indonesia lists climbing as a national priority sport alongside badminton and weightlifting ([via VnExpress](https://vnexpress.net/news/sports/rapid-indonesian-speed-climbers-push-boundaries-and-smash-records-4660920.html)).
- **China has the deepest speed squad.** The men's world-record holder Zhao Yicheng and 2025 world champion Long Jianguo are both Chinese; [post 3](/posts/climbing/2026-10-19-competition-speed-climbing-en) covers the records in detail.
- **Korea is strong in lead and boulder.** Lee Do-hyun won men's lead gold at the 2025 World Championships in Seoul, and Seo Chae-hyun won women's lead gold at the 2026 Asian Games ([World Climbing Seoul report](https://worldclimbing.com/events/ifsc-climbing-world-championships-seoul-2025/news/lee-and-garnbret-take-lead-world-titles), [2026 Asian Games medal table](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sport_climbing_at_the_2026_Asian_Games)).

Japan's strengths are boulder and lead; speed is comparatively weak. The federation itself says it lacks lead and speed facilities, which matches the spread of results.

## What you can take from Japan

If you are a climber or parent in Taiwan, the parts of Japan's example backed by data are roughly these:

- **Competition and selection are tied together, and the criteria are public.** The Japan Cup is the selection event, and the selection order is published on the federation website.
- **The federation works with commercial gyms to develop young climbers.** JMSCA's project explicitly sets out to partner with gyms nationwide.
- **Volume and variety of problems.** Two commentators mention this, but no study has shown it is the main cause.

As for "Asian body types suit climbing," the data does not really support it.

## Practice: three scenarios

The answer sits under each question. Think it through first.

**1. A friend says: "Japanese climbers are light, so they're good at climbing." How do you reply?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

It's a common claim, but Number's 2019 report points out that about ten years earlier the top of the world was almost all European, and Japanese climbers only stood out recently. If body type were the main reason, that time gap is hard to explain. The better-documented factors are systemic: the Japan Cup doubles as selection, and the federation works with gyms on youth development.

</details>

**2. You read that "in one 2023 lead final, seven of the eight finalists were Japanese." Can you say "Japan's lead climbers are seven times as good as the rest of the world combined"?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

No. The number of finalists depends on quotas. Before 2025, quotas gave extra spots based on world ranking, and Japan had many highly ranked climbers, so it sent many athletes. After the 2025 change Japan lost 11 spots. The number of Japanese finalists reflects both strength and the rules.

</details>

**3. One article says "Japan has 800 gyms," another says "666." Which is right?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The two numbers come from different years and methods: 666 is a blogger's 2020 count from a map, with the author saying it isn't guaranteed; 800 is a consulting firm citing JMSCA's 2021 figures. When you quote either, say which year and who counted.

</details>
