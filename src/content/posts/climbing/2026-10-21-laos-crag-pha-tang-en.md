Pha Tang is north of Vang Vieng, in the village of the same name, where the main road crosses the Nam Song River. The wall is actually called **Na Pha Daeng**; because there is a similarly named Pha Daeng sector nearby, The Crag Journal's 2023 author used the village name to avoid confusion.

It is the best crag in Vang Vieng: 48 routes from 5b to 8b on pale limestone streaked with black, with big tufas high on the wall. The cost is distance, quiet, and an approach through paddies and a stream. **theCrag shows only 23 logged ascents for the whole crag** — you are close to climbing walls nobody has touched.

## What kind of crag this is

The Crag Journal's 2023 description is specific: very light white limestone with strongly contrasting black streaks, and plenty of inspiring tufa higher up. The atmosphere was tranquil, the climbing exceeded expectations, and "some of the routes are total classics".

theCrag's area description adds that there is "a good number of long routes on excellent limestone, mostly sport climbing but also some multi pitch routes", and that because few people come here, some routes need cleaning from dust and cobwebs.

Compared with the rest of Vang Vieng:

- **Lots of routes:** 48, the largest single crag in the area.
- **Good rock:** pale limestone with clear holds and good friction, and the black streaks make routes easy to read.
- **Tufas:** the tufas high on the wall are the signature feature here and something Pha Daeng does not have.
- **A bit dusty:** some routes are dusty, but the authors say nothing was overly dirty.
- **Quiet:** 23 logged ascents for the whole crag; the 2023 author also notes directly that Pha Tang does not get many climbers.

## When to go

Vang Vieng's dry season runs from October to April, with December the coolest. No public sun data exists for Pha Tang and theCrag does not mark the aspect; based on the 2023 description the setting is pleasant, but individual walls still need checking on site.

- **Nov–Feb:** the best window, 25–30°C by day, comfortable for a full day on the rock.
- **Mar–Apr:** above 35°C at midday. The pale limestone heats up, and holds that get sun are hot to touch. Go early or late.
- **May–Sep:** monsoon. The approach turns muddy, the Nam Song rises, and the ground by the paddies is worse. Wet rock is slippery, and tufas more so.

## Getting there

### Public transport

There is no public transport to Pha Tang. The practical option is to rent a motorbike:

1. Head north on Route 13 for about **20 km** to the village of Pha Tang (theCrag's description says "about 30km north of Vang Vieng"; both figures are kept here — Mountain Project's approach note is 20 km to the village, then 2 km further).
2. Continue about **2 km** past the village until an obvious wall appears on your left.
3. Park, walk in, climb. The whole ride is roughly 30 minutes.

The approach is part of the attraction: you cross a small stream, then walk between rice paddies and farmland with the wall in view the whole way. **Do not trample any crops** — that is a line the Crag Journal author specifically insisted on.

A tuk-tuk or a transfer arranged through your accommodation is also possible, but the service is unscheduled, so plan the return yourself.

### Driving

More than 40 km of tarmac and dirt road each way, slippery in the wet season, shared with traffic on a road with no shoulder. If you have no international licence or are unsure on local roads, hire a tuk-tuk or arrange a transfer.

### Parking

There is space to leave a motorbike below the wall. theCrag also notes that **about 4 km south along the river there is a small guest house offering food and accommodation**, and little else nearby — so if you want to stay at Pha Tang, the accommodation is 4 km away.

## Rules before you go

**Nobody manages it.** Pha Tang is not inspected daily by GCH or any Vang Vieng organisation. Bolts, hangers and anchors can be old or loose. Inspect them yourself and carry a wrench.

**Dust and cobwebs.** Both theCrag and the Crag Journal note that some routes need cleaning. Watch for rockfall while leading, and wear a helmet at the belay.

**Weather turns fast.** The paddies and river valley are open terrain, and afternoon storms arrive sooner than they do in the hills. When cloud gathers, stop; do not wait for rain.

**Resupply.** There is no shop at the crag, only the guest house 4 km south. Buy water, food and sun protection in Vang Vieng and bring it with you.

**Local etiquette.** The paddies belong to people, and the villages along the way are homes. Ask before you stop to take photos.

## Routes by level

YDS conversions follow the [Mountain Project international grade comparison chart](https://www.mountainproject.com/international-climbing-grades) and are approximate. The original grade is the one that counts.

One limit on the public data needs stating first: **theCrag's Pha Tang page does not publish the route names one by one** — the index of all 48 lives on the Na Pha Daeng sub-area page, and the only publicly named route is **Papillon**, shown among the nearby icons. Names for the rest come from the *Laos Rock Climbing Guidebook*, and route names are also painted at the base of the crag.

### Beginner: up to 6a (YDS 5.10a and below)

There are 8 routes at 6a and below (one 5b, five 5c, two 6a), and **the public pages do not name them**. On site, read the hand-painted names at the foot of the wall or use the guidebook; at 28 metres the easier lines are short.

### Intermediate: 6a+ to 6c+ (YDS 5.10b–5.11c)

Thirteen routes (one 6a+, three 6b, three 6b+, five 6c, two 6c+) — the biggest and most rewarding band. The one publicly verifiable name is Papillon.

| Route | Grade | YDS | Length | Sector | Note |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Papillon | 6c | 5.11a/b | — | Na Pha Daeng | theCrag icon; Crag Journal 2023: "much better than it looks" |

### Advanced: 7a to 7c+ (YDS 5.11d–5.13a)

Nineteen routes (four 7a, four 7a+, two 7b, three 7b+, four 7c, two 7c+), and **the public pages do not name them**. This is the thickest part of the crag.

### Expert: 8a and up (YDS 5.13b and up)

Three routes (one 8a, one 8a+, one 8b), and **the public pages do not name them**. With only 23 logged ascents on the whole crag, the 8s are effectively local projects; asking a local is faster than working through a topo.
