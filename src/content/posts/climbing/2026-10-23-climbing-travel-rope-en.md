In Taiwan, many climbers buy a 60 m rope first, and it covers both Long Dong and the gym. Abroad, that changes. The official Kalymnos climbing site treats 70 m as the minimum, and places with many long routes are more comfortable with 80 m. Over two weeks, the same rope gets lowered several times a day on limestone that is polished smooth in places and sharp in others. On a budget airline, the rope may be the heaviest thing in your bag.

The [previous article](/posts/climbing/2026-10-19-climbing-trip-gear-list-by-destination-en) worked out how long a rope each destination needs and when you need a second one. This one answers the next question: if you are buying a new rope for the trip, or deciding whether the one you own is good enough to take, which numbers matter? It does not repeat the 1, ½, and twin symbols on the rope end, the EN 892 tests, or the two families of belay devices. Those are in [Choosing a Rope](/posts/climbing/2026-10-24-climbing-rope-en) and [Choosing Your First Belay Device](/posts/climbing/2026-10-24-climbing-belay-device-en) in the Climbing Gear series. By the end you should be able to pick two or three candidates from the table below, or decide that the rope you already have will do.

## First, decide whether to bring a rope

You do not always need to fly a rope out from home. You can rent ropes and quickdraws at Tonsai in Thailand and on Kalymnos, and shops in Siurana, Spain, sell 80 m ropes. In Japan and Korea you will mostly need to bring your own. Details are in [What to Bring on a Climbing Trip Abroad](/posts/climbing/2026-10-19-climbing-trip-gear-list-by-destination-en).

The rest of this article is for when you bring one. With a rented rope you cannot see its history, so at least run the whole length through your hands to feel the sheath and core before you use it. How to check is in [Inspecting, Caring For and Retiring Climbing Gear](/posts/climbing/2026-10-19-climbing-gear-inspection-retirement-en).

## Six things to check

### 1. Weight per meter: convert it to a total

Every manufacturer lists weight per meter (g/m). Multiply by length and you have the weight of the rope itself, without a rope bag.

Of the 32 ropes in the table below, the lightest at 80 m is the [Beal Opera 8.5](https://www.beal-planet.com/en/products/opera-8-5mm-unicore-dry-cover), 48 g/m or about 3.84 kg. The heaviest is the [BlueWater Lightning Pro 9.7](https://www.bluewaterropes.com/product/9-7mm-lightning-pro-dynamic-single-rope/), 64.4 g/m or about 5.15 kg. The difference is about 1.3 kg.

Whether that 1.3 kg hurts depends on how you fly:

- **With a 23 kg checked bag**: in the [packing examples](/posts/climbing/2026-10-19-climbing-travel-packing-examples-en), one person with a 70 m rope and a week of clothes comes to about 13–14 kg, with room to spare. Rope weight is not the bottleneck here. Durability matters more.
- **Cabin bags only**: budget airlines often allow "7 kg across two pieces." An 80 m rope alone takes more than half of that, and every kilogram you save is room for a pair of shoes or a set of draws. How the rules work is in [Flying with Climbing Gear](/posts/climbing/2026-10-19-flying-with-climbing-gear-en).

Something you can do tonight: put your current rope on a luggage scale and divide by its length. That gives you its g/m, which you can compare directly with the table.

### 2. Length: check that 80 m exists

Not every model comes in 80 m. The Petzl [MAMBO 10.1](https://www.petzl.com/us/en/sport/products/single-ropes/mambo-10.1-mm/MAMBO-10-1-mm_SPO_a2m1r000002bySvAAI.html) and the Sterling [VR9 9.8](https://sterlingrope.com/vr9-9-8-mm/) stop at 70 m, and so does the BD 9.9. Durable, inexpensive thick ropes often skip 80 m, and that is where choosing a travel rope most often gets stuck.

Ropes also get shorter. Petzl says new ropes leave the factory slightly longer than stated, but they shrink 2–3% in the first few uses and up to 10% over their life ([Petzl Tech Tip](https://www.petzl.com/us/en/sport/tech-tips/basic-techniques/change-in-the-length-of-my-rope-after-use/CORDES-evolution-de-la-longeur-de-ma-corde_SPO_a77w0000000PCKLAA4.html)). If the routes at your destination are right at 35 m, a two-year-old 70 m rope that has had its ends cut may already be too short.

Petzl recommends taking a rope about 5 m longer than you think you need, and always tying a knot in the end when single-pitch climbing ([Petzl](https://www.petzl.com/us/en/sport/tech-tips/basic-techniques/rope-too-short--warning---danger-/CORDES-Corde-trop-courte---attention-danger--_SPO_a77w0000000PCKGAA4.html)).

### 3. Durability: diameter and sheath proportion

On a two-week sport climbing trip, one rope gets worn against the same kind of rock again and again. Edelrid's manual puts it plainly: frequent top-roping or rappelling on rough, sharp rock with a lot of dust, sand, or chalk can mean replacing a rope within weeks ([Edelrid dynamic rope manual](https://avs.edelrid.com/images/attribut/54517_GAL_Dynamic_Ropes_ANSICHT_DINA4_Homepage.pdf)).

Two numbers drive durability:

- **Diameter**: thicker ropes have more material. Petzl's MAMBO page gives the large diameter as the reason it lasts. Climbing.com's 2026 buyer's guide also says ropes thicker than 9.4 mm are generally more durable ([Climbing.com, 2026](https://www.climbing.com/gear/best-climbing-ropes/)).
- **Sheath proportion**: the sheath's share of the rope's weight. Edelrid names raising the sheath proportion as one of two ways to make a rope more abrasion-resistant; the other is lowering sheath friction ([Edelrid](https://edelrid.com/us-en/knowledge/knowledge-base/rope-basics-braiding-principles)). The table runs from 29% on the Sterling Nano to 42% on the Mammut Crag We Care Classic.

Sheath proportion is not the whole story. Climbing.com notes that yarn count, weave, and treatment also affect wear, and that too much sheath makes a rope stiff. So use it to compare ropes of the same diameter. At 8.9 mm, the Edelrid [Swift 48 Eco Dry](https://edelrid.com/eu-en/sport/ropes/swift-48-eco-dry-8-9mm) has a 41% sheath and the Sterling Nano 29%.

Reviews have plenty of examples of thin ropes wearing fast. OutdoorGearLab picks the Beal Joker 9.1 as its multi-pitch choice, but says it is not the rope for day-in, day-out cragging ([OutdoorGearLab, 2024](https://www.outdoorgearlab.com/reviews/climbing/climbing-rope/beal-joker)). In UKClimbing's review of the Edelrid Siskin 8.6, the reviewer's partner switched to a thicker rope for especially rough crags ([UKClimbing, 2025](https://www.ukclimbing.com/gear/climbing/ropes/edelrid_siskin_eco_dry_86mm_-_the_lightest_single_rope_on_the_market-16343)).

Another route is aramid. The Edelrid Swift Protect Pro Dry and the Mammut 9.5 Alpine Core Protect Dry add aramid in the sheath or between sheath and core, which the makers say improves cut resistance over sharp edges. These ropes cost more, and on the day prices were checked, Mammut's US store had every length of the Alpine Core Protect out of stock.

### 4. Middle marks and bicolor ropes

Halfway through lowering off a long route, your belayer needs to know how much rope is left. On a multi-pitch rappel with a doubled rope, the middle has to sit at the anchor. Both depend on finding the middle.

There are two ways to mark it:

- **Middle mark**: the manufacturer dyes the middle of the rope. Most ropes in the table have one. The drawback is that it fades. The 2026 reviews from OutdoorGearLab, GearJunkie, and Climbing.com all mention middle marks on some ropes fading or wearing off.
- **Bicolor rope**: the rope changes color or weave pattern at the middle. Sterling calls this the best way to identify the middle ([Sterling](https://sterlingrope.com/sterling-solid/bicolor-and-bipattern-bumps)). It costs more: the bicolor Sterling Velocity at 80 m is almost USD 190 more than the solid color.

Both drift as the rope shrinks. When the two halves shrink by different amounts, the middle mark is no longer in the middle, and cutting off one end does the same ([Petzl](https://www.petzl.com/us/en/sport/tech-tips/basic-techniques/change-in-the-length-of-my-rope-after-use/CORDES-evolution-de-la-longeur-de-ma-corde_SPO_a77w0000000PCKLAA4.html)). However clear the mark, still tie a knot in the end.

When a mark fades and you redo it, be careful what you use. A 2002 UIAA notice says marking a rope with felt-tip-type liquids can cut its energy absorption by up to 50%. That includes pens sold specifically for marking ropes, so use only products the rope maker approves ([UIAA](https://theuiaa.org/documents/safety/Notification_about_the_marking_of_ropes_by_climbers.pdf)). Mammut specifies the Edding 3000 ([Mammut](https://www.mammut.com/be/en/support/product-support/care-instructions/ropes)), and Beal sells its own [Rope Marker](https://www.beal-planet.com/en/products/rope-marker).

Something you can do tonight: line up both ends of your rope, run the two strands through your hands together to find the true middle, and see how far it is from the factory mark.

### 5. Thin ropes and belay devices

Thin ropes are lighter but harder to hold, and the belay device has to support them. The [GRIGRI](https://media.petzl.com/sfc/servlet.shepherd/version/download/068Tx000009YCjEIAW), for example, takes single ropes from 8.5 to 11 mm and works best on 8.9–10.5 mm. Between 8.5 and 8.9 mm, Petzl asks for extra vigilance and a high level of belay experience. Edelrid's [OHM II](https://avs.edelrid.com/images/attribut/54738_GAL_OHM_II_ANSICHT.pdf) only accepts 8.9 mm and up.

The catch on a trip is that the belay device is not only yours. In the UKClimbing Siskin review, the reviewer's partner in Spain had an Edelrid Eddy that was not approved for 8.6 mm. Before you leave, dig out your manual and your partner's, write down the compatible diameters, and only then shop for the rope. How to read compatibility is in [Choosing Your First Belay Device](/posts/climbing/2026-10-24-climbing-belay-device-en).

New and treated ropes are slicker. The reviewer of the Edelrid Tommy Caldwell DT 9.6 found it very slick when new, settling down after about a month ([Climbing Gear Reviews, 2023](https://climbinggearreviews.com/2023/09/07/edelrid-tommy-caldwell-eco-dry-dt-9-6-review/)). If you buy a rope a month before the trip, use it a few times at the gym or a local crag before you fly.

### 6. Triple rating: what it solves

A triple-rated rope is certified as a single, half, and twin rope. It answers "can one rope be used in different ways," not "can one rope stand in for two."

On its own, a triple-rated rope is a single rope, and a doubled rappel is still only half its length. If a rappel is longer than that, you still need a second rope. When that happens is covered in the exception section of [What to Bring on a Climbing Trip Abroad](/posts/climbing/2026-10-19-climbing-trip-gear-list-by-destination-en).

What it does buy you is a lighter second rope: bring one half rope and use the two together as a pair of half ropes, following both ropes' manuals. A UKClimbing reviewer also found a triple rating handy on trips where you switch between single and double rope techniques without wanting to carry many ropes ([UKClimbing, 2020](https://www.ukclimbing.com/gear/climbing/ropes/edelrid_swift_protect_pro_dry_triple-rated_rope-13054)). If you only climb single-pitch sport routes, there is no reason to pay extra for it.

Dry treatment is not on the list. The destinations in this series are all rock crags, so there is no need to buy a dry rope just for the trip; the reasoning is in [What to Bring on a Climbing Trip Abroad](/posts/climbing/2026-10-19-climbing-trip-gear-list-by-destination-en). Several reviews do find that dry treatment also keeps out dirt and slows sheath wear, and the table notes it.

## Choosing for three kinds of trips

### Two weeks of limestone sport climbing, with checked bags

Think Kalymnos, Thailand, or Spain. Rope weight is not the bottleneck. Durability is.

- Pick a 9.5–9.8 mm single rope with a sheath proportion near 40%.
- Length depends on the destination: 80 m for Kalymnos and Chulilla, at least 70 m elsewhere.
- If the budget allows, go bicolor so your belayer can track the rope on long lowers.

Ropes in the table that fit: Petzl ARIAL 9.5, Mammut 9.5 Crag Dry, Beal Booster III 9.7, Petzl CONTACT 9.8, Edelrid Heron Eco Dry 9.8. On a budget, the Mammut 9.5 Crag We Care Classic works, but it has no dry treatment, so keep it on a rope tarp and off the dirt at the crag.

### Cabin bags only, or a half rope added for multi-pitch

Think short budget-airline trips, or the big multi-pitch routes at Tonsai. Every kilogram counts.

- Pick an 8.9–9.2 mm triple-rated rope, favoring a sheath near 40%: Petzl VOLTA 9.2, Edelrid Swift 48 Eco Dry 8.9, Sterling Aero 9.2.
- The 8.5–8.6 mm Opera and Siskin are the lightest, but they sit near the GRIGRI's lower limit, and reviews say they are not for daily cragging. Skip them if your partner is not used to thin ropes.
- Accept faster wear: when an end fuzzes, cut it, then weigh the rope again and find the middle again.

### Especially sharp rock

Think sharp limestone ridges, or granite with a lot of crystals.

- The aramid ropes, the Edelrid Swift Protect Pro Dry 8.9 and Mammut 9.5 Alpine Core Protect Dry, are designed by their makers for cut resistance.
- On the day prices were checked, both were out of stock or only available in some lengths, so give yourself time to find one before the trip.

## How to choose: four steps

1. **Find the longest route**: look up the longest sport route at your destination on [theCrag](https://www.thecrag.com/) or in the local guidebook, double it, add 5 m, and choose 70 or 80 m.
2. **Check the baggage rules**: see whether your flight gives you 23 kg checked or only 7 kg in the cabin, and set the total weight you can accept.
3. **Write down device diameters**: for both your belay device and your partner's, and keep only the range where they overlap.
4. **Filter the table**: drop the models without your length or outside your devices' range, pick two or three by diameter and sheath proportion for your trip, then compare price and how the middle is marked.

Once the rope arrives, use it a few times at home before the trip so it is less slick, and check where the middle mark actually sits. How to dry the rope and keep it away from salt spray and heat on the road is in the last section of [What to Bring on a Climbing Trip Abroad](/posts/climbing/2026-10-19-climbing-trip-gear-list-by-destination-en).

The next article, [Flying with Climbing Gear](/posts/climbing/2026-10-19-flying-with-climbing-gear-en), covers getting this rope and the rest of your gear through security, and how much can go in checked and cabin bags.
