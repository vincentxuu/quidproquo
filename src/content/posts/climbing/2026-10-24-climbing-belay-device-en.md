The shop has two ropes, 9.8 mm and 8.9 mm, at about the same price. Thinner is lighter, you think, so the thin one. The staff ask: "What belay device will you use with it?" You don't know.

A belay device clips to your harness belay loop, the rope runs through it, and when the climber falls, it and your brake hand stop them. Buy it right after your [harness](/posts/climbing/2026-10-24-climbing-harness-en), because you need it as soon as you belay a friend in the gym, and it decides how thick or thin a rope you can buy later. This post answers one question: which one to buy. **It doesn't teach belaying**; that's in [gym top-rope belaying](/posts/climbing/2026-10-17-gym-top-rope-belay-en), [gym lead climbing](/posts/climbing/2026-10-17-gym-lead-climbing-en) and [outdoor belaying](/posts/climbing/2026-10-17-outdoor-belay-en). By the end you should know which family you need and be able to pick one or two devices from the tables below.

## Two families: manual and assisted braking

Belay devices fall into two broad families:

- **Manual**: friction comes from the rope bending through the device; braking depends entirely on your brake hand. Most are a small tube, hence "tube device".
- **Assisted braking**: pinches the rope by itself in a fall. As [gym top-rope belaying](/posts/climbing/2026-10-17-gym-top-rope-belay-en) explains, it assists rather than brakes automatically, and your brake hand stays on the rope.

Assisted braking splits again, by how the device works:

- **Cam devices**: like the GRIGRI, a cam clamps the rope. The GRIGRI manual lists it as EN 15151-1.
- **Assisted tubes**: like the ATC Pilot or Edelrid Jul², tube-shaped with no moving parts; in a fall the rope is pinched between device and carabiner, and whether it locks depends on how the device and your brake hand are positioned. The [Jul²](https://www.edelrid.com/us-en/sport/belay-devices/jul-2) and Giga Jul pages both list EN 15151-2; that standard is titled "manual braking devices", and ordinary tubes fall under it too.

The UIAA's [first braking-device standard (UIAA 129), published in 2009](https://www.theuiaa.org/safety-standard-for-braking-devices-published/), covers four kinds: manual, locking-assisted, abseil devices, and abseil devices with a panic function. The European standard is EN 15151. [Petzl's GRIGRI manual](https://www.petzl.com/sfc/servlet.shepherd/version/download/068Tx000009YCjEIAW), for example, says "EN 15151-1 type 6: devices for belaying and rappelling without a panic blocking element." Which type a given device is, its manual will tell you.

What "depends on position" means is clear in this two-minute video from Edelrid's own Knowledge Base channel. From 0:36 is the correct base position: rope threaded the right way, carabiner locked, the brake-hand thumb through the thumb rest and the fingers holding the brake strand, the device held high with the thumb rest facing down. Loaded like that it locks, and in the demo fall not a centimetre of rope runs through. From 1:25 is the wrong position: thumb rest facing up, which holds the device open, and in the fall the rope runs through with almost no braking force. That's what "assisted, not automatic" means. This is the maker's own channel, it covers assisted tubes with a thumb rest, and it doesn't name a model; for your own device's base position, read its manual.

**Whichever family you buy, the brake hand stays on the rope.** You'll see this line again. An assisted-braking device gives you an extra chance when you're distracted or your grip slips; it doesn't let you let go, and the manufacturers' manuals all say so.

**Something you can do tonight**: next time you're at the gym, look at the rental desk's belay devices and tell which are tubes, assisted tubes and cam devices.

## Four criteria

### 1. Start with where you'll belay

Which family your first device should be depends on where you'll climb over the next year.

The German Alpine Club (DAV)'s [indoor climbing basics booklet, English edition of July 2024](https://alpenverein.de/files/2409-indoor-klettern-basics-english_ol.pdf), keeps it short: it recommends assisted-braking devices, which it calls semi-automatic belay devices.

Price-comparison site WeighMyRack's [buying video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tHcQHLmBXyY) points the same way: if you'll mostly climb in the gym and single-pitch sport, buy an assisted tube first. The reasons are that it costs about half as much as a cam device and the technique is easy to learn. UK mountain instructor JB Mountain Skills [answers differently](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FMj1fCvnCOs): UK beginners should start with a tube that has guide mode, because UK trad mostly uses half ropes and the device needs to take two strands. They don't conflict; the difference is what you'll climb:

- Gym and sport only: buy assisted braking first.
- Definitely heading for multi-pitch or trad: you'll also need a two-strand tube with guide mode. Buy it first, or buy both.

**Something you can do tonight**: write down where you'll climb most in the next year. Only the gym, or crags as well? Will you be climbing multi-pitch with someone?

### 2. Compatibility: rope diameter must fall within the device's range

Diameter is how thick the rope is; single ropes sit around 9–10 mm. Thinner is lighter, thicker is more durable, and how to choose is in [post 8: Rope](/posts/climbing/2026-10-24-climbing-rope-en). Here you only need one thing: a belay device is designed for a range of diameters.

Compatibility is item two in [the UIAA's 2026 buying advice](https://theuiaa.org/uiaa/uiaa-advice-ten-things-to-consider-when-buying-climbing-gear), with this example: a belay device designed for 9–10 mm rope may not function properly with a thicker 11 mm rope.

The compatible diameters are in the device's manual. The GRIGRI manual says:

- Compatible with 8.5 to 11 mm EN 892 single ropes.
- Works **optimally from 8.9 to 10.5 mm**.
- 10.5 to 11 mm: with thick or well-used ropes, giving slack takes more effort.
- 8.5 to 8.9 mm: thin ropes are harder to grip and harder to control when lowering, rappelling or holding a fall, and need more vigilance and experience.

The manual adds two warnings:

- **Diameters have a tolerance.** Commercial nominal diameters can vary by up to ±0.2 mm, so combinations near the limits need extra care.
- **Some ropes are slippery.** Sheath-treated ropes, new ropes, and icy, wet or muddy ropes can reduce the GRIGRI's blocking. Whenever you use an unfamiliar rope, get used to the device's feel with it again.

Ranges differ by device, and the tables below list them. The limit people hit most is the top end: the ATC Pilot, Smart 2.0 and Pinch stop at 10.5 mm, and the Giga Jul at 10 mm. Gym house ropes are often worn, fuzzy and thick, so ask the diameter before bringing your own device.

Back to the two ropes in the shop: find out what diameters your belay device suits first, then choose between 9.8 and 8.9.

**Something you can do tonight**: find your belay device's manual (paper or on the maker's site) and note its compatible and optimal diameter ranges. Then check that the rope you use most falls inside them.

### 3. Feeding slack: which technique did you learn?

Belaying a leader means paying out slack constantly. Different devices use different feeding techniques, and mixing them up is how accidents happen:

- **Tubes and assisted tubes**: fed much like a standard tube. [Black Diamond's ATC Pilot page](https://blackdiamondequipment.com/products/atc-pilot-belay-device) also says it threads similarly to other tubular devices.
- **GRIGRI**: feeding slack fast means holding the cam down with a finger. In his [2025 analysis of a GRIGRI accident](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WBGkKqLhM8Y), Hard Is Easy points out that Petzl requires at least three fingers on the rope at all times when you press the cam to feed; in that accident the belayer pressed the cam to feed and the rope slipped out of his hand.
- **NEOX, Pinch, Vergo**: designed so you can feed slack without holding the cam. Japanese mountain physician Take-channel's [comparison video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=D2YzrH2jQdA) points out that the NEOX manual has dropped the cam-holding feeding method.

Any of these can be learned. What matters is learning from the manual for the device you own, and not carrying the GRIGRI technique over to another device, or the other way round.

**Something you can do tonight**: ask your instructor or gym which feeding technique they teach and on which device.

### 4. Carabiners and accessories: follow the maker

The device clips to your harness belay loop with a locking carabiner, usually a Type H (HMS). [The DAV's indoor climbing basics booklet](https://alpenverein.de/files/2409-indoor-klettern-basics-english_ol.pdf) also says to match the carabiner to the device maker's recommendation. That matters even more for assisted tubes, which lock by pinching the rope against the carabiner:

- **Mammut Smart 2.0**: Mammut recommends the Smart HMS carabiner.
- **Climbing Technology Click Up+**: needs CT's specified carabiner.
- **Edelrid Pinch**: can clip straight to the belay loop without a carabiner. Hard Is Easy warns that on a very thin belay loop it's possible to clip it wrong, so always partner-check before climbing.

How to choose carabiners is in the next post, [Carabiners](/posts/climbing/2026-10-24-climbing-carabiners-en).

**Something you can do tonight**: open the maker's page for the device you're considering, find the "compatible carabiner" line, and note the model.

## Choosing by situation

### Gym only

- Buy assisted braking first. On a budget, an assisted tube: ATC Pilot or Smart 2.0, or the Jul², rated to 11 mm, if your gym's ropes are thick.
- If you top-rope belay a lot in the gym, or belay friends much heavier than you, look at the GRIGRI+.
- Whatever you buy, the brake hand never leaves the rope. Learning to belay: [gym top-rope belaying](/posts/climbing/2026-10-17-gym-top-rope-belay-en).

### Gym plus outdoor sport lead

- The GRIGRI is the reference cam device; if you'd rather not learn to hold the cam, look at the NEOX, Pinch or Vergo.
- On a budget, an assisted tube handles lead belaying too; just check the rope diameter is in range.
- Practise lead feeding to your device's manual: see [gym lead climbing](/posts/climbing/2026-10-17-gym-lead-climbing-en) and [outdoor belaying](/posts/climbing/2026-10-17-outdoor-belay-en).

### Heading for multi-pitch or trad

- You need a two-strand tube with guide mode: ATC-Guide or Reverso, or the Pivot if you often lower a second from above.
- For one device covering assisted sport belaying and multi-pitch, look at the Giga Jul, but it's expensive, has a switch, and tops out at 10 mm.
- How a multi-pitch system fits together: [multi-pitch basics](/posts/climbing/2026-10-17-multi-pitch-basics-en); whether to back up a rappel with a prusik: [lower or rappel](/posts/climbing/2026-10-17-lower-or-rappel-en).

## Inspection and retirement

Taking the [GRIGRI manual](https://www.petzl.com/sfc/servlet.shepherd/version/download/068Tx000009YCjEIAW) as the example, inspection comes down to three things:

- **Check where the rope rubs**: before each use, check the body for deformation, cracks, marks, wear or corrosion, check the condition of the cam and friction plate, and make sure no sand or other debris is in the mechanism.
- **Check the direction every time you load the rope**: threaded backwards, neither the blocking function nor the descent control works. Markings on the device help you get it right, and the manual asks for a function test before each use. In [a 2024 video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=O1RG2HLUHSo), Mike Vert shows from 0:45 what a fall does to a GRIGRI threaded backwards: it doesn't pinch the rope at all. Partner-check before climbing and tug the climber's strand to confirm it locks.
- **Check the markings**: the body shows the compatible rope diameter, year of manufacture and standards. The manual lists an illegible marking, or not knowing a device's full history, as a reason to retire it; don't belay with a device of unknown origin that has no marking.

For the full inspection and retirement rules, see the last post in this series, [inspecting and retiring climbing gear](/posts/climbing/2026-10-19-climbing-gear-inspection-retirement-en).

## Practice: four scenarios

Answers are under each question. Think first, then look.

**1. Your belay device's manual says "for 9–10 mm ropes", and a friend lends you an 11 mm rope.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

This is exactly the UIAA's example: a device designed for 9–10 mm may not work properly with an 11 mm rope. Choose a rope within the manual's range, or a device compatible with the rope.

</details>

**2. You've just bought a brand-new, sheath-treated 8.7 mm rope and plan to belay a friend on lead with a GRIGRI.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

8.7 mm is within the GRIGRI's compatible range but outside its optimal range (8.9–10.5 mm). The manual says thin ropes are harder to grip and control, and new or sheath-treated ropes can reduce blocking. Stacked together, this setup demands real experience and vigilance. If you're still building belay experience, make your first rope one in the optimal range.

</details>

**3. A friend says his ATC Pilot "locks by itself", so he often belays with his phone in one hand and the other resting loosely on the rope.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Whether an assisted tube locks depends on how the device and brake hand are positioned. In the Edelrid demo above, an assisted tube held at the wrong angle let the falling rope run through with almost no braking. Assisted means assisted, not automatic: the brake hand stays on the rope, in the braking position. Ask him to put the phone away.

</details>

**4. You only climb in the gym, but in six months you want to try multi-pitch with friends. The shop recommends a GRIGRI.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The GRIGRI is excellent in the gym and for sport, but it takes a single strand and can't belay a second from the anchor. WeighMyRack and JB Mountain Skills both say multi-pitch calls for a two-strand tube with guide mode. You can buy an assisted-braking device like the GRIGRI now and add an ATC-Guide or Reverso later; practise both.

</details>
