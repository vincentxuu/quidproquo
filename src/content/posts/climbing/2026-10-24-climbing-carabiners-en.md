Your belay device doesn't clip straight to your harness; a locking carabiner sits in between. In the whole belay system, that carabiner has no backup: if it opens or breaks, the belay device comes off you. So you buy it together with the belay device, and it's the last place to cut corners.

This post answers one question: which one to buy. For how to belay, see [Learning to top-rope belay in the gym](/posts/climbing/2026-10-17-gym-top-rope-belay-en) and [Learning to lead in the gym](/posts/climbing/2026-10-17-gym-lead-climbing-en); for choosing the belay device itself, see [Belay devices](/posts/climbing/2026-10-24-climbing-belay-device-en); quickdraw carabiners are a different matter, covered in [Quickdraws](/posts/climbing/2026-10-24-climbing-quickdraws-en). By the end you should be able to read the markings on a carabiner, know which kind your belay device needs, and pick one from the table below.

## Four criteria

### 1. Type: a locking Type H for belaying

[The UIAA 121/EN 12275 pictorial](https://www.theuiaa.org/documents/safety-standards/Pictorial_UIAA121%20Connectors.pdf) classifies connectors into types. The relevant ones:

| Type | Use |
|---|---|
| H (HMS) | Belaying |
| T (directional) | Quickdraws |
| B (basic) | General use |
| K (Klettersteig) | Via ferrata; must have an automatic lock |
| X (oval) | Aid climbing |
| Q (quick link) | Screw-gate quick link |

So the carabiner that clips your belay device to your belay loop should be a **locking Type H**. Before buying, check the marking on the carabiner or its manual to confirm it's Type H.

The pictorial sets two more rules: gate opening at least 21 mm for Type K and 15 mm for all other types, and for all types the gate must stay closed under an opening force of 5 N or less.

Type H has a wide top. At 12:02 in its [carabiner explainer](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=81V4w5OJdFs&t=722s), Japanese mountain school Tozan Dojo says the shape was made for the Munter hitch in the first place; the instructor also says to belay on an HMS, because a D shape concentrates the load at one point and can pinch the rope. If you drop your belay device and need to belay or rappel on a Munter, you need a Type H to do it.

Something you can do tonight: dig out the locker you use now and find the small print on the spine. Tozan Dojo says HMS carabiners carry a circled H; if you see an H, it's the belay type; if it only says B or T, replace it.

### 2. Shape and cross-section: pear or D, and is the rope-bearing part round?

Type H carabiners are mostly pear-shaped: wide at one end, narrow at the other. The wide end holds the rope and the belay device and leaves room. D shapes are smaller and lighter, but the top is narrow and doesn't suit a Munter hitch.

The cross-section matters too. To save weight, many carabiners use an H- or I-beam section with edges; where the rope runs over round stock, it runs more smoothly and wears less. [Petzl's Attache page](https://www.petzl.com/us/en/sport/products/locking-carabiners/attache/ATTACHE_SPO_a2m68000000XZaUAAW.html) says only the rope-bearing basket is round stock and the rest keeps an H section to save weight, and it names the benefit as smoother rope running with tube devices such as the VERSO and REVERSO and with a Munter hitch.

Shape also depends on your belay device. When Petzl launched the new Attache, the presenter of its video on the WeighMyRack channel (who speaks for Petzl as "we") said you can use a pear-shaped carabiner on a GRIGRI but he wouldn't recommend it: the basket is so big it can get hung up and cross-loaded. If you do, add the BAR; otherwise he'd still recommend the Sm'D or another D shape ([from 1:33](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NURlP2V3EL0&t=93s)).

Something you can do tonight: clip your belay device to your carabiner, thread the rope, and look at whether the part the rope runs over is round or edged.

### 3. Locking: screwgate or auto-lock

- **Screwgate**: you turn a sleeve to lock it. Cheap, few parts, still works with some sand in it. The downside: you have to remember to lock it. Many show a red band when unlocked that disappears once locked.
- **Auto-lock**: locks itself when you let go, so you can't forget. Twist-lock (twist and open) is faster; triple-action (push, twist, open) is harder to open by accident, and harder to open, full stop. Mike Vert uses a twist-lock auto-locker on his GRIGRI: when he hands the GRIGRI to a beginner and they clip it in, it locks itself and he doesn't have to squint to check ([from 3:30](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=iXRU2mSU7Vs&t=210s)).
- **Slider**: Edelrid's design; push a slider with your thumb and the gate opens. [OutdoorGearLab](https://www.outdoorgearlab.com/topics/climbing/best-locking-carabiner) tested the Edelrid Pure Slider, which uses the same slider lock, and found it "very quick and easy to unlock and lock".

The most common complaint about screwgates is overtightening until they won't open. OutdoorGearLab says the Attache's screw gate gets stuck relatively easily. JB Mountain Skills says he's read online about Attache barrels jamming but hasn't had it happen; his habit is to screw it all the way and finger-tighten it, no extra force ([from 11:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0XMFM9FdI88&t=660s)).

Something you can do tonight: screw your screwgate all the way, then snug it gently with your fingers. Remember that feel and stop there every time.

### 4. Anti-cross-loading: a bonus, not a must

If the carabiner rotates on the belay loop, it can end up cross-loaded. As the table below shows, minor-axis strength is roughly a third of major-axis strength. So several brands make designs that stop the rotation:

- **Horn**: the DMM Rhino has a bump on the spine that stops devices like the GRIGRI sliding to the spine side.
- **Inner gate**: the BD GridLock uses a small wiregate to keep the belay loop in the other basket.
- **Bar or captive gate**: the Mammut Smart HMS, DMM Belay Master 2 and Petzl Attache with BAR hold the carabiner in the right orientation with a bar or hinged gate.

But first look at how big the real risk is. The standard requires a Type H carabiner to hold at least 7 kN cross-loaded. Mike Vert's view is that sport climbing usually doesn't generate much force, so a rotated belay carabiner is mostly a nuisance: it loads in a weird way, but he calls it "not a safety consideration" ([from 6:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=iXRU2mSU7Vs&t=360s)).

The catch with anti-cross-loading designs is compatibility. Each one only pairs well with some belay devices:

- [OutdoorGearLab](https://www.outdoorgearlab.com/topics/climbing/best-locking-carabiner) says the Rhino keeps a GRIGRI in place but not a tube-style device.
- [Treeline Review](https://www.treelinereview.com/gearreviews/best-climbing-carabiners) says the Mammut Smart HMS works well with tube-style devices but "doesn't play nice" with a GRIGRI.
- [WeighMyRack's belay carabiner guide (2024)](https://blog.weighmyrack.com/choosing-the-best-belay-carabiner/) says that with a GRIGRI it's best to use the GridLock upside down, so the GRIGRI sits in the belay keeper area; in the normal configuration the GRIGRI can still end up cross-loaded.
- Mike Vert doesn't like designs that trap the device in a small basket: you take the device on and off twice for every climb, which adds up over a day.

Something you can do tonight: clip in your belay device, thread the rope, and have your partner slack and tension the rope a few times while you watch whether the carabiner rotates into cross-loading. If it does, then consider an anti-cross-loading model; before buying, read the manuals for both the device and the carabiner.

## Choosing by situation

### Gym only, with a GRIGRI

- Pick something that stops the GRIGRI rotating: the DMM Rhino, or an Attache with BAR.
- With a GridLock, follow WeighMyRack's advice and clip it upside down so the GRIGRI sits in the inner-gate basket.

### Tube device, heading outdoors or to multi-pitch later

- One Attache is the most versatile: the round stock makes tube devices, Munter hitches and guide mode run smoothly.
- If you want anti-rotation, pick the Mammut Smart HMS 2.0 (Treeline says it works well with tube devices), or an Attache with BAR.
- On a tight budget, the RockLock Screwgate; the cost is weight.

### Lots of gym top-roping and rappelling

- The rope wears carabiners fast, so pick one with a steel insert like the Edelrid HMS Bulletproof Triple FG.
- If you worry about forgetting to lock, choose auto-lock, or an Attache with the BAR: the BAR only goes on when the gate is locked.

## Inspection and retirement

- **Before every use**: the gate closes fully on its own and locks all the way; the screw sleeve turns smoothly with no sand in it.
- **A chipped nose or gate, or any sharp edge**: retire it.
- **A groove worn where the rope runs**: it can cut the rope; the detailed criteria are in the post linked below.

For the full inspection and retirement rules, see the last post in this series, [How to inspect and retire climbing gear](/posts/climbing/2026-10-19-climbing-gear-inspection-retirement-en).

## Practice: four scenarios

Answers are under each question. Think first, then look.

**1. You belay with a GRIGRI in the gym, and your Attache keeps rotating into cross-loading with the GRIGRI sliding to the spine side.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Fix it and don't panic: the standard requires a Type H carabiner to hold at least 7 kN on the minor axis, and Mike Vert says sport climbing usually doesn't generate much force, so rotation is mostly awkward. The long-term fix is to add a BAR to the Attache, or switch to a DMM Rhino with its horn. In its new-Attache introduction, Petzl also says it doesn't recommend a pear-shaped carabiner on a GRIGRI unless you add the BAR.

</details>

**2. A friend lends you a BD GridLock, and you see they've clipped the GRIGRI to the big basket and the belay loop to the small one.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

That's the GridLock's normal orientation. WeighMyRack's belay carabiner guide says that clipped this way, the GRIGRI can still end up cross-loaded; with a GRIGRI it's best to clip the carabiner upside down so the GRIGRI sits in the inner-gate basket. Fix it before you start belaying.

</details>

**3. After lowering your partner, your screwgate won't open.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Screwgates jam when they're overtightened, and OutdoorGearLab says the Attache's screw gate gets stuck relatively easily. From now on, follow JB Mountain Skills' habit: screw it all the way, finger-tighten and stop.

</details>

**4. A shop assistant recommends a 43 g light D-shaped locker as your belay carabiner because it's light. You use a tube device and want to learn the Munter hitch.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

First check the body for an H marking. The Munter hitch needs a wide top to flip through, which is exactly what Type H (HMS) was designed for; a small D locker has a narrow top, and OutdoorGearLab says it's less versatile than an HMS. A light D locker is fine as a second carabiner for anchors, but your main belay carabiner should be Type H.

</details>
