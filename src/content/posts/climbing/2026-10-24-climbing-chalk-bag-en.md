A chalk bag is a small pouch of climbing chalk tied around your waist: when your hands get slippery mid-route, you reach in and chalk up. It's usually bought alongside your first shoes: renting one at a gym costs NT$80 a visit ([T-UP Wanhua price list](https://tupclimbing.tw/pricing/84), checked 2026-10-19), so if you go regularly, owning one pays off. The bigger, wide-mouthed version that sits on the floor is a chalk bucket, popular with boulderers.

This post answers one question: which one to buy. For how much chalk to use and why "thin layers, little and often", see [Climbing conditions and friction](/posts/climbing/2026-10-19-climbing-conditions-friction-en); for what liquid chalk does to your skin, see [Skin care for climbers](/posts/climbing/2026-10-19-climbing-skin-care-en). By the end you should be able to decide between a bag and a bucket, and pick one or two from the tables below.

## Two routes: waist bag or bucket

**Waist chalk bag**: tied around your waist, it goes up the wall with you. On top-rope or lead, it's the only way to chalk up halfway up a route.

**Chalk bucket**: sits on the ground, with a wide opening you can get both hands into. REI says it suits group bouldering sessions that need a lot of chalk ([REI Expert Advice](https://www.rei.com/learn/expert-advice/chalk-chalk-bags.html)). The pockets are usually bigger than a bag's, with room for brushes, tape and a phone; the catch is that most have no belt, so they can't come up the wall.

You can boulder with a chalk bag too; it just takes one hand at a time. [August Climbing](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=YjF-yQENq4w) bought a bucket precisely because, carrying a chalk bag around the gym, he couldn't fit both hands in it; with a bucket both hands go in at once, and he hoped it would make less mess when chalking up. [Caleb J. Ross](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=exLiITQRIdo) puts it more bluntly: for most people, bag versus bucket is personal preference.

So: if you top-rope or lead, buy a bag first; it works for bouldering too. If you only boulder and often spend whole days at the gym, then consider a bucket.

Something you can do tonight: think about what you've climbed most in the last three months. If any of it was top-rope or lead, start with the waist bag table below.

## Five criteria

### 1. Opening size: your hand has to fit

If your hand catches on the way in, you'll be fiddling for an angle every time you chalk up.

REI roughly splits bags into three sizes: small ones for people with small hands or who watch every gram, standard ones that most people use, and large ones for routes where you coat your whole hands and maybe forearms, like wide cracks. Manufacturers size them too:

- **BD Mojo** comes in S/M and M/L ([Black Diamond](https://www.blackdiamondequipment.com/products/mojo-chalk-bag)).
- **Organic** comes in four sizes from Mini to XL; the XL is 5.5 in (about 14 cm) across ([Organic Climbing](https://organicclimbing.com/products/large-chalk-bag)).

One model gets called out in reviews as unfriendly to big hands: the Organic Large ([Treeline Review, 2026](https://www.treelinereview.com/gearreviews/best-climbing-chalk-bags)). If you have big hands, go up a size in the same model.

Something you can do tonight: spread your hand and measure across your knuckles from thumb base to little-finger base. When you try bags in a shop, slide your fingers in together and check that your hand reaches the bottom and comes out without scraping the rim.

### 2. Shape and stiff rim

- **Cylindrical**: the most common; holds a lot and stands up better on the ground. REI says it suits longer climbs.
- **Tapered**: wide at the top, narrow at the bottom. REI says it suits shorter sport routes, since it's less likely to hang up on precise moves.

Beyond shape, look for a **stiff rim**: a band of stiff material sewn into the mouth so the opening stays open. Soft-rimmed bags collapse with use, and you end up hunting for the opening. Treeline says Static's canvas bag tends to collapse in use; [Climbing.com](https://www.climbing.com/gear/best-climbing-chalk-bags/) says the Mammut Alpine's stiff mouth keeps it open for quick dips. The BD Mojo's wire rim can be bent into whatever shape you like.

Something you can do tonight: if you already have a bag, set it on a table with the closure open and see whether the mouth stays open by itself. If it collapses, make a stiff rim a requirement next time.

### 3. Closure: no spills in transit

Most chalk bags use a **drawcord and toggle**, mainly to stop chalk spilling when you stuff the bag in your pack (REI). Buckets mostly use a **roll-top** plus magnets, Velcro or a buckle.

- **Magnetic closure**: closes with a light touch. When [jim](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ggHKVUp_wEQ) compared the BD Mondo and Mondito, the Mondito, which has no magnets, still leaked a little chalk in his pack even after he rolled it up.
- **Toggle placement**: Treeline suggests a cinch in the middle, or on the side of your dominant hand. The BD Mojo and Organic cinch in the middle, so either hand can work them; the Mammut Alpine's cinch is on the left, which suits left-handers.
- **Loose chalk users**: Treeline recommends a closure that cinches especially tight.

Something you can do tonight: cinch your bag shut, turn it upside down and tap it twice. If chalk comes out, put it in a plastic bag before it goes in your pack.

### 4. Lining, brush holder, pockets

- **Fleece lining**: holds down chalk dust and spreads chalk more evenly on your hands (REI). Petzl stitches the lining to the bottom of the bag so it doesn't pull inside-out when you withdraw your hand ([Petzl BANDI](https://petzl.com/US/en/Sport/Packs-And-Accessories/BANDI)). The Japanese channel やむラジ points out that many people put their chalk in a zip-lock bag inside the chalk bag, which makes the lining pointless ([やむラジ, 2026](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6_BCSvyv_4w)).
- **Brush holder**: almost every bag has one. Holders and brushes don't always match in size, and a loose one lets the brush fall out. Brushes are for cleaning old chalk off holds; see [Climbing conditions and friction](/posts/climbing/2026-10-19-climbing-conditions-friction-en).
- **Pockets**: for a phone, tape, keys. Most chalk bags have none, and those that do often can't take a phone: Treeline says neither of the Mammut Alpine's two pockets holds one.

Something you can do tonight: put your brush in the holder, turn the bag upside down and shake it. If the brush falls out, use a thicker brush or wrap a rubber band around the handle.

### 5. Belt or harness clip: two camps

There's no consensus here, and both sides have a point:

- **Use the belt**: Black Diamond's Gear Myths argues for the belt that comes with the bag. In their test, clipping the bag to the harness haul loop with a carabiner put the bottom of the bag about 6.5 inches (about 16 cm) lower than on the belt, and it swings on steep ground; a belt also lets you slide the bag round to wherever suits you. They also recommend keeping non-climbing accessory carabiners away from your climbing gear ([Black Diamond](https://blackdiamondequipment.com/blogs/stories/gear-myths-methods-for-clipping-a-chalk-bag)).
- **Clip it to the harness**: REI says you can skip the belt and clip the bag to your harness, which gives you a backup carabiner.
- **Use a climbing-rated carabiner**: Treeline never carries non-climbing-rated carabiners on a harness, so nobody grabs the wrong one in an emergency and watches it break under load. The little carabiners some chalk bags ship with are exactly that kind.
- **A multi-pitch trick**: Treeline mentions climbers who use a cordelette as the waist belt, so it can come off and be used to bail if needed.

Our suggestion: at the gym, start with the belt; it's simplest. If you clip it to your harness, use a real climbing carabiner, not the keyring-style one that came with the bag.

Something you can do tonight: look at the carabiner that came with your bag. If it has no kN rating and no CE mark stamped on it, take it off and keep it off your harness.

## How you carry chalk: loose, ball, liquid

Chalk itself isn't the subject here, but the kind you use affects which bag to buy.

- **Loose chalk**: poured straight into the bag; you get a thick coat fast, but it spills and makes dust (REI). Pair it with a tight closure (Treeline).
- **Chalk ball**: chalk wrapped in porous fabric that lets chalk through when you squeeze it; cleaner and less wasteful, but hard to get a thick coat (REI). If you use a ball, a bag is enough and a bucket's space is wasted (Caleb J. Ross). If you have big hands, the bag needs to be wide enough to grip the ball in it (Treeline).
- **Liquid chalk**: chalk mixed with alcohol; rub it on, the alcohol evaporates and leaves a layer of chalk, with the least dust (REI). The downside is that it dries out your skin; see [Skin care for climbers](/posts/climbing/2026-10-19-climbing-skin-care-en).

**Some gyms ban loose chalk.** Examples from abroad:

- [Cove Bouldering](https://www.covebouldering.com/policy-changes-jan-2024) in Canada banned loose chalk from 2024; powder must be inside a chalk ball, bomb or sock, and liquid chalk is allowed.
- [BlocHaus](https://syd.blochaus.com.au/haus-rules/) in Australia allows only liquid chalk and chalk balls.
- [Whistler Core](https://whistlercore.com/pages/climbing-gym-rules) in Canada doesn't even allow chalk balls; liquid chalk only.

In Taiwan, the safety rules at [T-UP Wanhua](https://tupclimbing.tw/terms-and-conditions/77) and [Passion Climbing Gym](https://www.passionclimbinggym.com.tw/h/datadetail?key=imts0&cont=34898) currently say nothing about chalk. Rules change and differ between gyms, so ask your regular gym before you buy loose chalk.

A bit of chalk etiquette while you're at it: chalk up with your hands over the bag and don't clap ([IndiRock](https://indirock.co.uk/good-chalk-etiquette-part-2/)); close the bag when you're not using it.

Something you can do tonight: open your regular gym's website or Instagram and look for its rules. If chalk isn't mentioned, ask at the desk next time: "Is loose chalk OK?"

## Choosing for three situations

### You've started going to the gym regularly, bouldering and top-roping

- One waist bag is enough; it works for bouldering too. An entry bag like the BD Mojo or Mammut Crag will do.
- Big hands: buy the M/L or the Organic XL rather than forcing your hand in.
- To buy it off the shelf in Taiwan, the Mammut Gym Basic is listed by Keep On (out of stock on 2026-10-24).

### You only boulder and often spend whole days at the gym

- A bucket wastes less chalk and holds brushes, tape and a phone. If you want it to close at a touch, pick a magnetic one like the SAKAB or Mondo.
- If you only use a chalk ball, a bag is enough; skip the bucket.

### You climb sport routes outdoors or multi-pitch

- Rock scrapes your bag outdoors; Cordura-shelled bags like the Organic last longer.
- For multi-pitch, where you carry small items, choose one with pockets and a locking belt buckle, such as the Mammut Alpine.
- If you clip it to your harness, use a climbing-rated carabiner, or follow the trick Treeline mentions and use a cordelette as the belt.

## Inspection and retirement

A chalk bag isn't protective equipment, and a failure won't injure you, so there's no retirement age. Things to watch:

- **Belt buckle**: a cracked or loose plastic buckle can let the bag drop mid-route.
- **Carabiner**: if you clip the bag to your harness, inspect that carabiner like any other climbing carabiner; see the last post in this series, [Inspecting and retiring climbing gear](/posts/climbing/2026-10-19-climbing-gear-inspection-retirement-en).
- **Washing**: Treeline recommends washing 2–3 times a year, emptying the chalk first and ideally washing it on its own. Treeline says that unless the maker specifies otherwise, most bags can be washed with a gentle detergent by hand or on a gentle machine cycle; advice varies by brand, so check the label first.
- **Collapsed rim or a closure that won't cinch**: still usable, but it will leak, so consider replacing it.

## Practice: three scenarios

The answer is under each question. Think first, then look.

**1. You mostly boulder and occasionally top-rope. A friend says boulderers must have a bucket, but you only want to buy one thing for now.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Buy a waist bag first. On top-rope you need to chalk up on the wall, which only a bag allows; you can boulder with a bag too; if you use loose chalk, follow Treeline's advice and pick one that cinches tight. If bouldering takes over later, add a bucket then.

</details>

**2. Your new chalk bag came with a small aluminium carabiner, and you want to use it to clip the bag to your harness haul loop.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Don't use that little carabiner. It's most likely not climbing-rated; on your harness, someone could grab it by mistake in an emergency and it would break under load. To clip the bag to your harness, use a real climbing carabiner; or just use the belt, which in BD's test sat higher and swung less.

</details>

**3. You're travelling and want to climb at a local gym, with a bag of loose chalk and your chalk bag in your pack.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Check that gym's rules first. Some gyms abroad allow only chalk balls or liquid chalk, like BlocHaus in Australia; some don't even allow chalk balls and permit liquid chalk only, like Whistler Core in Canada. Bringing a chalk ball or a small bottle of liquid chalk is the safer bet.

</details>
