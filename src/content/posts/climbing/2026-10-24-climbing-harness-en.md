In the shop you pick up a harness, step in, pull the waistbelt to where it "seems about right" and tighten it. The staff ask if you'd like to hang in it to check. You say no; the gym's rental harness goes on the same way.

This is post 3 of the Climbing Gear series. [Post 0](/posts/climbing/2026-10-19-climbing-gear-buying-order-en) explained that a harness is personal protective equipment, with standards and labels. [The UIAA's 2026 buying advice](https://theuiaa.org/uiaa/uiaa-advice-ten-things-to-consider-when-buying-climbing-gear) singles out harnesses (and helmets): **fit affects their ability to protect you**. Try before you buy where possible, and since sizing varies a lot between brands, use the manufacturer's size chart rather than S, M or L. This post answers two questions: how to tell whether a harness suits you, and which current models are worth trying first. Tying in and partner checks are in [gym top-rope belaying](/posts/climbing/2026-10-17-gym-top-rope-belay-en); helmets have their own post, [post 6](/posts/climbing/2026-10-24-climbing-helmet-en). By the end you should know how many gear loops you need, whether you want adjustable leg loops, and how to try a harness on in the shop.

## The five parts of a harness

Names as used in [Petzl Taiwan's harness guide](https://www.petzltw.com/pages/舒適的盪在空中指南_如何挑選攀岩吊帶) (in Chinese, published 2026-07-16):

- **Waistbelt**: goes around your waist, adjusted with a metal buckle.
- **Leg loops**: go around your thighs; adjustable or fixed.
- **Belay loop**: the loop at the front joining waistbelt and leg loops. [REI's harness guide](https://www.rei.com/learn/expert-advice/climbing-harness.html) calls it the strongest point on the harness; your locking carabiner goes here when belaying or rappelling.
- **Tie-in points**: two small loops on the waistbelt and leg loops that the rope passes through. Petzl Taiwan stresses that **both must be threaded**, never just one.
- **Gear loops**: loops on the sides for carrying gear. Petzl Taiwan says **gear loops must never bear body weight**; they only carry equipment.

There are also **elastic leg-loop straps** at the back, which hold the leg loops in position on your thighs.

To see it on a real harness, the sport-climbing intro that Ryan of HowNOT2 filmed with Ben of Hard Is Easy gets to harnesses at 6:34: a waistbelt and two leg loops meeting at the front in a single belay loop. Ryan's one warning is that some gym harnesses sew everything into a single master point; when you buy your own, get one with a real belay loop. The segment covers only the overall structure, so for how to use the tie-in points and gear loops, go by Petzl Taiwan and REI above. From 9:40 it's an ad for the HowNOT2 shop.

## Four criteria

### 1. Type and gear loops: choose by what you'll climb

[REI](https://www.rei.com/learn/expert-advice/climbing-harness.html) sorts harnesses into several types:

| Type | Features | Suits |
|---|---|---|
| Sport/indoor | Self-locking buckle, 2–4 gear loops (usually 2 is enough), light | Gym, outdoor sport |
| Trad | Adjustable leg loops, 4 or more gear loops, thicker padding | Placing your own gear, long hours on a route |
| Alpine | Fully adjustable waist and legs, 4 or fewer gear loops, thin belay loop | Mountains, easy on and off |

For gear loops, Petzl Taiwan's rule of thumb is easy to remember:

- **Indoor top-roping**: you only carry a belay device, so 2 are enough.
- **Outdoor sport, single pitch**: you only carry quickdraws, so about 4.
- **Trad and multi-pitch**: more gear, so 6 is more comfortable.

Almost every harness on sale has at least 4, so the count is rarely the problem; size and position are. That's what the "Gear loops" column in the model table below compares.

Something you can do tonight: write down what you're most likely to climb in the next year: only the gym, outdoor sport, or starting trad. Use that to decide how many gear loops you need, so a "do-everything" harness doesn't tempt you into buying something you won't use.

### 2. Leg loops: adjustable or fixed

Petzl Taiwan says adjustable loops let you fit over thicker winter layers outdoors; fixed loops are lighter and skip the buckles. Fixed leg loops are usually a band with some elastic, so they stretch a little, but not far.

Both are safe. The difference is whether you have a way out if you pick the wrong size:

- **Fixed leg loops**: [GearJunkie's 2026 harness guide](https://gearjunkie.com/climbing/best-climbing-harnesses) puts it plainly for the BD Solution: the leg loops are fixed, so try it on before buying, because they can't be adjusted later.
- **Adjustable leg loops**: [Switchback Travel](https://switchbacktravel.com/climbing/best-harnesses) finds them "almost mandatory for muscular legs".
- **Not sure yet what you'll climb**: in a [2024 WeighMyRack video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Xk88XkSQnPA), a Mammut presenter says a four-buckle harness, two on the waist and one on each leg, makes it easier to find the right size; add a layer at the gym or crag and a single-buckle harness can end up off-centre. That's the brand's own pitch.

Something you can do tonight: put on your thickest winter trousers and measure around the widest part of your thigh, then measure again in shorts. If the two numbers differ a lot and you plan to climb outside in winter, put adjustable leg loops first.

### 3. Fitting: waistbelt above the hips, tight enough not to slip out upside down

Two Taiwanese sources seem to put the waistbelt in different places:

- [Petzl Taiwan](https://www.petzltw.com/pages/舒適的盪在空中指南_如何挑選攀岩吊帶) (in Chinese): wear the waistbelt **above the hip bones**, tightened fully so it sits firmly on them and you won't slip out the top if you end up upside down.
- [Adventure Taiwan](https://www.adventuretaiwan.com/how-to-wear-climbing-harness/) (in Chinese): pull it up to roughly **belly-button height**, tight enough that you can slide a flat hand between body and belt but can't easily turn it.

[REI](https://www.rei.com/learn/expert-advice/climbing-harness.html) connects the two: put the waistbelt slightly above your iliac crest, which is **near belly-button level for most people**. REI's tightness check is no more than a two-finger gap between waist and belt. So the two descriptions mostly agree; where your belly button sits varies, so go by your hip bones, and your harness's manual takes priority.

Leg loops matter less. Petzl Taiwan says comfortable and snug is enough. REI says tighter leg loops are more comfortable when hanging but restrict movement, looser ones the opposite; both are safe, so it's personal preference.

#### Hang in it in the shop

REI says you can't know whether a harness is comfortable without hanging in it or weighting it. Some shops have a test station or a wall where you can hang on a rope. Once you're hanging, check:

- **Can you sit upright?** Like sitting in a chair, without using your core. If you have to work to stay upright, shorten the elastic straps at the back of the leg loops; if that doesn't help, try another harness.
- **Does the waistbelt shift?** If so, tighten until it stops. Before weighting it, also try pulling the waistbelt down over your hips; you shouldn't be able to. If the shop allows it, slowly invert to test for slippage.
- **Any pressure points?** If so, consider another model.

In this Japanese video, Kuriyama of the mountaineering school Kuri Adventures demonstrates how to put a harness on. From 2:56: check that the leg loops aren't twisted before stepping in, because strength drops sharply where webbing twists. The waistbelt goes above the hip bones; set below them, it slips right off when you end up head-down. From 3:59 he checks that the rear loop sits on the spine and the belay loop sits straight in front; if it's off-centre, fall forces don't land where the maker designed them to. From 4:47, the leg loops: not too tight, not too loose, with room to slip one flat hand in easily. From 5:04 he pulls the belay loop upwards, and leg loops and waistbelt should share the load as if you're sitting. Waistbelt tension is at 3:44: once cinched, two flat hands side by side should only just slide in at the front, without the belt squeezing your waist. That's a different check from REI's two fingers; follow your own harness's manual. It's the school's own channel, the end of the video promotes its courses, and no sponsor is mentioned.

Tighten the leg loops and the rear elastics so the loops stay high on the thighs; then the force is shared between waistbelt and leg loops and you stay upright. It's the maker's own channel.

#### Women's, men's, and how sizes line up

Petzl and Black Diamond both make women's and men's versions. [Petzl's SELENA page](https://www.petzl.com/US/en/Sport/Harnesses/SELENA) says the women's model differs in waistbelt shape, rise (the distance from waistbelt to leg loops) and the ratio of waistbelt to leg loops. [OutdoorGearLab's women's guide](https://outdoorgearlab.com/topics/climbing/best-climbing-harness-womens) notes that athletic bodies don't always fit the "narrow waist, bigger thighs" assumption, so men's or unisex models are worth trying too. Fit matters more than the label.

Sizing also changed in 2025. In a [WeighMyRack video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0xZ3cRBVFWI), Petzl America's marketing manager Ben Eaton says the 2025 Sama, Selena, Adjama and Luna keep the same size ranges. What changed is where the belay loop centres: it used to centre when fully cinched, and now it centres with about 2 cm left before fully tight. He went from a large to a medium himself and suggests owners of the old version consider sizing down. But he also says to pick the size that fits and is comfortable first, and worry about centring second.

Something you can do tonight: put on your harness (or your next rental), check the two-finger gap at the waistbelt, then try to pull the belt down over your hips. If it goes, it's too loose.

### 4. Buckles: does yours need doubling back?

Adventure Taiwan says newer harnesses usually don't need doubling back, but some do: the webbing is threaded through the buckle and then back again. To tell, look at the buckle's design, or for a word like **DANGER** printed on it; if it's there, double it back. REI's fitting steps also say to make sure every buckle is doubled back (not needed on self-locking buckles).

A rental harness and your own may have different buckles, so the first thing with any new harness is to check how its buckle works. The partner-check routine is in [gym top-rope belaying](/posts/climbing/2026-10-17-gym-top-rope-belay-en).

From 5:50 in this HowNOT2 try-on video, Ryan uses an aid-climbing harness that needs doubling back (the Misty Mountain Titan) to show what to look for: if you can see an "O" at the buckle it's open; once you can't see either side of the top buckle and only a "C" is left, it's closed. His point is that because most harnesses are self-locking now, the odd one that needs doubling back is easier to forget. It shows only one buckle design, so follow your own harness's manual; the harnesses in the video are also sold in the HowNOT2 shop.

<details><summary>What EN 12277/UIAA 105 tests</summary>

[The UIAA 105/EN 12277 pictorial](https://www.theuiaa.org/documents/safety-standards/Pictorial_UIAA105%20Harnesses.pdf) lists the key numbers:

- Sit-harness strength test: a dummy in the harness is pulled to 15 kN.
- Every loop provided for abseiling (rappelling) must withstand at least 15 kN (10 kN for small-body versions).
- Slippage at every buckle: 20 mm maximum.
- All harnesses must be labelled with the year of manufacture.
- Load-bearing stitching must contrast with the webbing in colour or surface appearance; the UIAA adds that at least 50% of visible stitching in load-bearing parts must contrast in colour.

Petzl Taiwan's guide gives the certifications for climbing harnesses as "CE EN 12277 Type C (sit harness), and UIAA 105".

**What these numbers do and don't mean**: a new harness of this model passed these lab tests. It doesn't mean a harness worn wrongly, buckled wrongly, worn out or past its lifetime can take the same load. The contrasting stitching is there so you can see when it's worn through, which [the last post in this series](/posts/climbing/2026-10-19-climbing-gear-inspection-retirement-en) uses.

The flip side: if you can't find these markings on a harness, don't buy it. In the JB Mountain Skills video below there's an off-brand harness bought online that looks much like the real thing but has no CE marking.

## Choosing for three situations

### Gym only, not sure about going outside

- Make your first harness one that adjusts at waist and legs: the BD Momentum or Petzl Corax.
- If your size is uncertain, or you may share with family or a partner, the Corax's two sizes cover a wide range.
- There's no need to buy a trad harness just because you "might climb trad one day".

### Mainly gym and outdoor sport

- Look at fixed-leg harnesses like the BD Solution or Petzl Sama/Selena, lighter and slimmer than the entry models.
- Always hang in one in the shop; the leg loops can't be adjusted after you buy.
- If you often climb outside in winter or have big thighs, look at adjustable leg loops instead.

### Getting into trad or multi-pitch

- Look at the Petzl Adjama/Luna, with 5 gear loops and adjustable legs.
- Rack the quickdraws and protection you'll actually carry and reach for them, checking you can find the rear loops.
- Save lightweight models like the Sitta for when you know you need to cut weight.

## Inspection and retirement

- **Check the year of manufacture**: EN 12277 requires every harness to be labelled with it. With a borrowed or second-hand harness you don't know its history; as [post 0](/posts/climbing/2026-10-19-climbing-gear-buying-order-en) noted, Petzl lists "you do not know its full usage history" as a reason to retire gear.
- **Check the stitching**: load-bearing stitching contrasts in colour so you can see when it's worn through.
- **Check the belay loop**: it takes the most abrasion. In a [2024 WeighMyRack video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Xk88XkSQnPA), a Mammut presenter says the Mammut harnesses it covers have red indicator thread inside the stitching of the tie-in points and belay loop, and once you can see red it's time to retire the harness; he adds that you should inspect it every time regardless (from 1:20).
- **Check the buckles**: watch for bent or corroded buckles and elastic that has lost its stretch.

Full inspection and retirement rules are in the last post of this series, [How to inspect and retire climbing gear](/posts/climbing/2026-10-19-climbing-gear-inspection-retirement-en).

## Practice: four scenarios

Answers are under each question. Think first, then look.

**1. You try a harness with the waistbelt on your belly, but you can pull it down past your hips by hand.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Too loose, or too low. Petzl Taiwan says the belt goes above the hip bones, tightened fully so you can't slip out upside down; REI's check is no more than two fingers of gap and no pulling it past your hips. Tighten and try again; if it still comes down at its tightest, go down a size or try another model.

</details>

**2. You only top-rope in the gym. The staff recommend a trad harness with 6 gear loops and adjustable legs, "so you can use it outside later."**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Petzl Taiwan's guidance is 2 gear loops for indoor top-roping and about 4 for outdoor sport. A trad harness is heavier and bulkier than you need now. Buy for what you'll climb in the next year.

</details>

**3. A friend lends you an old harness with "DANGER" printed on the buckle and says, "Just thread it through."**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Adventure Taiwan says buckles with a word like DANGER usually need doubling back: thread the webbing through, then back again. Without that, the buckle may not hold. Do a partner check once it's on. Also, you don't know a borrowed harness's history or manufacture year; as post 0 noted, Petzl lists "you do not know its full usage history" as a reason to retire gear.

</details>

**4. In summer you try on a sport harness with fixed leg loops, and in shorts it fits just right. You plan to climb outside a lot in winter too.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Fixed leg loops can't be adjusted after you buy, which is why GearJunkie recommends trying them on first. In thick winter trousers they may be too tight. Try it again wearing your winter trousers; if you'll often climb in the cold, look at adjustable leg loops instead, such as the BD Momentum, Petzl Corax or Adjama/Luna.

</details>

## Further viewing: gear-testing and instruction channels

Several of the videos embedded above come from these two channels. If you want to dig further, start here, and keep each channel's limits in mind.

- **[HowNOT2](https://www.youtube.com/@HowNOT2)**: run by Ryan Jenks. It started in 2016 as highline safety instruction and grew into breaking climbing, caving and canyoneering gear, plus the Bolt Busters series that pulls bolts out of real rock. Its value is in the cases beyond the rated number: knots, wear, old gear, bad placements. Limits: most tests are slow pulls on a home-built rig with one to three samples, and Ryan himself calls it entertainment, not science; the channel is backed by an online gear shop, and some tested items are sold there.
- **[Hard Is Easy](https://www.youtube.com/@HardIsEasy)**: run solo by a climber who signs as Ben, a former software developer. After watching a hard catch slam a climber into the rock in Tonsai, Thailand, he designed his first experiment; the channel now focuses on experiments and physics explanations about belaying, ropes, carabiners and belay devices, with several recent episodes filmed in Mammut's lab. Limits: most experiments are his own design, not EN/UIAA standard tests.
