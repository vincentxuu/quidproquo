Nobody wears a helmet in the gym. The first time you go to an outdoor crag with friends, you notice everyone has one on, including the person belaying at the bottom. You ask which one to buy: someone says the white Petzl, someone else says lighter is better, and the shop has them from under 1,000 to nearly 6,000 Taiwan dollars.

This is post 6 in the Climbing Gear series. [Post 0](/posts/climbing/2026-10-19-climbing-gear-buying-order-en) explained that helmets are personal protective equipment, with standards and labels; the [UIAA's buying advice](https://theuiaa.org/uiaa/uiaa-advice-ten-things-to-consider-when-buying-climbing-gear) singles out helmets (and harnesses): **how well they fit affects how well they protect you**, and sizing varies a lot between brands, so check the manufacturer's size chart. This post answers two questions: which construction suits the terrain you're heading for, and which current helmets are worth trying on first. Why belayers outdoors wear helmets too is covered, with accident reports, in [outdoor belaying](/posts/climbing/2026-10-17-outdoor-belay-en); harnesses are in [post 3](/posts/climbing/2026-10-24-climbing-harness-en). By the end you should know which type of helmet you want and how to try one on in a shop.

## Four criteria

### 1. Construction: hard shell, foam shell, hybrid

[Petzl Taiwan's 2026 helmet guide](https://www.petzltw.com/pages/頂上防禦力提升指南_如何挑選戶外頭盔) (in Chinese) sorts helmets into three types by material:

| Type | Construction | Weight | Durability | Price | Petzl examples |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Hard shell | ABS outer shell with a foam liner | Heaviest, mostly 300-plus g | Toughest | Affordable | Boreo, Borea |
| Foam shell | EPS or EPP foam body under a thin polycarbonate (PC) shell | Middle, mostly 200-plus g | Middle | Middle | Meteor |
| Hybrid | Foam body, with PC or ABS reinforcing key areas only | Lightest, under 200 g | Handle with care | Highest | Sirocco |

[REI's helmet guide](https://www.rei.com/learn/expert-advice/climbing-helmet.html) uses only two types: hardshell (very hard outer shell, strap suspension, thin foam liner; cheap, durable, long-lasting) and foam shell (thick foam under a thin shell that deforms to absorb an impact; light, well ventilated, more expensive). Petzl Taiwan's foam shell and hybrid both count as foam shells in REI's scheme.

REI also warns that brands use different names, so judge by construction. Petzl itself is an example: the Taiwan guide calls the Boreo a hard shell, while [Petzl's US site](https://www.petzl.com/US/en/Sport/Helmets/BOREO) describes its ABS shell with dual foam liner as "hybrid construction".

Petzl Taiwan adds that with certification, both materials can absorb a single impact; the difference is what happens afterwards. Foam absorbs energy by deforming and must be retired once deformed; ABS copes better with repeated knocks and wear.

Which to choose? The two sources don't quite agree:

- [DAV's 2010 article on gear lifetime](https://sac-hoherrohn.ch/wp-content/uploads/2019/02/dav_lebensdauer-von-alpinausruestung.pdf) (in German): hardshells generally cope better with rockfall and are clearly tougher under multiple impacts (tumbling down broken ground); light foam helmets give the best energy absorption in a single impact. So DAV suggests **foam rather for alpine sport climbing, hardshell for classic alpine terrain**.
- REI: for **multi-pitch trad and mountaineering**, where you carry a lot and wear the helmet for hours, low weight and ventilation matter, so consider foam; for cool-weather single-pitch sport, where you can take the helmet off between climbs, you can choose a cheaper, durable hardshell.

They weigh different things: DAV the kind of impact, REI the burden of wearing it all day. DAV is writing about alpine settings; REI singles out single-pitch sport climbing on its own. Both are laid out here so you can match each to the terrain you're heading for.

In this beginner's guide to sport climbing that HowNOT2 shot with Hard Is Easy, helmets come up at 10:07. HowNOT2's Ryan wears an all-plastic hard shell; Ben from Hard Is Easy wears one that is half foam with a more durable shell over the top. Ben's short version: for sport climbing, go for a hybrid with more foam and more ventilation, because you get hot when you climb, and sport-oriented helmets are lighter, so you put one on and forget about it. That is Ben's own advice, not a finding that overrides DAV or REI, and he is talking about sport climbing, not alpine rockfall. The video says no one sponsors the channel and that viewers support it by buying gear from HowNOT2's site, which means HowNOT2 sells gear itself.

### 2. Side protection: read what the manufacturer says

A helmet isn't only for rocks falling on top of your head. In a tumbling fall, or swinging sideways into a ledge, it's the side that gets hit. In the same video, from 11:55, Ben says that if you flip upside down on a climb you will hit your head, and you want it to be your helmet that hits, not your head; he has watched plenty of top climbers who skip helmets flip over too.

EN 12492 already tests front, side and rear absorption (see the fold-out at the end of this section), just from a lower drop height than the top. Several brands add their own side-protection labels. Since 2020 every Petzl sport helmet carries [TOP AND SIDE PROTECTION](https://www.petzl.com/US/en/Sport/News/2020-1-14/Enhanced-Protection-Now-Comes-Standard-in-All-Petzl-Sport-Helmets), which Petzl says guarantees side, front and rear protection beyond what the EN/UIAA standard requires; Black Diamond says the Capitan E and Vision meet "the proposed UIAA increased side and back protection requirements". These are manufacturer claims, tested by methods the manufacturers set.

The comparison table below copies what each manufacturer says about side protection, and says "not stated" where it says nothing.

Something you can do tonight: turn your helmet (or the one in the shop) over and see whether the foam reaches the sides and the back of the head. A helmet with a single block of foam on top gives less protection in a side impact.

### 3. Head size and head shape: always try it on

[Petzl Taiwan](https://www.petzltw.com/pages/頂上防禦力提升指南_如何挑選戶外頭盔) measures head circumference as a horizontal loop just above the forehead. Most helmets come in two sizes; leave a little room so you can wear a thin hat underneath in the cold.

The right circumference isn't enough. Petzl Taiwan also notes that some helmets are longer and narrower, others rounder, so it's best to try one on before your first purchase. [OutdoorGearLab](https://www.outdoorgearlab.com/topics/climbing/best-climbing-helmet) is more blunt: foam helmets are molded to a fixed shape, and the headband adjusts size, not shape.

This matters especially for Asian readers. English-language reviews don't discuss Asian head shapes directly; what we found are opinions in two Japanese videos, personal views rather than measurements:

- In a comparison filmed at a mountaineering shop by こうたろチャンネル (from 24:00), the staff member says most overseas brands are designed around European and American head shapes, Japanese heads are rounder, and he points to Grivel's wider "Japan fit" as an example.
- Hiking blogger ヤマノ (from 3:00) says Western brands tend to be longer front to back and often don't suit Japanese heads, so don't skip trying them on.

Taiwanese readers' heads aren't necessarily the same as Japanese heads, so take these two as a reminder: the same M/L can feel very different between brands, so try several.

### 4. Fitting: no wobble when you shake your head, no slack in the chin strap

REI's steps:

1. Put the helmet on squarely, with the front rim straight across your forehead, not tipped back.
2. Adjust it, then shake your head side to side and tilt it back slightly. The helmet should stay snug and not move.
3. Buckle the chin strap with **no slack**. The front and rear straps should form a "Y" around each ear.

REI also suggests trying the adjustment system to see how intuitive it is. In the cold you may wear a hat under it and need to readjust.

WeighMyRack's video on the 2024 Sirocco shows an adjustment order from 2:30 that pairs well with REI's steps:

- Back first: set the height of the rear cradle below the bump at the back of your skull, then tighten.
- Then the Y straps by the ears: not pressing on the ear, not below the jawline.
- Chin strap last: not loose, but loose enough to talk and breathe.

The presenter is from Petzl, so this is a brand demonstration; the price he quotes differs from the current list price, which takes precedence.

<details><summary>What EN 12492/UIAA 106 tests</summary>

[Petzl Taiwan](https://www.petzltw.com/pages/頂上防禦力提升指南_如何挑選戶外頭盔) says a climbing or mountaineering helmet needs either CE certification (to EN 12492) or UIAA certification.

[The UIAA 106/EN 12492 pictorial](https://www.theuiaa.org/documents/safety-standards/Pictorial_UIAA106%20Helmets.pdf):

- **Top energy absorption**: a 5 kg mass dropped 2 m onto the helmet; force transmitted to the headform must be no more than 10 kN for EN, 8 kN for UIAA.
- **Front, side and rear absorption**: 5 kg from 0.5 m at 60 degrees, same limits.
- **Penetration**: a 3 kg cone dropped 1 m must not touch the headform.
- **Chin strap**: 500 N with no breakage and no more than 25 mm extension.
- **Slippage**: pulled from the front and back, the helmet must not come off the headform.

The table also shows the UIAA being stricter than EN on some items: 8 kN versus 10 kN for top absorption.

**What it does and doesn't mean**: passing shows a new helmet can absorb these impacts. It doesn't mean a helmet that's been hit, warped in the sun or covered in stickers still can. The [last post of this series](/posts/climbing/2026-10-19-climbing-gear-inspection-retirement-en) covers those.

</details>

## Choosing for three situations

### Your first helmet, mostly single-pitch at the crag

- Choose a hard shell: the Petzl Boreo or BD Capitan E. You can take it off between climbs, so weight matters less than toughness.
- Try both brands and pick the one that doesn't move when you shake your head and doesn't press by your ears.

### Starting multi-pitch, or wearing it for long hours

- Look at foam shells: the Petzl Meteor or Mammut Crag Sender, and the BD Vision if the budget allows.
- Don't pack heavy things on top of the helmet; pressed foam keeps the dent.
- If you'll wear a beanie or a hood under it, bring it when trying helmets on; you may need the larger size.

### Already committed to long routes and alpine terrain

- Now a hybrid like the Petzl Sirocco is worth considering: it's the lightest, and the easiest to crush.
- You could do what Ryan Tilley does: a durable helmet for the crag, a light one for the mountains, and rotate.

## Inspection and retirement

- **Check the sides**: on light helmets, dents often show up on the sides. Ryan Tilley checks the sides first when retiring a helmet; dents or cracks mean it goes.
- **Deformed foam means retirement**: Petzl Taiwan says foam absorbs impact by deforming, absorbs less once deformed, and must then be retired.
- **Stickers**: the shop staff in the こうたろチャンネル video warn that a sticker can hide damage.

For the full inspection and retirement rules, see the last post in this series, [How to Inspect and Retire Climbing Gear](/posts/climbing/2026-10-19-climbing-gear-inspection-retirement-en).

## Practice: four scenarios

Answers are under each question. Think first, then look.

**1. You put on a helmet. The front rim sits high above your forehead, the chin strap is loose, and the helmet wobbles when you shake your head.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Follow REI's steps: wear it squarely with the rim across your forehead, adjust until it doesn't move when you shake your head, and buckle the chin strap with no slack, the straps forming a Y around your ears. If you can't stop it moving, try another model. The UIAA also notes that fit affects how well a helmet protects you.

</details>

**2. You're heading out for your first day of single-pitch at the crag, and a friend says the Sirocco is the lightest and best, so just buy that.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The Sirocco is a hybrid, and Petzl Taiwan itself says this type needs careful handling and costs the most. On single pitches you can take the helmet off between climbs, and REI says that's when a cheaper, durable hard shell makes sense. Look at something like the Boreo or Capitan E first, and consider the Sirocco once you know you'll be on long routes.

</details>

**3. You find a well-reviewed helmet online whose size range includes your head circumference, and you want to order it straight away.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The right circumference doesn't mean the right shape. Petzl Taiwan says some helmets are longer and narrower, others rounder, and Japanese shop staff and bloggers warn that Western brands may not suit Asian heads. Try it on in a shop if you can, comparing at least two brands; if you do buy online, check the return policy first.

</details>

**4. A friend offers you the foam helmet they've used for several years. There are a few dents on the side, and they say "the top is fine, it's still good".**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Don't use it. A foam helmet absorbs impact by deforming and absorbs less once deformed; Petzl Taiwan says it must then be retired, and Ryan Tilley checks the sides first when retiring a helmet. You also don't know its full history, and as [post 0](/posts/climbing/2026-10-19-climbing-gear-buying-order-en) mentioned, Petzl lists that as a reason to retire gear. [The UIAA's 2026 buying advice](https://theuiaa.org/uiaa/uiaa-advice-ten-things-to-consider-when-buying-climbing-gear) also says helmets, harnesses and ropes should not be acquired second-hand, because damage to them is especially hard to detect.

</details>
