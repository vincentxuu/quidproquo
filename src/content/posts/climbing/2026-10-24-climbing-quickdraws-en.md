> 🌏 [中文版](/posts/climbing/2026-10-24-climbing-quickdraws)

A quickdraw is two carabiners joined by a sewn sling. On lead you clip one end to a bolt and the rope into the other, and when you fall, that's what catches the rope. Gym lead routes are mostly pre-hung with draws; once you start sport climbing outdoors, you need a set of your own.

This post answers one question: which quickdraws to buy. How to clip and orient them is in [gym lead climbing](/posts/climbing/2026-10-17-gym-lead-climbing-en); preparing for your first outdoor lead is in [first outdoor sport lead](/posts/climbing/2026-10-17-first-outdoor-sport-lead-en); choosing locking carabiners is in [Carabiners](/posts/climbing/2026-10-24-climbing-carabiners-en). By the end you should know which gate and sling length you want, and be able to pick one model from the tables below.

> ⚠️ **Recall notice: Decathlon SIMOND quickdraws**
>
> [Taiwan's Bureau of Standards, Metrology and Inspection (BSMI) announced on 2026-10-19](https://www.bsmi.gov.tw/SafetyGIP/wSite/ct?xItem=130064&ctNode=8206&mp=65) (in Chinese) that Decathlon Taiwan is recalling SIMOND "登山快扣" (mountaineering quickdraws), models **8589547, 8589548, 8608405, 8935765, 8935909**, sold in Taiwan from 1 January 2022 to 22 July 2026. The notice says some draws have a defect where the wire is riveted, which can break the wiregate and create a risk of the quickdraw breaking in a fall. SIMOND's [recall page](https://simondrecallquickdraw.fandi.fr) maps these five models to the Alpinism 11 cm, Alpinism 17 cm, Alpinism pack of 5, Vertika 11 cm and Vertika 17 cm.
>
> If you own one, check it the way the notice describes: hold the draw in one hand, put your thumb between the two wires near the rivet and press down hard; flip it over and repeat on the other side, confirming both sides of the wiregate are still firmly attached. If it's affected, stop using it and return it to any Decathlon store for a refund.

## The anatomy: two carabiners and a sling

The [UIAA 121/EN 12275 classification](https://www.theuiaa.org/documents/safety-standards/Pictorial_UIAA121%20Connectors.pdf) has a separate type for quickdraw carabiners: Type T (directional). Which type your draws' carabiners are is marked on the body. The locking Type H carabiner you belay with is a different thing; see [Carabiners](/posts/climbing/2026-10-24-climbing-carabiners-en).

[DAV's 2010 article](https://sac-hoherrohn.ch/wp-content/uploads/2019/02/dav_lebensdauer-von-alpinausruestung.pdf) noted that keylock gates without a nose had become standard on the bolt end and wiregates on the rope end, and that top-standard carabiners of the time had gate-open strengths above 9 kN. Some draws still use that combination, such as the Hotforge Hybrid in the tables below; others use solid gates or wiregates at both ends. The criteria below are about choosing for each end.

How many to carry depends on how many bolts your route has. Gear shop Aspiring says in its [buying video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qdisp_XlDcU&t=120s) that 12 will do for most routes, but check the guidebook for the bolt count and carry a few extra. The HowNOT2 and Hard Is Easy intro video in the video section says you usually need 10 to 15 outdoors.

## Four criteria

### 1. Bolt end and rope end: keep them distinct, never swap

On a sport quickdraw, the rope end usually has a rubber or plastic keeper holding the carabiner in place on the sling, while the bolt end swings free. Aspiring explains at the start of the same video: the top carabiner sits loose on the sling so it can move as you climb past, and the keeper on the bottom one makes clipping more accurate, stops that carabiner cross-loading, and keeps the rope from unclipping as you climb past. The loose end tells you which one goes on the bolt.

Don't swap them. [Wild Country](https://wildcountry.com/en-us/carabiners-beta-talks) says to always clip bolts with the same carabiner, and to tell friends when you lend your draws. The bolt end rubs metal on metal and picks up small burrs; clip the rope into that carabiner and the burrs can damage it. Hard Is Easy tried this in Mammut's lab: a draw that hung for a year at a windy crag had a groove and small burrs worn into its bolt-end carabiner by the hanger. Turned around, with that carabiner taking the rope, it opened up the rope's sheath in three drops ([video from 25:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7qEvvobz5YQ&t=1500s)).

A loose bolt end also matters for whether a draw can unclip itself from a bolt. [An AAC accident report from November 2024](https://americanalpineclub.org/news/2024/11/18/the-prescription-november24) describes one case: a climber with ten years' experience passed the third bolt at Pilot Mountain, North Carolina, and fell while wrestling with an awkward bulge. The alpine draw on the hanger had already come unclipped; he hit the ground and fractured both heel bones.

The editors' advice:

- The hanger-end carabiner should be loose in the sling, never held by a rubber keeper.
- Both gates should face the same way.
- Clip so the spine faces your direction of travel and the gate faces away.
- For a critical clip, such as before or after a runout, use a locking carabiner on the hanger end.

Climbing Focus demonstrates how it happens on a bolt hanger in a podcast episode ([from 17:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lvRYPEXDzec&t=1020s)).

Something you can do tonight: lay out your draws and check that the two ends of each are distinguishable by colour or shape. Where they aren't, wrap a ring of tape around the bolt end.

### 2. Gate type: solid keylock or wiregate

[OutdoorGearLab's buying advice](https://www.outdoorgearlab.com/topics/climbing/best-quickdraw/buying-advice) compares them:

- **Solid gates**: now mostly keylock, with the nose slotting into a hole in the gate and no notch, so they don't snag when you unclip.
- **Wiregates**: generally cheaper and slightly lighter, with less gate flutter; but the notch on the nose tends to catch on things.
- **Combinations**: both solid, both wire, or solid on top and wire below all exist. OGL says which you prefer to clip is largely personal.

What wiregates are good for is laid out clearly in this carabiner explainer from Japanese mountain school Tozan Dojo (in Japanese). From 5:19 the instructor says wiregates handle snow and ice better than solid gates. He then taps both gate types: the solid gate clicks and the wiregate doesn't, because the solid gate springs open for an instant on impact. He calls it the whiplash effect, the same thing as gate flutter, and with the gate open a carabiner's strength drops sharply. Gate-open strength is printed on every carabiner, so go by the marking on yours. From 9:42 he covers the wiregate's downside: the hook under the nose catches rope and slings, which makes unclipping awkward. No sponsorship is mentioned; the channel belongs to his own mountain school, and the description has course contact details.

So which one? The videos from 2024 onward agree that it's mostly personal. A staffer at Canadian outdoor retailer MEC says neither gate is better or worse ([1:53](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6mY-5dg4hmw&t=113s)). Mike Vert finds solid gates a bit easier to clip and bent gates the easiest for the rope; for sport climbing he recommends a nose with no notch, and mentions that some wiregates add a hood over the notch so it doesn't hook the bolt (see the video section).

Something you can do tonight: at the gym, borrow draws with each gate type, hang them at waist height, and clip each ten times with each hand. The one you fumble less is your gate.

### 3. The sling: length and width

OGL's view:

- **Length**: most draws come in 12 cm or 18 cm. Short ones are more common; long ones are more versatile and let you clip from lower down, but cost more and hang lower on your harness.
- **Width**: thin slings are light and suit multi-pitch; wide slings are easy to grab, which matters for sport climbing, and the thinnest are almost impossible to hold.

Mike Vert adds a point OGL doesn't: thin slings spin when you clip, which makes them harder to clip, and a stiff join between the rope-end carabiner and the sling makes clipping easier. JB Mountain Skills uses the 17 cm Petzl Spirit most for sport.

Something you can do tonight: next time you're pumped at the gym, grab a quickdraw sling to rest and notice whether it cuts into your fingers. If it does, that width isn't your sport draw. Be careful when you grab: JB says someone he knows slipped while grabbing a quickdraw and tore their hand open, leaving a big scar.

### 4. Certification and where you buy

[Treeline Review](https://treelinereview.com/gearreviews/best-climbing-quickdraws) reminds you: carabiners should carry the UIAA 121 mark, and slings should meet UIAA 104 and be labelled EN 566. There are reports of counterfeit carabiners bought online, so buy from reputable sources.

After you buy, keep an eye on recalls. The SIMOND recall above is one example: only specific models of one brand's draws are affected, and you only know by matching the model number.

Something you can do tonight: read the markings on your carabiner spine and find UIAA or EN 12275 and the strength numbers. Usually there are three, for major axis, minor axis and gate open; the UIAA sets no minor-axis requirement for Type T, so a Type T carabiner may not show that number. Then write down your purchase record (brand, model, date) so you can match it against recall notices later.

## Choosing by situation

### Moving from gym lead to your first outdoor trip

- Gym lead routes are mostly pre-hung, so your first set is really for outdoors.
- On a budget: Hotforge Hybrid or Session, 12 of them. Solid-over-wire or solid at both ends both work; go by how they felt when you test-clipped at the gym.
- With more budget: Spirit Express or Djinn Axess.

### Outdoor sport, starting to project

- Choose the main set as above, mixing in some 17 cm draws to cut rope drag on wandering routes.
- Add 2–3 Bulletproofs for the first bolt and the spot where you keep falling. Evening Sends also says they suit long-term projects on popular routes.
- How many: Andrew Bisharat of Evening Sends (2024) says 12 is often cited as the minimum but suggests at least 24, so one set can stay on a project and you still have enough to warm up.
- If you hang on draws a lot while projecting, go for a wide sling. OGL and JB Mountain Skills both say wide slings are easier to grab.

### Multi-pitch and trad

- If you only sport climb, choose your first set as above and skip the lightweight draws; when you actually start multi-pitch or trad, add Ange Finesse, Litewire, Phantom Wire or Astro.
- If you'll do both sport and trad from the start: JB Mountain Skills suggests one lightweight set like the Phantom for both, and a sport set later if you really get into it.
- On wandering routes, Mike Vert and Aspiring both suggest carrying a few long draws to cut rope drag.

## Inspection and retirement

- **Grooves on the rope end**: the rope running over the lower carabiner wears a groove into it, and the groove's edge can turn sharp. OGL says it's most common on the first draws of a route, and faster in sandstone areas or with a dirty rope. [Wild Country](https://wildcountry.com/en-us/carabiners-beta-talks)'s standard: a groove without sharp edges is still usable; replace the carabiner once more than 20% of the material thickness is worn away.
- **It has cut a rope**: an [AAC accident report](https://publications.americanalpineclub.org/articles/13201106501/) records one at Red River Gorge: the lower carabiner of the draw on the first bolt completely severed the rope, and the climber hit the ground with head injuries. Muir Valley afterwards replaced its aluminium fixed draws with stainless steel ones.
- **Never use the bolt end for the rope**: as in criterion 1, burrs damage the rope.
- **After swapping a carabiner, check it goes through the sling**: some Mammut draws have an orange marker on the sling; seeing it means the carabiner passes through both the sling and the keeper. Clipped through the rubber keeper only, it would pull off in a fall ([Coast Mountain Collective, 2024](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Ydo87doRpaU&t=24s), a gear-shop channel).
- **Slings**: replace frayed, sun-bleached or stiff slings. OGL says bleached, stiff nylon dogbones are especially weak.

How deep is too deep? Hard Is Easy tested it with a drop tower in Mammut's lab in 2026.

- From 12:45, the carabiner itself: the most extreme groove they made still held 25.4 kN (rated 26 kN). Their conclusion is that the grooves you usually see on quickdraws aren't a problem for the carabiner's strength.
- The problem is the rope. On an artificially worn groove with a small edge, the sheath was worn through to the core after 33 drops; a new carabiner, and a carabiner one of them had used for three or four years with a rounder groove, both lasted 57.
- From 21:24: that edge can barely be felt, yet it shortens rope life significantly; and even when perfectly smooth, a groove that shrinks the radius the rope bends around also shortens rope life.
- From 23:30: a flat-worn carabiner from the first bolt of a route, whose edge didn't feel very sharp, cut the sheath open in 6 drops.

The description credits Mammut with dedicating time and resources to the tests.

## Practice: four scenarios

Answers are under each question. Think first, then look.

**1. You lend your draws to a friend for a route. When they give them back, a few have the bolt end and rope end reversed, with the rope-end keeper moved to the other end.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Put them back, and make sure the carabiner that used to go on bolts goes back to the bolt end. The bolt end picks up burrs that damage the rope. Wild Country says to tell people which end is which when you lend draws. If you can't tell which carabiner was which, the cautious move is to stop using that draw for leading.

</details>

**2. You bought a set of SIMOND draws at Decathlon in 2023 and have climbed on them for two years without a problem.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Check the model number first; the BSMI recall notice lists five: 8589547, 8589548, 8608405, 8935765, 8935909. A 2023 purchase in Taiwan falls within the notice's sales period. If the model matches, check both sides of the wiregate with your thumb as the notice describes; if it's affected, stop using it right away and return it to a Decathlon store for a refund. "Two years without a problem" doesn't mean the rivet is sound.

</details>

**3. You're buying your first set. Test-clipping at the gym, you keep missing with wiregates, and bent solid gates feel smoother. But online you read that wiregates are lighter and flutter less.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Buy solid gates. OGL, the MEC staffer and Mike Vert all say gate choice is largely personal. Clipping smoothly on lead matters more than saving a few grams. Choose keylock at both ends, such as the Session, Spirit Express or Djinn Axess.

</details>

**4. You're working a project and your own draw has been hanging on the first bolt the whole time. Cleaning it today, you run a finger inside the rope-end carabiner and feel a groove with a slightly scratchy edge.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Retire it. Wild Country says a groove without sharp edges is usable, but a sharp edge isn't; in Hard Is Easy's test, a carabiner from a first bolt whose edge didn't feel very sharp cut the sheath open in 6 drops. From now on, put a draw with a steel insert on the rope end, such as the Edelrid Bulletproof, on the first bolt.

</details>

Limits: most tests are slow pulls on a home-built rig with one to three samples, and Ryan himself calls it entertainment, not science; the channel is backed by an online gear shop, and some tested items are sold there.
- **[Hard Is Easy](https://www.youtube.com/@HardIsEasy)**: run solo by a climber who signs as Ben, a former software developer. After watching a hard catch slam a climber into the rock in Tonsai, Thailand, he designed his first experiment; the channel now focuses on experiments and physics explanations about belaying, ropes, carabiners and belay devices, with several recent episodes filmed in Mammut's lab. Limits: most experiments are his own design, not EN/UIAA standard tests.
