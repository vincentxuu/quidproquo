You've been climbing in the gym for a while, borrowing a friend's rope or using the gym's. Now you want your own. A retailer's site shows one brand in several diameters and several lengths, some labelled dry and some not, and prices that can differ by a factor of two.

This is post 8 of the Climbing Gear series, and it answers one question: which rope to buy first. The reader we have in mind is a Taiwanese climber who leads in the gym and sometimes goes to single-pitch crags like Longdong. **It doesn't cover how to belay**; that's in [gym top-rope belaying](/posts/climbing/2026-10-17-gym-top-rope-belay-en) and [gym lead climbing](/posts/climbing/2026-10-17-gym-lead-climbing-en). For taking a rope abroad, or whether to buy 80 m or a thin rope, see [Choosing a Rope for Climbing Trips](/posts/climbing/2026-10-23-climbing-travel-rope-en). By the end you should be able to read a rope-end label, decide on diameter, length and dry treatment, and pick one or two ropes from the table below.

## Six criteria

### 1. Rope type: read the 1, ½ or twin symbol on the rope end

[The UIAA 101/EN 892 pictorial](https://www.theuiaa.org/documents/safety-standards/Pictorial_UIAA101%20Dynamic%20Ropes.pdf) requires a dynamic rope to be marked with its type (EN 892 requires durable bands; UIAA 101 also allows marking printed directly on the rope):

- A **1** in a circle: single rope.
- A **½** in a circle: half rope.
- Two linked circles (often written ∞; the label may also say TWIN): twin rope.

The label also gives length, diameter, year of manufacture and manufacturer.

The three types are three different systems. From [Black Diamond's rope guide](https://blackdiamondequipment.com/blogs/stories/how-to-choose-a-climbing-rope):

- **Single**: the most common; one rope is enough, used in gyms, at sport crags and on multi-pitch. BD says singles run from about 8.8 to 10.2 mm.
- **Half and twin**: used two at a time, usually thinner (BD says mostly under 8 mm), mostly in the mountains. Twins are clipped together through every piece, functioning more like a single rope.

**Diameters overlap, so millimetres alone won't tell you.** [Mabu Valley's guide to dynamic ropes](https://mabuvalley.com/blogs/戶外豆知識/認識動力繩) (in Chinese) is clear: single, half and twin are different systems whose diameters can overlap, so check the end marking before the diameter. Some thin ropes are certified as both half and twin, like the 7.4 mm rope in BD's guide; either use is fine, but always as a pair.

If you climb only in the gym and at single-pitch sport crags, you want a rope marked **1**.

**Something you can do tonight**: at the gym, look at the end of the rope you're using. Find the circled symbol, the diameter and the year of manufacture.

### 2. Dynamic, not static

[Mabu Valley](https://mabuvalley.com/blogs/戶外豆知識/認識動力繩) explains that in a lead fall, dynamic rope absorbs energy by stretching in a controlled way, lowering the instantaneous load on the whole belay system. Low-stretch rope (commonly called static rope) is for fixing, ascending, descending and rope access, not for catching lead falls. BD agrees: you're not going to lead on a static rope; it's for jugging and hauling.

EpicTV's rope primer makes the same point: static ropes lack the dynamic stretch a fall needs, and are for industrial work or hauling on big walls, not for climbing ([EpicTV, 2024](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OXUFgmMra_8)).

#### Impact force: the more the rope stretches, the less it hurts

Picture falling from above your last quickdraw. The force on you, your belayer and the quickdraw at the instant the rope catches you is the impact force. The more the rope can stretch in that instant, the longer you take to slow down, and the lower the impact force. That's why lead climbing always uses dynamic rope.

Think of it in reverse, too: [DAV's 2010 article on gear lifetime](https://sac-hoherrohn.ch/wp-content/uploads/2019/02/dav_lebensdauer-von-alpinausruestung.pdf) (in German) says a rope's dynamic properties decline as it ages. [Mammut's rope-care page](https://help.mammut.com/en/article/a65324) says a fall above fall factor 1.0 compromises a rope. What fall factor is, and how much force an anchor takes in a fall, is in [building anchors](/posts/climbing/2026-10-17-building-anchors-en).

[The UIAA 101/EN 892 pictorial](https://www.theuiaa.org/documents/safety-standards/Pictorial_UIAA101%20Dynamic%20Ropes.pdf):

| Item | Single | Half | Twin (tested as a pair) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Falling mass | 80 kg | 55 kg | 80 kg |
| Peak force, first drop | ≤ 12 kN | ≤ 8 kN | ≤ 12 kN |
| Minimum drops without breaking | 5 | 5 | 12 |
| Static elongation (80 kg) | ≤ 10% | ≤ 12% | ≤ 10% |
| Dynamic elongation, first drop | ≤ 40% | ≤ 40% | ≤ 40% |

Sheath slippage must also be no more than 20 mm. In the drop test the mass falls freely from 2.3 m above the anchor point, with 2.5 m of rope before the first drop, running over an edge of 5 mm radius that simulates a carabiner. Mammut says ropes are built and tested to withstand up to five falls of factor 1.7, but an older or worn rope may not.

**What it does and doesn't mean**: this is a new rope under severe conditions. Real falls are mostly milder, but your rope isn't new either.

**Something you can do tonight**: look up the rope you're considering and check whether the product page states EN 892 and the rope type. If it doesn't, drop it from your shortlist.

### 3. Diameter: durable or light

BD splits single ropes into three bands:

- **9.5 mm and up**: durable workhorses; BD says they hold up to seasons of use at the crag.
- **Around 9.4 mm**: all-rounders, between light and durable.
- **8.8 mm and under**: high-performance, lightweight ropes for redpoints or when weight counts.

By BD's split, thicker ropes buy durability and thinner ones save weight. Where a first rope should sit between 9.5 and 10 mm is something the videos disagree on:

- **Go thicker**: EpicTV recommends a dedicated gym rope and a dedicated outdoor rope, with the gym one ideally 10 mm or more, such as the Edelrid Python, to stand up to constant falls. Nomads With A Purpose also says 10 mm and up suits beginners who'll top-rope a lot.
- **9.7 and up**: UKClimbing's Beal Booster III review says that at crags with short routes and lots of big falls, thinner ropes wear out fast, while 9.7 and up is much more durable and easier to grip.
- **9.5 to 9.8**: Brant Hysell of The Gravity Lab suggests starting in the 9.5 to 9.8 range. Ben of Hard Is Easy calls 9.5 a good all-rounder but wouldn't go below 9.5 for a first rope, because it needs to last; in the same video, HowNOT2's Ryan prefers 9.8 for longevity.
- **9.5 is enough**: UK climbing instructor JB Mountain Skills says a 9.5 is all you need for a first rope; he finds 9.8 too thick, trading handling for durability.

The evidence for each view is in the video section below.

**Diameter also has to match your belay device.** Thin ropes are harder to grip, thick ones harder to pay out; every device's manual lists compatible diameters, and the GRIGRI, for example, works best on 8.9 to 10.5 mm. Watch the upper limit in particular: the Edelrid Giga Jul only goes to 10 mm, so a 10 mm rope is already at the top. Ranges for each model, and how to read them, are in this series' [belay device](/posts/climbing/2026-10-24-climbing-belay-device-en) post; the locking carabiner that clips the device to your harness is in [carabiners](/posts/climbing/2026-10-24-climbing-carabiners-en).

**Something you can do tonight**: find the manual for your belay device (or your usual partner's), and write down its compatible and optimal diameter ranges. Your candidate rope should fall within the optimal range.

### 4. Length: 60, 70 or 80 m

[BD](https://blackdiamondequipment.com/blogs/stories/how-to-choose-a-climbing-rope):

- **60 m**: mountain routes with long approaches and frequent unroping, or multi-pitch with a tag line.
- **70 m**: BD calls this the standard for cragging and multi-pitch today; many rappels and pitches are set up for it.
- **80 m**: sport crags with long routes; and if you take a lot of falls, you can cut off worn ends and still have plenty of rope.

The gym doesn't need that much, but gyms differ. The Gravity Lab says a standard gym rope is about 45 m, and HowNOT2's Ryan also says to ask your gym what length to get. Outdoors depends on the area: The Gravity Lab suggests 70 m at minimum; Ryan's examples are Spain, where many routes are nearly 40 m tall and you need an 80 m rope to get down, and Yosemite, where he owned nothing but a 60 m for his first 15 years of climbing.

Cutting a rope down is how many climbers actually use it. JB Mountain Skills says ropes usually wear at the ends first; a 60 m becomes a 50 m once trimmed, and eventually a 40 m gym rope.

The most direct consequence of a short rope is the end slipping through the belay device while lowering. [Outdoor belaying](/posts/climbing/2026-10-17-outdoor-belay-en) cites American Alpine Club figures showing over half of lowering accidents in a decade had this cause; [first outdoor sport lead](/posts/climbing/2026-10-17-first-outdoor-sport-lead-en) covers checking route length before you go. Whatever the length, tie a stopper knot in the end.

**Something you can do tonight**: call or message your gym and ask how tall the lead wall is. Then look up the crag you want to visit, find its longest route, and double it. That's the minimum rope length you need.

### 5. "Dry" isn't always water repellent

Many ropes are labelled "dry", meaning water-repellent treatment. BD says dry treatment adds some durability and allows use in icy conditions. But "dry" on the label doesn't mean the rope passed a water-repellency standard.

[The UIAA standard for water-repellent ropes, published in 2014](https://theuiaa.org/home/new-uiaa-standard-developed-for-water-repellant-ropes), works like this: the whole sample is lightly abraded, equivalent to a few days' use, then soaked for 15 minutes following a set procedure. To be labelled "UIAA Water Repellent", it must absorb no more than 5% of its weight in water. For comparison, an untreated rope absorbs around 50% in this test, and ropes labelled "dry" with inadequate treatment absorb 20–40%. The UIAA's announcement quotes rope maker Beal: ropes with surface treatment only can't pass.

So if you need genuine water repellency (ice, alpine), look for "UIAA Water Repellent", not just "dry".

Hard Is Easy ran this test start to finish in Mammut's lab; start at 27:23. The sample is first worked back and forth over bolts to fuzz it up, then water runs over it from above for 15 minutes before it's spun dry and weighed. The UIAA announcement says "soaked", while the lab demonstrates a steady pour with the rope not fully submerged; for the exact procedure, the standard itself is the authority, and the video simply shows what "abrade, then wet" looks like. Ben ends on the same point as this section: a treatment is not the same as passing UIAA Water Repellent. It's one sample, run by Mammut staff.

**So does a first rope need to be dry?** The videos disagree. EpicTV says dry ropes cost more and pay off where you'll meet snow, ice or water; Nomads With A Purpose says single-pitch sport climbing doesn't require it, though it keeps a rope cleaner and extends its life; The Gravity Lab says that unless the price gap is huge, he always buys dry, because it repels water and dust and lasts longer. These are user opinions, not measurements.

**Something you can do tonight**: on your candidate rope's product page, see whether the water-repellency field says "UIAA Water Repellent", "dry cover" (sheath only), or nothing. Those are three price tiers.

### 6. Where to buy: check certification, and be wary of very cheap rope

JB Mountain Skills' view is that a rope from a proper rope maker is safe: he has used Decathlon ropes, which didn't handle as nicely as expensive ones but were still safe; what to avoid is an unknown rope off a marketplace like Temu. Brant of The Gravity Lab recommends sticking to dedicated rope makers, naming Edelrid, Beal and Mammut; that's his personal preference.

In Taiwan, the only shop we confirmed is [Mabu Valley](https://mabuvalley.com), which lists four Beal ropes: Karma 9.8, Booster III 9.7, Zenith 9.5 and Top Gun II 10.5 (checked 2026-10-24). We didn't check Taiwanese retailers for other brands one by one; ask your local shop what it has in stock.

**Something you can do tonight**: if you've seen an unusually cheap "climbing rope" on a marketplace site, look for its EN 892 marking and a photo of the rope end. If you can't find them, skip it.

## Choosing for three situations

### Gym lead only

- Length: ask how tall the walls are and what length the gym recommends. The Gravity Lab says a standard gym rope is about 45 m; the Edelrid Python comes in 30 and 40 m, the Mammut 9.8 Crag Classic in 40 m.
- Diameter: go thicker, 9.8 to 10 mm; EpicTV suggests 10 mm and up. Dry treatment isn't essential; get a rope bag.

### Gym plus single-pitch crags like Longdong

- Length: look up the routes you plan to climb and double the longest. BD calls 70 m today's crag standard; 60 m works too, and becomes a gym rope once it's trimmed.
- Diameter: 9.5 to 9.8 mm. Start with the Mammut 9.8 Crag Classic, 9.5 Crag Classic or Sterling VR9.
- To buy locally in Taiwan: the Beal Booster III 9.7 (stocked by Mabu Valley).

### Seaside, humid or sandy crags

- Choose a treated rope: the Mammut 9.5 Crag Dry and Sterling Velocity XEROS are fully treated; the Booster III treats only the sheath.
- Wash the rope when it gets sandy. Mammut says a clean rope lasts longer and can go in a washing machine; the steps are in its [care instructions](https://help.mammut.com/en/article/a65324).
- For taking a rope abroad, or an 80 m or lighter rope, see [Choosing a Rope for Climbing Trips](/posts/climbing/2026-10-23-climbing-travel-rope-en).

## Inspection and retirement

- **Ends wear first**: JB Mountain Skills says it's nearly always the ends that wear first. Mammut recommends pulling the whole rope through your hand, feeling and looking for cuts, spots much thicker than normal, sections that feel stiff or very soft, discolouring, and exposed core. Damage in the middle means retirement; near the ends, you can cut it off and keep using the rope.
- **Record the length after cutting**: Mammut's method is to measure again, re-mark the middle and write the new length on the tape at the end, so you don't miscalculate the next time you check a route. On chipped ropes like the Beal Booster III, UKClimbing suggests keeping the end with the chip.
- **After a big fall**: Mammut says a fall above fall factor 1.0 compromises a rope.
- **Keep the rope out of the dirt**: HowNOT2 and Hard Is Easy show flaking it onto a rope bag or tarp; when it gets dirty, wash it following Mammut's instructions.

The full inspection and retirement rules are in the last post of this series, [inspecting and retiring climbing gear](/posts/climbing/2026-10-19-climbing-gear-inspection-retirement-en).

## Practice: four scenarios

Answers are under each question. Think first, then look.

**1. You find an 8.9 mm rope marked ½ and with the twin symbol, cheaper than single ropes, and want to lead on it at the crag.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

No. Those markings mean it's a half and twin rope, used as a pair, not as a single rope for leading. For a single rope, look for 1 on the end. Diameters overlap, so check the symbol, not just the millimetres.

</details>

**2. A rope in the shop says "dry", and the staff say it's "fully waterproof, fine for ice climbing."**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

"Dry" doesn't mean it passed the UIAA water-repellent standard. The UIAA says ropes labelled dry but inadequately treated absorb 20–40% water in the test. For genuine water repellency, look for "UIAA Water Repellent" (no more than 5% uptake).

</details>

**3. On a marketplace site you see a "climbing rope" at half the usual price for 60 m, and the product page shows no EN 892 or rope-type marking.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Don't buy it. A dynamic rope should state EN 892 and its rope type; if you can't find them, it doesn't make the shortlist. JB Mountain Skills says ropes from proper rope makers are all safe, and what to avoid is an unknown rope off a marketplace. Buy from a shop whose sourcing you can verify.

</details>

**4. You only lead in the gym, and its walls are 15 m tall. A friend recommends an 80 m, 9.2 mm lightweight rope, "so you can take it abroad later too."**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

By this post's criteria, that rope is wrong on both counts. Ask the gym what length it recommends; a 15 m wall doesn't need 80 m, which is just extra weight to carry in. 9.2 mm is a lightweight rope; Ben of Hard Is Easy says thin ropes don't last and wouldn't go below 9.5 for a first rope, and the gym is where you work routes and fall over and over. Buy a 9.8 to 10 mm gym rope in the length your gym recommends first; when you actually travel, buy a separate one following [Choosing a Rope for Climbing Trips](/posts/climbing/2026-10-23-climbing-travel-rope-en).

</details>
