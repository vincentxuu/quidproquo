Rappelling looks simple: run the rope through the anchor, put it in your device, slide down. The danger is in the details. Miss one strand in the device, skip the end knots, or let the friction hitch ride up into the device, and any one of those can drop you to the ground.

The previous post, [Lower or Rappel?](/posts/climbing/2026-10-17-lower-or-rappel-en), covered *whether* to rappel: what the AAC and Long Dong's TOCC each recommend, and the three most lethal rappelling mistakes. This one covers *how*. First it breaks a rappel into eight steps, from clipping in at the anchor to pulling your ropes down; then it explains seven rappel methods one by one, including where each goes wrong. You need them whether you're cleaning a single pitch or bailing off a [multi-pitch](/posts/climbing/2026-10-17-multi-pitch-basics-en) route.

This is part 8 of the Sport and Trad Climbing series. Read [Lower or Rappel?](/posts/climbing/2026-10-17-lower-or-rappel-en) first. For judging the anchor itself, see [Bolts Fail: How to Judge a Bolt](/posts/climbing/2026-10-17-judging-bolts-en) and [Building Anchors](/posts/climbing/2026-10-17-building-anchors-en).

Up front: **practise every step on the ground or in a gym, in front of an instructor, until you get it right.** Taiwan's multi-pitch courses all cover multi-pitch rappelling; see the course list in [Multi-Pitch Basics](/posts/climbing/2026-10-17-multi-pitch-basics-en). This post explains what each step guards against and how professional bodies do it. It doesn't replace a course.

The main video source is [Charlie Centa's "How to rappel/abseil - safely AND efficiently"](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fMlxmDCie0s) (August 2026, 15 minutes). Centa describes himself as a climbing guide with the [Spanish Mountain Guides Association (AEGM)](https://www.aegm.org/), teaching in Catalonia. He demonstrates a multi-pitch rappel on two joined ropes, starting with why the old method falls short and working all the way through to descending technique. Each step below gives the matching timestamp, then compares it with [Petzl](https://petzl.com/int/en/sport/tech-tips/basic-techniques/rappelling/escalade-grde-voie-rappel_SPO_a77w0000000KypxAAC.html), the [UIAA](https://theuiaa.org/mountaineering/alpine-skills-which-knot-to-use-to-join-two-ropes), the [AAC](https://publications.americanalpineclub.org/articles/13201200200/Know-the-Ropes-Rappelling-Fundamentals-to-Save-Your-Life), the [BMC](https://thebmc.co.uk/en/climb-skills-abseiling), the [German Alpine Club (DAV)](https://alpenverein.de/artikel/klettern-abseilen_c1137d30-6bc6-436a-9c7c-d7660a300a6d) and other professional sources.

## Why rappelling gets a whole post

Rappelling may not be where accidents happen most often, but when they do happen they're often fatal. Rock and Ice's 2020 report [30 Years of Climbing Accident Data](https://rockandice.com/climbing-accidents/30-years-of-climbing-accident-data-an-investigative-report) tagged three decades of AAC accident reports and found 251 rappel errors. 76 of them were fatal, close to 30%. In the author's public dataset, about 20% of all climbing accidents were fatal.

The errors cluster into a few kinds:

- **Rappelling off the rope ends**: no end knots, uneven strands, an inaccurate middle mark.
- **Anchor failure**: old webbing, a single piton. Several people often share one station: two died at Tahquitz Rock in 2022, and when an old piton pulled on North Early Winter Spire in 2025, [three died](https://publications.americanalpineclub.org/articles/13201217683).
- **No backup**: no friction hitch, so letting go means losing control.
- **The device on only one strand, or on the wrong one**: the 2024 death on the Stanley Headwall in Canada is an example. The AAC's [The Prescription—The Stacked Rappel](https://americanalpineclub.org/news/2026/1/20/the-prescriptionthe-stacked-rappel) traces it to what appears to be a device clipped to only one strand.

The same backdrop shows up again and again: it's getting dark, people are tired, they're in a hurry. AAC accident reports are voluntary, so they count incidents, not rates. They're still enough to show that rappelling isn't the rest break after the climb.

**Something to do tonight**: think back to your last rappel and note in your phone which of the eight steps you skipped.

## Which kind of rappel?

First identify which kind you're doing. The eight steps below assume the most common two-strand rappel, Methods 1 and 2; how the other methods differ from them is covered in their own sections further down.

| Method | When it's used | One rappel can go | Device | Most common fatal mistake |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| [1. Single rope, doubled](#method-1-single-rope-doubled) | Cleaning single pitches, short multi-pitch retreats | Half a rope | Tube device | Inaccurate middle, no end knots |
| [2. Two ropes joined](#method-2-two-ropes-joined) | Multi-pitch retreats | One rope length | Tube device | One strand only, long tail clipped as the rope |
| [3. Single rope plus pull cord](#method-3-single-rope-plus-a-thin-pull-cord) | Multi-pitch with one rope | One rope length | Tube or GRIGRI | Knot migration, wrong strand, only cord in hand when stuck |
| [4. Blocked GRIGRI](#method-4-grigri-single-strand-rappel-blocked) | Rappelling on an assisted-braking device | Set by the pull line when blocked | GRIGRI and similar | Device on the unblocked strand |
| [5. Fixed single rope](#method-5-fixed-single-rope-tube-device) | Fixing lines, coming down after top-rope soloing | The fixed length | Tube (high friction) or GRIGRI | Believing the rope is fixed when it isn't |
| [6. Munter and carabiner brake](#method-6-no-device-munter-and-carabiner-brake) | Dropped device | As for two strands | Carabiners only | Rope unscrewing the gate, no backup |
| [7. Figure-8](#method-7-figure-8-descender) | Canyoning, rescue, or an eight is all you have | As for two strands | Figure-8 | Eight levering a carabiner open |

Simul-rappels, tandem rappels and counterbalance rappels, where two people go down together, are rescue or emergency techniques; see [Recognise them, don't improvise them](#recognise-them-dont-improvise-them-simul-rappels-and-rescue-rappels) at the end.

**Something to do tonight**: circle the method you use most in the table and read that section first.

## Gear

| Item | Purpose | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Rappel device | A tube-style belay device that takes two strands | Thin ropes give less friction; see [Belay Devices](/posts/climbing/2026-10-24-climbing-belay-device-en) |
| Tether | Clips you to the anchor when you arrive | An adjustable one (e.g. Petzl CONNECT ADJUST) is easiest; use a locker, not a quickdraw |
| Extension sling | Extends the device in front of you | A 60 cm sling, or a 120 cm sling doubled; nylon |
| Friction-hitch cord | Ties an autoblock as a "third hand" | Soft cord, thinner than the rope, short enough that it can't reach the device |
| 3 locking carabiners | One each for tether, device and friction hitch | Glance at each gate before you weight it |
| Knife and 5–6 mm cord | Back up or replace old tat when bailing off multi-pitch | Optional for single pitch |

On tether material, the AAC's 2012 [Know the Ropes: Rappelling](https://publications.americanalpineclub.org/articles/13201200200/Know-the-Ropes-Rappelling-Fundamentals-to-Save-Your-Life) and the [DAV's 2020 rappelling article](https://alpenverein.de/artikel/klettern-abseilen_c1137d30-6bc6-436a-9c7c-d7660a300a6d) both say nylon, not Dyneema. The AAC's reason is that Dyneema has almost no stretch and breaks at low force when shock-loaded; the DAV adds that it ages faster.

**Something to do tonight**: lay the gear above out on the floor, check it off, and add anything missing to your shopping list.

## Step 1: Clip in first, then inspect the anchor

At the rappel station, the first thing you do is clip yourself to the anchor with your tether and a locking carabiner. [Petzl's rappelling steps](https://petzl.com/int/en/sport/tech-tips/basic-techniques/rappelling/escalade-grde-voie-rappel_SPO_a77w0000000KypxAAC.html) open with exactly this: every manoeuvre at the anchor is done while directly connected to it. Centa recommends an adjustable tether because it makes the load test easier later ([3:26](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fMlxmDCie0s&t=206s)).

Once you're clipped in, inspect the anchor. On rappel your life hangs entirely on it, with no other redundancy. That's the point the DAV article makes.

- **Bolts, chains, rings**: corrosion, wear, and whether quick links are screwed shut. [TOCC's "Top anchors at Long Dong"](https://www.tocc-climbing.org/post/%E9%BE%8D%E6%B4%9E%E7%9A%84%E4%B8%8A%E6%96%B9%E5%9B%BA%E5%AE%9A%E9%BB%9E) (in Chinese) warns that Long Dong is a sea cliff. Aluminium rings that climbers add themselves often lose much of their strength in under a year, and 304 stainless quick links are a serious concern. Report anything abnormal to TOCC's Rebolting Team.
- **Old webbing**: the AAC's 2025 [Know the Ropes: Crafty Rappel Techniques](http://publications.americanalpineclub.org/articles/13201217362) says every rappel anchor is guilty until proven innocent. In the 2022 Tahquitz Rock accident, the station was a single sun-faded sling. It broke, and two people died.
- **A single point**: the [BMC's Alpine skills: 6 tips for multipitch abseiling](https://thebmc.co.uk/en/alpine-skills-multipitch-abseiling-tips) says that if you aren't completely happy, leave a piece of your own gear or replace the in-situ tat.

In this 2024 video, [HowNOT2](https://www.youtube.com/@HowNOT2) pulls old webbing and cord from rappel stations until it breaks. From 3:50 it compares sun-faded slings with ones that still have their colour: the faded webbing broke at about 3 kN, close to the sling in the Tahquitz accident. The conclusion: don't rappel on a single old sling of unknown history. HowNOT2 also runs a gear store, plugged at the end of the video.

## Step 2: Threading, and joining two ropes

**A single rope doubled**: the middle of the rope has to sit exactly at the anchor. Middle marks drift when a rope shrinks or has been cut. If you're unsure, line up both ends to find the true middle; the method is in [Lower or Rappel?](/posts/climbing/2026-10-17-lower-or-rappel-en).

**Joining two ropes**: on multi-pitch routes, two ropes joined together give you a longer rappel. The standard joining knot is the flat overhand: lay the two ropes side by side and tie a single overhand with both. The [UIAA's "Which knot to use to join two ropes"](https://theuiaa.org/mountaineering/alpine-skills-which-knot-to-use-to-join-two-ropes), [Petzl](https://petzl.com/INT/en/Sport/Knot-for-joining-rappel-ropes), [Edelrid](https://edelrid.com/int-en/knowledge/knowledge-base/knots-for-connecting-ropes-when-rappelling-comparison) and the DAV agree on two things:

1. **Tighten all four strands separately**: pull each of the four strands around the knot hard, one at a time. Pulling the two sides together isn't enough.
2. **Leave tails of at least 30 cm**.

Edelrid adds: dress the knot cleanly, with the two ropes parallel and not crossed.

The advantage of the flat overhand is that the knot sits off to one side, so it's less likely to catch in cracks or on edges when you pull the ropes.

### Where sources disagree

| Question | One side | The other side |
|---|---|---|
| Is the flat figure-8 OK? | A Yosemite Search and Rescue member and a Yosemite guide, [writing in Climbing](https://climbing.com/skills/rappelling-best-practices): never use it; it rolls apart under load | [Tests by ENSA](https://climbing.com/skills/how-to-rappel-safely-with-euro-death-knot-offset-figure-8/) (the French national ski and mountaineering school): suitable if tightened, with tails of 30 cm or more |
| Add a second knot? | Petzl: yes when the ropes differ in diameter; the UIAA switches to a double fisherman | [bergundsteigen 2025](https://www.bergundsteigen.com/en/artikel/alpinhacks-abseilen-mit-hilfsleinen-rad-line-co/): one knot is enough, and two in a row snag more easily |
| Centa's choice | A double overhand (both ropes wrapped twice). The alternating colours show at a glance whether it's dressed, and it's less prone to rolling ([5:10](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fMlxmDCie0s&t=310s)) | He doesn't give a tail length; use the 30 cm above |

All of these positions rest on the same premise: the knot is tight and the tails are long enough. In 2026, HowNOT2 tested joining knots with IFMGA guide Ian Nicholson. In the failure scenarios from 7:49, a loose flat figure-8 with short tails rolled apart at under 1 kN, and body weight alone was enough to pull it off the end. The closing advice: cinch each strand and leave at least 12 inches (about 30 cm) of tail. HowNOT2 sells climbing ropes itself, and plugs them at the end.

In the other direction, the Stanley Headwall party used a flat overhand. The AAC report quotes the partner as saying **the joining knot was not the cause**; it appears the device was on one strand. Which knot you pick matters less than tightening it and leaving long enough tails every time.

Tails shouldn't be too long either. [bergundsteigen's "Fehler beim Abseilen: 20 Tipps"](https://bergundsteigen.com/artikel/fehler-beim-abseilen-20-tipps) (in German) records a close call where someone mistook a tail of about 1.5 m for the rope and clipped it into the device.

**Decide which strand you'll pull while you thread**: you pull the side the knot is on. Centa clips his tether carabiner to the strand he'll pull as a reminder ([12:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fMlxmDCie0s&t=720s)); [the DAV's rappelling tips](https://www.alpenverein.de/artikel/abseilen-klettern-tipps_a8187791-6f13-4d2e-a5c6-9d979859eaed) and Petzl do the same.

**Something to do tonight**: practise the flat overhand with two lengths of rope. Tighten all four strands one at a time and measure the tails for 30 cm. Then tie it again pulling only two strands and feel the difference.

## Step 3: Knot the ends, and shout before you throw

[The AAC's 2012 article](https://publications.americanalpineclub.org/articles/13201200200/Know-the-Ropes-Rappelling-Fundamentals-to-Save-Your-Life) lists rappelling off the ends as one of the three main causes. The fix is a **closed system**: a stopper knot in each end, so the device can't slide off. The AAC suggests knotting each strand separately rather than together, so twists can work out; the triple barrel knot is a good choice.

Sources differ on whether to tie them every time:

- **Always**: the AAC, and an [American Alpine Institute guide](https://climbing.com/skills/5-steps-for-safer-rappelling). The latter points out that people rarely think of end knots on single-pitch terrain, yet that's where most people accidentally rappel off an end.
- **It depends**: the DAV says to tie them whenever you aren't 100% sure both strands reach. The BMC's Steve Long says knots catch on spikes, and "there is no set rule".

A knot isn't foolproof either. [The AAC's 2025 article](http://publications.americanalpineclub.org/articles/13201217362) cites HowNOT2's test: about 6 kN pulls a stopper knot through an ATC. Tie it big and leave a tail.

Before you throw:

- **Shout first**: Centa shouts "Rope" so people below can cover their heads ([10:37](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fMlxmDCie0s&t=637s)); the [BMC's How to abseil](https://thebmc.co.uk/en/climb-skills-abseiling) uses "Rope below!".
- **Throw each strand separately**: keep some rope weight in your hand so the throw drags the rest down.
- **In wind or brush, saddlebag instead**: knot the ends, clip them to your harness, coil the rope into bundles at your side and pay it out as you go. The AAC, YOSAR and bergundsteigen all recommend this.

**Never rappel past a snag.** Centa ([11:02](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fMlxmDCie0s&t=662s)) and bergundsteigen both say it: the rest of the rope must never be above you, or you can't reach it and you'll end up climbing back up.

**Something to do tonight**: practise the triple barrel knot, once with each hand, then coil the rope into two bundles and practise saddlebagging.

## Step 4: Friction hitch first, then extend the device

This is the core of Centa's video, and the step where methods differ most.

**The old way**: device straight on the belay loop, friction hitch on a leg loop. Centa names two problems ([2:16](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fMlxmDCie0s&t=136s)). If the friction hitch touches the device it gets pushed along and stops working. And the carabiner ends up sitting on the leg loop's metal buckle.

**The common method now: extend the device, put the friction hitch on the belay loop**:

1. **Friction hitch first**: tie an autoblock on both strands with your cord and clip it to the belay loop. Centa explains the order ([7:27](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fMlxmDCie0s&t=447s)). At a hanging stance there's no ledge, and the weight of the ropes hangs on you. An autoblock can be wrapped straight onto tensioned rope. Once it's on, pull up some slack; the rope weight below loads the autoblock, and you have slack to mount the device without a fight. The DAV recommends the same order, which also tests whether the hitch grips.
2. **Extend the device**: Centa runs a 60 cm sling through the belay loop, brings the two ends together and clips the device carabiner through both ([8:21](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fMlxmDCie0s&t=501s)). A doubled 120 cm sling or an adjustable tether works too.
3. **Put both strands in the device** and lock the carabiner.
4. **Take in the slack** between the device and the anchor, and between the device and the autoblock.

[The Mountaineers wrote up the method taught on the AMGA Single Pitch Instructor course](https://mountaineers.org/blog/extended-rappel-and-updated-belay-techniques), and list what extending buys you. The autoblock and clothing stay clear of the device, and several people can rig at once.

### Where the friction hitch goes: the professionals disagree

| Method | Who teaches it | Reasoning and drawbacks |
|---|---|---|
| Extended device, hitch on the belay loop | Petzl, AMGA-based courses, ACMG/IFMGA guide [Cyril Shokoples](http://rescuedynamics.ca/articles/pdfs/BRAKESRappelAcronym.pdf), the Austrian Alpine Club (ÖAV, [as reported by bergundsteigen in 2008](https://assets.bergundsteigen.com/2021/08/38-41-kurzprusik-ad.pdf)), Centa | Shokoples advises against the leg loop: the backup can fail if you turn sideways or lift your leg too high |
| Device on the belay loop, hitch on a leg loop | The BMC; the DAV's "classic" method | The hitch has to be short, or it rides up into the device |
| DAV: all three work | The DAV adds a "combined" method: extended device, hitch on the leg loop | The DAV's caution on the belay-loop method: in an emergency you instinctively grab the hitch, which stops it gripping |

Wherever it goes, everyone agrees on three things:

- **Keep the hitch short**, so it can't reach the device when loaded. [bergundsteigen's 2008 "Kurzprusik a.D."](https://assets.bergundsteigen.com/2021/08/38-41-kurzprusik-ad.pdf) (in German) documents a training accident: the hitch was too long and clipped to the wrong place. When the trainee leaned back, it slid into the descender, stopped working, and he slid about 20 m.
- **Use soft cord**: the [DAV](https://alpenverein.de/artikel/klettern-abseilen_c1137d30-6bc6-436a-9c7c-d7660a300a6d) considers a stiff 6 mm accessory cord the likely cause of one accident, because it didn't grip.
- **It's only a backup**: the [BMC](https://thebmc.co.uk/en/climb-skills-abseiling) warns that a friction hitch isn't fail-safe and can release if it rubs on something. Keep your hand on the brake strands.

**Something to do tonight**: tie a length of rope to a door handle and rig an autoblock and an extended device in order. Pull it tight and check there's still a fist's width between the two.

## Step 5: Sit on it before you unclip

Your device is rigged, but don't unclip your tether yet. Lengthen the adjustable tether so your weight goes onto the rappel system. Only once the tether goes slack and everything checks out do you unclip it.

This is the **load test**. [The DAV analysed two course accidents](https://alpenverein.de/artikel/klettern-abseilen_c1137d30-6bc6-436a-9c7c-d7660a300a6d): in one, the device was clipped to only one strand; in the other, it was clipped to a gear loop on the harness. Its conclusion was that both would have been caught if the system had been loaded and checked before unclipping. When Centa does his load test on camera, he finds a carabiner that isn't fully locked ([11:28](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fMlxmDCie0s&t=688s)).

HowNOT2's 2024 intro to canyoning covers the same thing in the section from 26:12 headed "WARNING: MUST WATCH". The order: clip in with your tether, rig the device, pull up until the tether is slack, sit back to test, and only then unclip. It's set in a canyon with different gear, but "load first, unclip second" is the same in climbing.

**Then get a partner check.** Shokoples' BRAKES acronym gives one item per letter:

| Letter | Check |
|---|---|
| B (Buckles) | Harness buckles |
| R (Rappel device / Rope) | Device rigged right, both strands through |
| A (Anchor) | The anchor |
| K (Knots) | Every knot in the system: joining knot, end knots |
| E (Ends) | End knots, and whether the rope reaches |
| S (Safety backup) | The friction hitch |
| S (Sharp edges) | Edges the rope runs over |

Shokoples asks you to touch each item and say it out loud. The AAC's 2025 article uses a different version of BRAKES, where B is Belay device, E is Extension and there's only one S. German-speaking countries use the A.B.S. check (anchor, brake, stop). The versions differ but the point is the same: load-test while still clipped in, then add a partner check.

**A stacked rappel** makes the partner check easier. Both climbers rig their devices at the anchor and check each other before descending one at a time; the upper climber's device also locks both strands. The AAC's conclusion on the Stanley Headwall accident: the simplest fix is a partner or function check, and the stacked rappel deserves broader adoption.

**Something to do tonight**: write the seven BRAKES items on your phone wallpaper or near your harness buckle, and say them through before your next rappel.

## Step 6: Descend with the brake hand working and the hitch on standby

Unclip your tether, keep your brake hand on both strands, and start down. As Centa puts it ([13:42](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fMlxmDCie0s&t=822s)), the brake hand controls your speed. The autoblock is only an emergency backup, so on the way down you're trying to keep it from engaging, not letting it grab and releasing it over and over.

On the way down:

- **Smooth, no bouncing**: in [Edelrid's test](https://edelrid.com/int-en/knowledge/knowledge-base/knots-for-connecting-ropes-when-rappelling-comparison), an abrupt stop or sudden drop on rappel spiked the anchor load to about 2.6 kN, roughly three times the rappeller's body weight.
- **Watch the ends**: near the bottom, check both strands are still there and long enough.
- **When you need both hands, tie a backup knot**: Centa demonstrates it from [12:30](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fMlxmDCie0s&t=750s) and later calls it a "catastrophe knot". Tie an overhand on a bight in both strands below the device and clip it to your belay loop with another carabiner. When you're freeing a tangled rope or cleaning gear, that knot stops the device even if the autoblock slips. Untie it before you carry on. YOSAR says the same: never rely on one friction hitch.
- **If you're last and have to clean the anchor**: Centa threads the rope behind the anchor carabiners, on the rock side ([4:36](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fMlxmDCie0s&t=276s)). That way the loaded rope doesn't pinch them, and you can unclip them while sitting on your rappel.

**Something to do tonight**: practise the backup knot at home. Hold the "brake strand" in one hand, and with the other tie an overhand on a bight one-handed and clip a carabiner to it.

## Step 7: At the next station, clip in before you let go of the rope

At the next station the order is: **clip in, then take the rope out of your device.** [The BMC's Mike Margeson](https://thebmc.co.uk/en/alpine-skills-multipitch-abseiling-tips) describes his own close call: he arrived at a station, forgot to clip his cowstail, and took his device off.

[YOSAR members and guides writing in Climbing](https://climbing.com/skills/rappelling-best-practices) go further. The first climber clips in and pulls 5–10 feet of extra rope through the device, but **leaves the device on the rope**. Only when the second climber is down and clipped in do both take their devices off. Petzl's steps have the first climber clip both strands to the anchor on arrival, so the ropes can't be lost and the second climber's descent gets an extra backup.

Once the first climber is clipped in, there are three more jobs:

1. **Test-pull**: the [BMC](http://thebmc.co.uk/en/abseiling-do-you-know-the-risks) advises a short test pull to make sure the rope will run.
2. **Thread the pull end through the next station** and tie a stopper in it. bergundsteigen suggests doing this while the second climber descends, so the ropes can be pulled as soon as they arrive.
3. **Call "Off rappel"**, so the person above knows they can start.

Calls vary between schools. Agreeing on a set with your partner before you climb matters more than which words you use.

### Bottom belay (fireman's belay): can it replace the backup?

From below, the first climber can hold both strands in their hands and pull down if the person above loses control, stopping them. This is the fireman's belay. The AAC's 2012 article argues that only the first person down really needs an autoblock; the rest can rely on a fireman's belay.

The [Swiss Alpine Club's (SAC) official reply](https://www.sac-cas.ch/de/die-alpen/abseilen-mit-oder-ohne-selbstsicherung-15417/) (in German) disagrees. SAC teaching is that you always rappel with your own backup. If the person above has rigged the device wrong, say on a gear loop, no amount of pulling from below will save them. [Petzl](https://petzl.com/US/en/Sport/Rappelling-with-GRIGRI-and-NEOX?ProductName=GRIGRI) adds that if the person above is on a GRIGRI or NEOX, a hard pull from below can actually unlock it; Petzl allows a bottom belay by hand, but not hanging on the rope. So the fireman's belay is an extra layer, not a substitute for the rappeller's own backup.

**Something to do tonight**: agree on a set of station calls with your regular partner, write them down, and run through them before your next climb.

## Step 8: Pulling the ropes

Pull only once both of you are clipped in. Before you pull:

- **Untie every stopper knot**: [the AAC's 2025 article](http://publications.americanalpineclub.org/articles/13201217362) suggests that after the last rappel the first person down unties the stoppers straight away. Make it a habit and you'll never pull the ropes with the knots still in.
- **Make sure you're pulling the right strand**: pull the side the knot is on. Pull the wrong side and the knot jams at the ring.
- **Thread the pull end into the next station, or clip it first**: Margeson calls losing the whole rope down the cliff a schoolboy error.

While pulling:

- **Pull slowly**: the BMC advises a slow pull, and suspicion of spikes directly below the anchor.
- **Speed up and flick out as the end comes through**: [Mark Synnott's advice in Climbing](https://climbing.com/skills/how-to-rappel-safety-techniques), to get the rope away from the wall so it's less likely to snag.
- **Face the wall, head down**: falling rope brings rocks with it.

**If the rope gets stuck**: while you still hold both ends, you can prusik up both strands together, or climb back up the rope. The [BMC warns](http://thebmc.co.uk/en/abseiling-do-you-know-the-risks) **never to prusik up a single strand**, which could pull free at any moment with fatal consequences. This is already [self-rescue](/posts/climbing/2026-10-17-self-rescue-basics-en) territory, so don't try it for the first time at the crag without a course.

**Something to do tonight**: next time before you pull, stop for two seconds and say "knots untied, pulling this strand, that end's clipped".

## Method 1: Single rope, doubled

One rope runs through the anchor, doubled, and both strands go into a tube device. This is the most common rappel on single-pitch terrain, and together with Method 2 it is what the eight steps above assume. [Petzl](https://petzl.com/INT/en/Sport/Multi-pitch-rappelling-with-a-single-rope) puts it simply: if the rappel is no longer than half the rope, a doubled single rope is enough; if it's longer, switch to Method 2 or Method 3.

**When to use it**: coming down after cleaning a sport route, or bailing off multi-pitch where every rappel is under half a rope. Whether to rappel at a sport anchor at all depends on local rules. In an [AMGA video from 2025](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MKfPKYSW7iE&t=104s), Dale Remsberg of the AMGA instructor team says the AMGA and the AAC both recommend lowering off sport anchors. At Long Dong, TOCC asks climbers to rappel whenever possible. The reasoning on both sides is in [Lower or Rappel?](/posts/climbing/2026-10-17-lower-or-rappel-en).

**How it differs from the eight steps**:

- **When you switch from your tie-in to the rings, make sure you can't drop the rope**: AMGA-certified Rock and Alpine Guide Travis Weil clips in with his tether, pulls up a bight, ties it off and clips it to his harness, and only then unties. That way the rope can't fall.
- **Put the middle at the anchor**: pull to the middle mark, or confirm both ends are on the ground. Weil recommends a bi-pattern rope, whose middle doesn't fade the way a mark does. Middle marks drift when a rope shrinks or gets cut. In the [2024 Devils Tower fatality](https://publications.americanalpineclub.org/articles/13201217342) recorded by the AAC, comments on Mountain Project say the climber had cut the rope shorter and apparently had no new middle mark, so the ends came out uneven.
- **Knot both ends**: in [another of his videos, from 0:35](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qkbxXGYfGMk&t=35s), Weil ties barrel knots in both ends and notes they also stop the rope sliding back through the chains.
- **Before you pull, untie the stopper on the end you won't pull**: when you pull one end, the other goes up through the chains, and its knot is the one that jams. With a single rope there's no joining knot to remind you which side to pull, so remember it yourself.

**The most common fatal mistake**: an inaccurate middle and no end knots, so you rappel off the shorter strand.

## Method 2: Two ropes joined

Two ropes are joined with a knot that sits at one side of the anchor, and both strands go into the device. Use it for multi-pitch retreats where a rappel is longer than half a rope. Charlie Centa's video at the top of this post shows exactly this: he arrives tied into two ropes of different colours and joins them to rappel.

**Centa's sequence** (with timestamps):

1. Clip to the anchor with an adjustable tether ([3:26](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fMlxmDCie0s&t=206s)).
2. Untie and thread the **leader's** rope ends first ([3:45](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fMlxmDCie0s&t=225s)). The seconder's ends are on top of the pile, so the ropes throw cleanly instead of tangling.
3. Run the rope **behind** the anchor carabiners, so the last person can unclip them while sitting on the rappel ([4:36](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fMlxmDCie0s&t=276s)).
4. Join the ropes with a double overhand, check the colours alternate, and pull the knot up to the anchor ([5:10](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fMlxmDCie0s&t=310s)).
5. Autoblock first, then extend the device (Step 4).
6. Stopper knots in both ends, shout "Rope", throw each strand separately ([9:58](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fMlxmDCie0s&t=598s)).
7. Load-test, then clip the tether carabiner to the strand you'll pull as a reminder (Step 5).

**How it differs from Method 1**:

- **The joining knot**: tighten all four strands, leave tails of at least 30 cm; the disagreements are in Step 2.
- **Pull the side the knot is on**: pull the wrong side and the knot gets dragged into the ring and jams.
- **Ropes of different diameters**: Petzl adds a stopper knot to the flat overhand, and the UIAA switches to a double fisherman. When the difference is large, see knot migration under Method 3.

**The most common fatal mistakes**: the device on only one strand, as on the Stanley Headwall; clipping a long tail of the joining knot into the device instead of the rope.

## Method 3: Single rope plus a thin pull cord

When you carry one rope but need to rappel more than half its length, you add a 5–6 mm cord to it and use the cord to pull the rope down. It saves weight, but there are several schools of thought, and in 2026 the method was involved in a fatal accident.

**Block school: rappel on the rope only**. From [Petzl's Multi-pitch rappelling with a single rope](https://petzl.com/INT/en/Sport/Multi-pitch-rappelling-with-a-single-rope):

1. Run the rope through the ring and tie a knot bigger than the ring on one side, blocking it.
2. Clip a locking carabiner from the knot back to the loaded strand. Petzl says always: even if the block seems secure, the knot can pass through the ring.
3. Attach the cord on the blocked side. It is only for pulling; **never rappel on the cord**.
4. Put your device only on the strand opposite the knot. With a REVERSO, run the cord through the second slot so you keep it with you.

Petzl lists the drawbacks too. The knot and carabiner snag more easily. If the rope sticks out of reach, all you hold is the cord, which can't belay you back up. And the whole rope has to be rethreaded every rappel, so it's slow for a long series. [Alpine Team NZ taught the same in 2013](https://alpineteam.co.nz/post/abseiling-with-a-single-rope-plus-tag-line), on the grounds that 6 mm cord is too thin to rappel on directly.

**Two-strand school: both strands in the tube**. Mountain guide Chris Semmel's method in [bergundsteigen](https://www.bergundsteigen.com/en/artikel/alpinhacks-abseilen-mit-hilfsleinen-rad-line-co/):

1. Join the rope and cord with one flat overhand, 30 cm tails; never tie two in a row.
2. **Put the knot on the cord side, against the ring**.
3. Put both strands in the tube device and add a second carabiner below it for friction.
4. Pull the cord; the thick rope falls on its own and snags less.

[Beal's 2026 Back Up Line instructions](https://www.beal-planet.com/cdn/shop/files/BEAL_-_XNOTICE.BACK_UP_202607_-_Notice_livret_100x100.pdf) also allow rappelling on a single rope and the 5.5 mm Back Up Line together, with Beal's AIRFORCE 3 device and a 5 mm Machard friction hitch.

**Why the knot goes on the cord side: knot migration**. The cord has less friction in the device than the rope, so it runs faster on the way down. The joining knot gets dragged away from the ring and the cord's end gets shorter and shorter. [Climbing reported in September 2026](https://climbing.com/videos/death-of-27-year-old-climber-exposes-danger-of-knot-migration-during-rappel) on a fatal accident in Norway that July: both climbers rappelled on both strands with ATCs, the knot migrated, the cord's end went through a device, and the climber fell to their death. The report notes that with the middle on the thick rope, the anchor would likely have blocked the knot.

**Pulling the thick rope is a minority approach**. In [his video from 0:37](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lC1PIOGWsZM&t=37s), IFMGA guide Kim Ladiges of the New Zealand Alpine Team says that when the pull will be hard he pulls the dynamic rope, so that if something goes wrong he's holding the rope that can fix it. That only works if the second climber pre-rigs a device on both strands, and the first climber keeps their device on the rope at the bottom or ties the ends to the anchor. In the same Climbing report, British IFMGA guide Calum Muskett called putting the middle on the cord and pulling the dynamic rope inherently unsafe, and advises always fixing the dynamic rope and retrieving with the cord.

**A stuck rope costs more**: in a [2026 Pingora incident reported by Climbing](https://climbing.com/news/a-climber-and-his-rescuer-share-takeaways-after-getting-benighted-on-pingora-peak), a climber pulled his rope with a 4 mm cord and the rope stuck. All he had in hand was static cord he couldn't lead back up on, and he spent the night on the wall waiting for rescue.

In this 2026 video, professional alpinist Colin Haley explains how knot migration happens from 2:52 and says from 3:53 that he almost always pulls the cord. From 4:58 he shows his hybrid: a clove hitch and locking carabiner on the thick rope to block it at the ring; his video description adds that he still puts both strands in the device. He ties two joining knots, which bergundsteigen explicitly rejects. Haley is not a certified guide; his website lists Petzl as a main sponsor, the cord in the video is Petzl's, and the video doesn't disclose this.

## Method 4: GRIGRI single-strand rappel (blocked)

Assisted-braking devices like the GRIGRI can only rappel a single strand. [Petzl's Rappelling with GRIGRI and NEOX](https://petzl.com/US/en/Sport/Rappelling-with-GRIGRI-and-NEOX?ProductName=GRIGRI) is clear: you can rappel on a retrievable system with one strand blocked, or on a fixed rope. [Its single-rope multi-pitch tip](https://petzl.com/INT/en/Sport/Multi-pitch-rappelling-with-a-single-rope) adds that a GRIGRI can't go down two strands like a tube device. The upside of a single strand is that one rappel can be longer than half a rope; the downside is that the wrong strand means an immediate fall.

**Steps** (from Petzl's tech tips and the November 2024 GRIGRI instructions):

1. Run the rope through the ring and tie the blocking knot in **the strand you won't rappel on**, bigger than the ring.
2. Clip a locking carabiner from the knot back to the rappel strand, so that if the knot pulls through the ring, the carabiner still holds the rope.
3. Past half a rope length, add a pull cord (Method 3), and knot both ends.
4. Put the GRIGRI only on **the strand opposite the blocking knot**, the one that is blocked and fixed. Check the direction against the markings; installed backwards, it won't brake. Clip the pull line to your belay loop with a quickdraw.
5. Still on your tether, sit your full weight onto the GRIGRI and confirm it locks and is on the right strand before unclipping.
6. On the way down, **keep your brake hand on the brake strand** and pull the handle gradually with the other. In Petzl's words, the handle helps adjust the friction, but the descent is controlled by the hand gripping the brake side. To free your hands, tie a knot in the brake strand close to the device.
7. At the bottom, pull the other strand to retrieve the rope.

The GRIGRI takes ropes from 8.5 to 11 mm; Petzl warns that ropes under 8.9 mm are harder to control on rappel.

**The most common fatal mistake: the wrong strand, or a strand that was never blocked**. The 2026 edition of the AAC's accident reports includes three cases, of which Red Rock was fatal:

- **[Red Rock fatality (2025)](https://publications.americanalpineclub.org/articles/13201217659)**: her GRIGRI was on the pull strand. When she weighted it, the rope ran straight through the anchor and she fell past her partner. It was after sunset, cold, and the party was in a hurry. The AAC's lesson: when conditions deteriorate and pressure mounts, slow down.
- **[Psycho Tower near miss (2025)](https://publications.americanalpineclub.org/articles/13201217645)**: in his own account, the climber loaded the pull strand into his GRIGRI+, gave it a tug, felt resistance and unclipped. He toppled backward as soon as he leaned back, and stopped himself only by grabbing the hanging tail. His lesson: a tug is not a test; sit your full weight on it. Waist-high anchors over a good ledge are where people skip it.
- **[Palisades accident (2024)](https://publications.americanalpineclub.org/articles/13201217622)**: a child of about 11 rappelled on one strand with an ATC, and that strand wasn't fixed at all. The fireman's belay below couldn't stop the whole rope pulling through the anchor. The AAC says a single knot clipped directly to the anchor would have prevented it.

Petzl adds two warnings: a strong pull on the brake strand from below can unlock a GRIGRI, and a GRIGRI pre-rigged on the rope while waiting can be flipped and unlocked by the weight of someone rappelling below.

## Method 5: Fixed single rope (tube device)

One end of the rope is tied off at the anchor and you rappel that single strand; the rope stays. You'll meet it when fixing a line, getting down to the start of a sea cliff, or coming down after top-rope soloing.

**What manufacturers say**: none of the three forbids single-strand rappelling on a tube device, but each asks for more friction or a backup.

- [Black Diamond's ATC-Guide instructions](https://s3.us-west-2.amazonaws.com/catsy.865/M10798_D_ATC-Guide_IS-WEB.pdf): recommends high-friction mode when rappelling a single line.
- [Petzl](https://petzl.com/INT/en/Sport/Multi-pitch-rappelling-with-a-single-rope): the REVERSO can rappel a single strand with a backup (a backup device or friction hitch).
- [Edelrid's GIGA JUL instructions](https://avs.edelrid.com/images/attribut/5XXXX_GAL_GIGAJUL_ANSICHT.pdf): nothing specific on single strands; for any rappel, knot the rope ends and back the device up with a friction hitch.

**Friction keeps dropping**: a single strand already gives less friction than two. The [1986 Sedona accident](https://publications.americanalpineclub.org/articles/13198703202) in the AAC's records points out that the shorter the rope hanging below you, the less friction you get: too tight at the start, too fast near the bottom. Ways to add friction, from weakest to strongest: a second carabiner alongside the first, a carabiner in the device's guide hole pinning it back, or a Z of rope below the device clipped back up. Try them all on the ground first; too much and the device locks up.

**The most common fatal mistake: thinking the rope is fixed when it isn't**:

- [Auburn fatality (2022)](https://publications.americanalpineclub.org/articles/13201216604): switching from top-rope soloing to rappelling, she went down on one strand after the rope had been untied from the anchor; witnesses believe she may have thought it was still tied.
- [Vermont accident (2023)](https://publications.americanalpineclub.org/articles/13201217172): the device was clipped to the long tail of the fixed line's figure-8, and the climber slid about 30 m.
- [Red River Gorge accident (2019)](https://publications.americanalpineclub.org/articles/13201215612): the device was set up on its low-friction side, and the autoblock engaged but was partially disengaged, possibly gripped by the brake hand. The AAC's conclusion: a single strand needs more friction and more care with backups than two.

From 1:37 he warns not to clip the Z to your leg loop, because it fails if you flip over. ## Method 6: No device: Munter and carabiner brake

If you drop or forget your device, there are two options: a Munter hitch, or a brake built from carabiners. Both are backups, not everyday rappel methods.

### Munter rappel

- **Carabiner**: a large pear-shaped (HMS) locker **clipped straight to the belay loop, not extended**. [The Mountaineers' write-up of the AMGA Single Pitch Instructor method](https://mountaineers.org/blog/extended-rappel-and-updated-belay-techniques) says the extended rappel isn't meant for a Munter.
- **Brake strand on the spine side**: on the gate side, the rope rubs the sleeve and can unscrew it.
- **Brake hand up for maximum friction**: a Munter brakes hardest with the strands parallel, the opposite of an ATC. With the brake strand down at your hip it loses about 25% of its friction, which is usually still enough ([Alpine Savvy](https://alpinesavvy.com/bonus-articles/munter-hitch-brake-strand-does-it-need-to-be-parallel)). [HowNOT2 points out the difference at 4:02 in a 2021 video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BshgXXn4UEk&t=242s).
- **Always add a friction-hitch backup**.
- **It twists the rope**: twisted ropes can jam at the anchor when you pull them. [bergundsteigen (2022)](https://www.bergundsteigen.com/artikel/alpinhacks-abseilgeraet-runtergefallen-3-0/) describes a no-twist version from the Swiss Alpine Club's manual and recommends it for courses.

**Accident**: in a [2024 Ouray accident recorded by the AAC](https://publications.americanalpineclub.org/articles/13201217271), the climber chose not to use a prusik and relied on a fireman's belay from his partner below. As he turned to look down, the Munter crept toward the gate, the rope rolled over it, unscrewed the sleeve and came out of the carabiner. He fell about 24 m and stopped only because he never let go of the brake strand. His conclusion: next time he rappels on a Munter, he'll use a twin-gate or auto-locking carabiner. [Petzl](https://petzl.com/US/en/Sport/Examples-of-dangerous-carabiner-loading-) also notes that rope or rock rubbing on a sleeve can unlock it and push the gate open.

From 13:45 in this 2024 JB Mountain Skills video, Jez rappels on a Munter on both strands with a prusik on the brake strand. He thinks the twisting isn't as bad as the internet makes out, but for several rappels in a row he'd rather use a carabiner brake. Jez is a qualified climbing and mountaineering instructor in North Wales, and his website credits the gear shop Outside as a supporter.

```youtube

title: Munter Hitch / Italian Hitch (JB Mountain Skills, 2024)
start: 825
```

For comparison, see [Short Guys Beta Works' 2026 Munter deep dive from 11:14](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hbJ1kS0avX4&t=674s), on why not to extend it and why a brake strand on the gate side can unscrew it.

### Carabiner brake

A brake from three locking carabiners (following [Alpine Savvy](https://alpinesavvy.com/blog/a-slick-carabiner-rappel-brake) and [bergundsteigen, 2021](https://www.bergundsteigen.com/artikel/alpinhack-abseilen-ohne-abseilgeraet/)):

1. Clip a locking carabiner to your extension.
2. Clip a second locking carabiner to the first.
3. Pass a bight of both strands through the second carabiner.
4. Clip a third locking carabiner into that bight and across the strands running up to the anchor, as a brake bar.
5. Lock everything and add a friction-hitch backup.

It twists the rope far less than a Munter, but has three risks. bergundsteigen warns that when the system is unweighted, for example when you step onto a ledge, the carabiners can shift and turn, and braking drops sharply. Alpine Savvy says it gives less friction than a device, so it suits less-than-vertical terrain. And it's easy to drop a carabiner when you take it apart.

From 9:27 in this 2024 German-language video, Austria's AlpenAcademy builds the three-carabiner brake, pushing the prusik down as it descends, and says plainly that it's much jerkier than a tube. The description has a patron link and shopping affiliate links. In English, see the [2018 Outdoor Research video with an AMGA instructor from 3:24](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VjWzxRJ9vtk&t=204s); the same video covers the Munter brake-strand side from 2:27.

### Don't: the body rappel

No gear at all: the rope runs around your hips and over your shoulder, and body friction slows you. It was used well into the 1970s. A [1994 SAC article](https://www.sac-cas.ch/de/die-alpen/abseilen-kein-problem-12767/) (in German) calls it tricky and error-prone, because seating and braking depend entirely on the rope's friction around your hips and shoulders. It also wears through clothing and burns skin, and if the brake hand lets go you fall.

In this 2020 video, the presenter opens by saying these methods are severely outdated, painful and not very safe; from 6:11 he says that without gear he'd rather sit tight and wait for rescue. It's here so you recognise the method, not so you learn it.

## Method 7: Figure-8 descender

Before tube devices took over, the figure-8 was the standard descender. Most climbing courses now teach tube devices instead, though canyoning and rescue still use eights. [bergundsteigen wrote in 2012](https://www.bergundsteigen.com/magazinartikel/abgeseilt/) (in German) that its few advantages and many drawbacks have removed it from training and textbooks.

**The biggest risk: levering a carabiner open**. A figure-8 can lie across the carabiner gate and, once loaded, lever a locked carabiner open. The [BMC](https://www.thebmc.co.uk/en/abseiling-do-you-know-the-risks) says this has caused at least one death and a number of serious injuries. A [2000 UIAA analysis](http://theuiaa.org/documents/safety/Karabiner_Breakings_when_using_a_Figure-of-eight.pdf) records a 1996 death in the UK, when a trainee abseiling off a 40 m bridge had the eight lever open his carabiner, and notes that locking sleeves aren't designed to resist that leverage. The fix is to check before every loading that the eight isn't lying across the gate. DMM's [braking-device instructions](https://assets.dmmwales.com/download/73/) recommend a carabiner like its Belay Master that holds the device in position; Pit Schubert of the German Alpine Club's safety research recommended [in 1997](https://www.sac-cas.ch/de/die-alpen/schwachstelle-bei-karabinern-mit-verschluss-sicherung-14092/) two carabiners side by side with opposed gates.

**Other drawbacks**: friction has to be chosen before you start and is hard to add halfway down ([Petzl's HUIT tech tip](https://petzl.com/US/en/Sport/Friction-modes-and-tie-offs-for-the-HUIT?ActivityName=canyoning)); on two strands it twists the rope, twists that reach the eight can jam it, and pulling gets harder.

This 2022 canyoning tutorial shows several ways to rig an eight. From 1:08 comes the traditional climbing setup, followed by adding a carabiner for more friction. The presenter describes himself as a professional canyoning guide in the French Alps; we found no certifying body. HowNOT2's canyoning intro covers the history too: [from 15:05](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JwSJrWQnNrA&t=905s), it explains that canyon descenders grew out of the figure-8 and that an eight can't add friction mid-rappel.

```youtube
title: 6 ways to rappel like a pro using the figure 8 (Sven Van Cleemput, 2022)
start: 68
```

**Something to do tonight**: if you own a figure-8, clip it to a carabiner and deliberately lay it across the gate, so you know what the wrong position looks like at a glance.

## Recognise them, don't improvise them: simul-rappels and rescue rappels

These are all ways for two people to go down together, and the names get mixed up, so first sort them out:

| Name | How it's rigged | Who controls the speed | What it's for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Simul-rappel | Each climber on one end of the rope through the anchor, counterweighting each other | Each controls their own | Saving time; most professional sources advise emergencies only |
| Tandem rappel | Rescuer and casualty hang from one device on extensions | The rescuer only | Getting an injured partner down |
| Counterbalance rappel | The rescuer rappels one strand, with the casualty on the other as a counterweight | The rescuer only | Getting from the belay to the casualty |
| Counterbalance ascent | The rescuer goes up while the casualty on the other end acts as the counterweight | The rescuer | Reaching a leader who has fallen |

Some articles and videos also call the simul-rappel a counterbalance rappel. AMGA guide Ryan Tilley reserves "counterbalance rappel" for one person controlling with a casualty on the other strand, and this post follows his usage.

### Simul-rappel

If one climber unweights the rope or rappels off the end, the other drops. [In the AAC's words](https://americanalpineclub.org/news/2018/11/30/simul-rappelling), the counterweight makes the climbers interdependent: if either loses control, makes a rigging mistake, or either side of the rope is cut, both suffer the consequences. [Brad Gobright died in 2019 at El Potrero Chico in Mexico](https://publications.americanalpineclub.org/articles/13201215647) while simul-rappelling: his end of the rope was too short, had no stopper knot, and slid out. The AAC thinks the accident very likely wouldn't have happened with stopper knots. [Gripped (2020)](https://gripped.com/profiles/simul-rappelling-is-dangerous-go-one-at-a-time/) says to use it only in emergencies, and that two climbers arriving at an anchor together is actually slower. An American Alpine Institute video, [from 3:04](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ricHt4aPJVk&t=184s), mentions that the AMGA's technical director has more or less said not to teach it; the presenter's own advice is don't do it and don't teach it.

This 2018 Outdoor Research video with IFMGA guides Dale Remsberg and Olivia Race is the one embedded in the Gripped article. From 2:13, Race explains that if her partner goes off the end of his rope, she loses her counterweight and they both fall. The whole video also shows how much hassle it takes to simul-rappel safely.

### Tandem rappel, counterbalance rappel and counterbalance ascent

All three are rescue techniques, for when a partner is hurt and has to be brought down. [Climbing's 2023 "Buddy Rappel"](https://www.climbing.com/skills/rappel-with-injured-partner/) warns not to rappel past the next station on a tandem rappel, because you can't climb back up the rope with two people on it. It also says the safest decision in the moment may be to call for help and wait. [bergundsteigen's 2025 guide rescue series](https://www.bergundsteigen.com/artikel/bergfuehrerserie-behelfsmaessige-kameradenrettung/) (in German) recommends practising repeatedly close to the ground on solid anchors (bolts) first. A counterbalance ascent puts the rescuer's and the casualty's weight together on the last piece that held the fall. [Climbing (2019)](https://www.climbing.com/skills/multi-pitch-911-escape-the-belay) advises that if that piece is marginal, consider waiting for help.

## Practice: six scenarios

The answers are under each question; think first, then look. For more, try [nobodyclimb's rope-system practice: rappelling](https://nobodyclimb.cc/games/rope-system/learn/sport-rappel).

**1. You're at a hanging stance with no ledge, and the weight of both ropes is on you. Device first, or friction hitch first?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The friction hitch. An autoblock can be wrapped straight onto tensioned rope; once it's on, pull up some slack and the rope weight sits on the hitch, so you have slack to mount the device calmly. Centa (from 7:27) and the DAV both recommend this order.

</details>

**2. Device and autoblock are rigged, your partner says "looks fine", and you're about to unclip your tether. What's missing?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

A load test. Lengthen your tether so your weight goes onto the rappel system and the tether goes slack, then check each item: both strands through the device, carabiners locked, autoblock gripping and clear of the device. "Looks fine" doesn't count. In both accidents the DAV analysed, the system took weight for the first time only after the tether was unclipped.

</details>

**3. You're first to the next station. As soon as you're clipped in you take the rope out of your device and start threading the next station. What's the problem?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Not necessarily wrong, but it could be safer. YOSAR leaves the device on the rope until the second climber is also down and clipped in; Petzl has you clip both strands to the anchor so the ropes can't be lost. Either way, make sure the rope can't drop down the cliff before your partner arrives.

</details>

**4. The rope jams halfway through the pull. You have one end in your hand; the other is stuck above. You want to put a prusik on your end and climb up.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

No. The BMC is explicit: don't prusik up a single strand, because the stuck end could pull free at any moment. With only one end, the usual answer is to lead back up on it with protection, which is self-rescue and needs a course. To prevent it next time: check which side the knot is on, pull slowly, and flick the rope out as the end comes through.

</details>

**5. You're rappelling on a GRIGRI. The rope runs through the ring with a blocking knot and carabiner on one side. Which strand does the GRIGRI go on?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The strand **opposite** the blocking knot, the one that's blocked and fixed. The other strand is the pull line; load your device on it and the whole rope pulls through the ring the moment you weight it. That is what happened in the 2025 Red Rock fatality recorded by the AAC. Once it's on, sit your full weight on it while still tethered; a tug doesn't count.

</details>

**6. You have a 9.5 mm rope and a 6 mm cord and plan to rappel on both strands with an ATC. Which side does the joining knot go on, and which strand do you pull?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Following bergundsteigen, put the knot on the cord side, against the ring, and pull the cord at the bottom. The cord slides faster through the device; with the knot on the rope side, it gets dragged away from the ring and the cord's end gets shorter until it can come out of the device. That is how the 2026 fatality in Norway happened. Add a second carabiner under the device for the friction the thin cord lacks.

</details>
