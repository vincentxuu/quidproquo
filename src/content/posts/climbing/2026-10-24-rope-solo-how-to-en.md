In March 2026, TAKEMOVIE posted a video of himself climbing *Star Trek* (スター☆トレック), a five-pitch 5.8 at Jōyama on Japan's Izu Peninsula, alone. He explains that because he is using a solo system, after the first pitch he has to go back down to the ground for his pack and then climb back up the rope ([video from 5:43](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=l68_-amCZl4&t=343s)).

This is rope soloing: no belayer, just one person protecting themselves with a rope and devices. [Part 5, Free Solo and Rope Solo](/posts/climbing/2026-10-19-free-solo-rope-solo-en), covered the accident record. This guide covers how it is done: what each of the three approaches looks like, how to choose the gear, where things most often go wrong, and what to check before you start.

It is written for climbers who can already rig a top rope outdoors, rappel and use ascenders. It is an overview, not a course; before you try any tier for the first time, have someone experienced show you in person, and try it first with a separate rope protecting you. The description of [HowNOT2's lead rope solo video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9NxGPzFSHPU) puts it bluntly: the video is there so you understand what you are looking at, because "you don't know what you don't know."

## Choose your tier first

| | Top-rope solo | Single-pitch lead solo | Multipitch solo |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rope | Fixed above the route; it doesn't move | Anchored at the base; you feed it | Fixed at the top of each pitch, then start again |
| Falls | Short; the devices above you lock | Lead falls with no one to soften them | Same as lead, plus far off the ground with no one to help |
| Gear | Two progress capture devices | A self-belay device or a GriGri-style method | The lead kit plus ascending gear |
| Main risks | Single-device failure, rope cut over an edge | Slack from rope feeding back through the device, the ground anchor, inverted falls | All of the above, plus fatigue and darkness |
| Prerequisites | Top-rope rigging, rappelling, ascending | Solid top-rope soloing, leading and placing gear | Solid single-pitch lead soloing, multipitch self-rescue |

Brent Barghahn, founder of Avant, which makes rope solo gear, says top-rope soloing done right is relatively controlled, lead rope soloing is probably ten times more complex and risky, and on multipitch you need emergency communication and someone who knows your plan ([Avant video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lx0iwchriQ0)). That is his estimate, not a statistic, but the order is right: get solid at one tier before moving to the next.

## Rules for all three tiers

These apply to every tier and are not repeated below:

1. **Tell someone where you are and when you'll be back.** [Petzl's self-belay tech tip](https://petzl.com/us/en/sport/tech-tips/expert-techniques/self-belay--solo-climbing-with-one-or-two-fixed-ropes/auto-assurage-2023_SPO_a77Tx00000000KzIAI.html) makes this a rule. If you get hurt alone, nobody will notice.
2. **Two systems.** For top-rope solo that means two devices; for lead solo, a belay device plus backup knots. [The top-rope solo accidents the AAC recorded](/posts/climbing/2026-10-19-free-solo-rope-solo-en) almost all start with "only one."
3. **Sit on it on the ground first.** A device on backwards or a cam locked open shows up as soon as you weight it.
4. **If you want to change your routine today, don't climb.** In all three [AAC top-rope solo cases covered in part 5](/posts/climbing/2026-10-19-free-solo-rope-solo-en), an experienced climber changed their usual setup because of wind, a rush, or a forgotten item. At Donner Summit in 2021, for example, a climber who had used the same system for ten years went from two devices to one because it was windy and he wanted to get home, and free-fell about 50 feet.
5. **Carry tools to get down and to get up.** [Petzl](https://petzl.com/us/en/sport/tech-tips/expert-techniques/self-belay--solo-climbing-with-one-or-two-fixed-ropes/auto-assurage-2023_SPO_a77Tx00000000KzIAI.html) recommends always carrying a descender (such as a GriGri) and an ascender with a foot loop, so you can get out of trouble mid-route.
6. **Wear a helmet.** The climber in the [2021 Donner Summit fall](/posts/climbing/2026-10-19-free-solo-rope-solo-en) wasn't wearing one. See [Choosing Your First Climbing Helmet](/posts/climbing/2026-10-24-climbing-helmet-en).

## Gear: start with what the maker allows

First, the uncomfortable part: **most of the devices in your hand were not designed for it.** [The AAC's February 2025 Prescription column](https://americanalpineclub.org/news/2025/2/11/the-prescription) said only one device on the market at the time, the El Mudo, was designed for top-rope (and lead) soloing. Everything else is borrowed.

| Device | What it is | What the maker says | Watch for |
|---|---|---|---|
| [Petzl GriGri / GriGri+ / NEOX](https://petzl.com/US/en/Sport/Self-belaying-is-not-authorized-for-GRIGRI--GRIGRI-Plus-and-NEOX) | Assisted-braking belay device | Not a self-belay device; Petzl does not authorize modifying it for lead solo | The most common lead-solo method, but an unsupported use |
| Petzl Micro / Nano Traxion | Toothed progress capture pulley | Certain ascenders "may be used by experts" for self-belay; never just one | Cam locked open turns it into a plain pulley; slack in the system can strip the sheath in a fall |
| Petzl Shunt / Rescucender | Rope clamp | "Never use for self-belaying" | Won't lock if your body presses on it or you grab it |
| [Edelrid SPOC](https://avs.edelrid.com/images/attribut/54477_GAL_SPOC_ANSICHT.pdf) | Toothed progress capture pulley | The instructions show top-rope solo but say a single rope clamp "does not provide sufficient safety" | Can cut the rope under dynamic load |
| [CAMP Lift](https://www.camp.it/api/GetSystemResource/210/1175) | Toothless rope clamp | Sold for rope access, tree climbing and rescue; [the manual](https://manualzz.com/doc/82299935/c.a.m.p.-lift-rope-clamp-owner-s-manual) says it is not suitable to arrest falls | Outside the maker's listed uses; some climbers run it as the top device, always with a second device below |
| [Trango Vergo](https://cdn.shopify.com/s/files/1/0282/7557/2841/files/VERGO_INSTRUCTIONS_updated_address.pdf?v=1614357800) | Assisted-braking belay device | Instructions say "NEVER use the VERGO for rope soloing or self-belaying" and forbid modification | Some people lead solo on it anyway, a use the maker explicitly forbids |
| [Climbing Technology RollNLock](https://www.climbingtechnology.com/en/outdoor-en/pulleys-and-ascenders/pulleys-ascenders/rollnlock) | Toothed pulley | States it "cannot be used for self-belaying while climbing" | An explicit "no" from the maker |
| [AlternativeCurrent SULU GO](https://alternativecurrent.it/en/sulu/) | Rope clamp and belay device | Labelled for top-rope solo, certified under the general rope-clamp and belay-device standards | No standard exists for solo use itself; a static rope needs an EN 355 energy absorber |
| [Stonecrag El Mudo](https://stonecrag.com/products/el-mudo) | Purpose-built solo device | Designed for top-rope and lead soloing; use at your own risk | Rope range 9.4–10.5 mm |
| [Roc Amadeus Roc Solo](https://roc-amadeus.fr) | Purpose-built lead-solo belay device | The first CE-certified lead-solo belay device; for lead soloing only | The maker still requires a backup system behind it |
| Silent Partner, Soloist | Older purpose-built solo devices | Discontinued; both manuals require backup knots | Still found second-hand; check the version and condition |

### Building a first kit

- **Top-rope solo:** two progress capture devices, one high and one low, ideally different models; a chest harness; two or three lockers; a descender; a rope protector; and a bag to hang on the tail as a weight. Petzl recommends two different models so you don't make the same mistake twice, such as forgetting to release the cam on two Micro Traxions.
- **Single-pitch lead solo:** a belay device (a purpose-built one, or a GriGri-style method you have discussed with an instructor), a chest harness, steel carabiners or a maillon, gear for the ground anchor, a stick clip and a rope bag.
- **Multipitch solo:** the lead kit plus two ascenders (or one ascender and a progress capture device), aiders or foot loops, a full anchor-building rack and a spare rope. For jugging technique see [How to Use Aid Climbing Gear](/posts/climbing/2026-10-24-aid-climbing-gear-en).

Rope: lead and multipitch soloing always use a dynamic single rope. Top-rope soloing can use a static rope, which handles edge wear better, but check your device's instructions: the SULU GO allows a static rope only with an EN 355 energy absorber, and the Silent Partner forbids static rope. For choosing a dynamic rope, see [Choosing a Rope](/posts/climbing/2026-10-24-climbing-rope-en).

[The American Alpine Institute](https://alpineinstitute.com/articles/toprope-solo-techniques) explains the division of labor: the top device is extended and managed by a chest harness so the rope flows through it, and the extension keeps the two devices from releasing each other; the second device is there for redundancy.

### Rigging the fixed line

Build the anchor itself (two equalized points, edges padded) as for any top rope; see [Setting Up a Top-Rope Anchor](/posts/climbing/2026-10-17-top-rope-anchor-setup-en). The difference is that the rope must be tied off to the anchor, not just run through it: a rope that only runs through will slide out the other side as soon as you load one strand. Three more things change:

- **The rope doesn't move, so an edge keeps working the same spot.** If you swing, the rope saws back and forth. Pad the edge. AAI's approach is to tie a BHK (a doubled loop knot) in the middle of the rope, clip it to the anchor with two opposite-and-opposed lockers and run one device on each strand, so if one strand is damaged you still have the other.
- **Weight the tail.** With nothing pulling the rope down, the devices won't slide up by themselves; you have to keep pulling rope through and your falls get longer. AAI suggests coiling the leftover rope and tying it off a few feet above the ground as a weight; with little rope left, [Josh Wharton](https://climbing.com/skills/how-to-toprope-solo) clips on a water bottle.
- **The first few meters carry a ground-fall risk.** Petzl notes that rope stretches about 10% under load, so on a 50 m fixed line a fall in the first 5 m may reach the ground. Treat the first few meters as unprotected and skip routes with a low crux.

**Petzl's two-rope method, done once for real.** Besides AAI's two-strand setup above, [Petzl's tech tip](https://petzl.com/us/en/sport/tech-tips/expert-techniques/self-belay--solo-climbing-with-one-or-two-fixed-ropes/auto-assurage-2023_SPO_a77Tx00000000KzIAI.html) has a version too: two ropes, one device on each, sharing a single carabiner. [In this video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=l7EwFS4F20Y), about 10 minutes long, Yann Camus follows it and critiques each step. The player starts at 0:26: the rope's midpoint is tied off at the top so each strand is anchored on its own. Then:

- **From 1:38, bumpers on the single carabiner:** with anti-rotation bumpers fitted, the carabiner loads correctly.
- **From 2:19, the device pair:** he uses a Micro Traxion and a Spoc, and has removed the Spoc's cord that holds its cam open. Petzl allows only the Micro or Nano Traxion for this method, so his setup is outside what Petzl covers.
- **From 2:40, the chest harness:** Petzl says a sit harness alone is enough; he adds a chest harness anyway so the rope runs more smoothly.
- **From 4:30, switching to rappel:** he calls this part "a little bit sketchy," because he unclips both devices at the same time and relies on a backup knot tied beforehand to cover the gap.
- **From 5:10, the rope:** use dynamic rope for two strands. Both strands take the fall together, and low-stretch rope makes the catch too hard.

Yann runs Bliss Climbing's solo courses and sells bumpers, and plugs his own products throughout.

### Two devices, ideally different models

The top device gets the most debate. CAMP doesn't list fall arrest as a use for the Lift, yet many climbers use it there: Josh Wharton writes in [Climbing](https://climbing.com/skills/how-to-toprope-solo) that it won't go up the rope if rigged upside down, has no button that locks it open, and its toothless lever is easier on the sheath. The Micro Traxion's risk is that with the cam locked open it is just a pulley.

If the chest harness goes around your neck, use thin cord or a breakaway clip. HowNOT2's [TRS guide](https://hownot2.com/trs-guide) says to make sure whatever you use will break before it strangles you.

**A pro climber's complete top-rope solo setup.** Australian pro climber [Tom O'Halloran's guide](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8vfLCYvZ-q8) runs about 49 minutes. He doesn't sell solo gear, and the description says "this is just how I like my setup. Not a safety course." The player starts at 1:35 with his main device, the TAZ LOV3: it works both up and down, so he can lower himself to rehearse a crux. [TAZ sells it as work-at-height gear](https://taz3d.fr/product/lov3/?lang=en), and [its manual](https://taz3d.fr/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/NT-taz_lov3_fr_eng-231017.pdf) warns against progressing while the device moves downward, so his use is beyond what the maker intends. Then:

- **From 2:24, Micro Traxion and GriGri:** the Micro Traxion works well but can't go down; from 4:14 he advises against a GriGri as the main device, since you spend all day pulling rope by hand.
- **From 9:48, anchors:** several knots; from 13:48, a bad example with a figure eight on each of two points and no load sharing.
- **From 19:00, rappelling in:** first check that the LOV3's safety button is engaged; then re-fix the rope at an anchor on the route, weighting the new system before taking the old one apart.
- **From 30:39, the backup device's extension:** too long and a main-device failure means a long drop that shock-loads a toothed device; he shortened it and keeps the device held in the right orientation.
- **From 35:11, dynamic rope stretch:** on a 70 m dynamic rope he ties a few slip knots down the route so the whole length isn't stretching below him.
- **From 39:22, going down on a Micro Traxion:** he shows the switch to a GriGri, and calls it "safe, it's just a faff."
- **From 44:21, before you go:** practice first on a belayed top rope, and tell someone where you're going and when you'll be back.

The description lists sponsors (The North Face Australia, Black Diamond Australia and others) and discount codes; the neck lanyard and carabiner keeper in the video are Avant products he bought himself.

### A backup knot is the last line, not a second device

Tie a knot below the devices at intervals and clip it back to your harness, so it catches you if every device fails. Keep the rope between the devices and the knot short enough that you wouldn't hit a ledge or the ground sliding down to it. But HowNOT2 [tested this on their drop tower](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DHgozZxLbyo&t=1426s): a Micro Traxion jammed open slid down the rope, then suddenly grabbed, and the 4.85 kN catch was about what strips the sheath of an 8.9 mm rope; with the device taped open so the backup knot caught it, the peak force was still high. Their rule: no slack, and a backup device, because a backup knot may not hold you cleanly.

### Getting down, and routes to avoid

Stop below the anchor; don't climb past it, or a fall becomes factor 2. Rappel the fixed line with a belay device, and stay attached to the rope through every changeover: [Petzl](https://petzl.com/us/en/sport/tech-tips/expert-techniques/self-belay--solo-climbing-with-one-or-two-fixed-ropes/auto-assurage-2023_SPO_a77Tx00000000KzIAI.html) warns that on even slightly overhanging rock, the rope can swing out of reach the moment you let go.

Josh Wharton advises against top-rope soloing steep or traversing routes, where he has bent two ascenders, and against windy days, when the rope blows off and snags.

### The Shunt that left the rope, and why the knots didn't help

In September 2021, Craig Faulhaber was top-rope soloing in Colorado on a Petzl Shunt with backup knots below it. In the fall, the Shunt came cleanly off the rope; the rope and knots were intact, and he broke 12 bones ([Climbing](https://climbing.com/skills/shunt-accidents-while-toprope-soloing)). Petzl removed self-belaying from the Shunt's instructions in 2012, but a retailer's site still showed the old notice.

The lesson: if the device leaves the rope, the knots below it can't save you. Before using second-hand or older gear, read the current instructions on the maker's site.

**For top-rope soloing from rigging to mid-pitch transitions, watch this episode.** This HowNOT2 episode runs about an hour, with Brent Barghahn of Avant as the main guide. The player starts at 10:24 with the real-world setup: he fixes the rope to a two-bolt anchor, rappels, and on the way down places a cam below an edge to re-fix the rope, so the rope's bounce from repeated falls doesn't saw over the edge. After that, these segments are worth watching:

- **From 15:42, the most dangerous moment:** rushing to top out near the anchor, the rope sags into a belly above the devices, and a fall there is factor 1, or even factor 2.
- **From 23:46, the backup-knot drop tests:** the ones cited in the previous section.
- **From 33:03, the CAMP Lift:** many people pair it with a Micro Traxion. The test shows that if the Lift sits below another device, the upper device sliding down knocks it open, so it is no backup at all.
- **From 37:48, Brent's own setup:** Micro Traxion on top, Spoc below. If you pull rope up by hand and then cinch from below, you hold the upper device's cam open; cinch from between the two devices instead.
- **From 49:38, switching to rappel mid-pitch and back:** from 52:04 he warns that when you reach a re-fix knot you should stop, not shove the devices up into it.

### Top-rope solo, step by step

1. Walk to the anchor from above, protected near the edge.
2. Build the anchor, tie the rope off to it, pad the edges.
3. Rappel to the base, noting which edges the rope crosses.
4. Weight the tail.
5. Rig both devices and the chest harness; sit-test on the ground.
6. Climb, moving the backup knot up as you go.
7. Stop below the anchor. Rig your descender and weight it before removing either progress capture device (the order in [Petzl's two-rope method](https://petzl.com/us/en/sport/tech-tips/expert-techniques/self-belay--solo-climbing-with-one-or-two-fixed-ropes/auto-assurage-2023_SPO_a77Tx00000000KzIAI.html)).
8. Rappel to the base and pull the rope.

Every changeover follows the same rule: put the new system on and weight it before taking the old one off. When HowNOT2 shows [switching to rappel mid-route and back](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DHgozZxLbyo&t=2978s), the system is briefly quadruple-redundant; as Brent says, you have to put everything on and take everything off anyway, so you might as well do it in the safest order.

### Top-rope solo pre-climb check

- [ ] Anchor on two equalized points, edges padded, rope reaches the ground
- [ ] Both devices the right way up, cams engaged, each on its own locker at the harness point its instructions specify (never a gear loop)
- [ ] Upper device held up by the chest harness, with a breakaway at the neck
- [ ] Weight on the tail
- [ ] Sat on it on the ground: both devices grab
- [ ] Descender and ascending tools on you
- [ ] Someone knows where you are and when you'll be back

## Tier 2: single-pitch lead solo

### How it differs from top-rope solo

In lead rope soloing, the rope's tail is anchored at the base. You tie into the other end, run the rope through a belay device on your harness, clip protection on the way up and feed out rope as you climb. When you fall, the device locks and you take a lead fall.

Three things change:

1. **A static system catches you.** No belayer hops to soften it, and [Andy Kirkpatrick](https://andy-kirkpatrick.com/blog/view/rope_soloing_101_part_2) says impact forces can be higher. The [Silent Partner manual](https://manualzilla.com/doc/5879704/silent-partner-user-s-manual) adds that climbs tend to feel about one number grade harder solo.
2. **The ground anchor must hold an upward pull.** When you fall, the rope yanks the ground anchor upward.
3. **Slack creeps in by itself.** Once you are high enough, the weight of the rope below can pull rope back through the device (backfeed), building up slack you don't know about.

### The ground anchor

Kirkpatrick says that without an anchor that holds both upward and downward loads, you have no system at all; on trad he aims for three solid pieces in each direction. On sport routes, a common method is an inline anchor: stick-clip the rope into both the first and second bolts, with a steel locker on the first. If the first bolt fails, the knot on the rope jams at the second bolt's connector ([bergundsteigen, 2026](https://bergundsteigen.com/en/artikel/lead-rope-solo-howto), written by Avant's founder).

March 2015, Owens River Gorge, California. A climber was stick-clipping bolt to bolt, hanging from only one bolt at a time. The old bolt snapped and he died in a fall of about 25–30 feet. The AAC concluded that clipping more than one bolt, or a true self-belay from a ground anchor, would have prevented it ([AAC report](http://publications.americanalpineclub.org/articles/13201213409/Fall-On-Rock-Bolt-Failure-Rope-Soloing)).

In Taiwan, most bolts at Longdong have been replaced with 316 stainless or titanium, but a few old 304 bolts remain, mostly in the First Cave. Check the anchor material in the TOCC route database before you go and keep old bolts out of your ground anchor; see [Bolts Fail](/posts/climbing/2026-10-17-judging-bolts-en).

### Choosing the belay device

- **Purpose-built devices:** the discontinued Silent Partner wraps the rope in a clove hitch around a centrifugal clutch that locks when the rope runs fast, whatever position you fall in; Kirkpatrick calls it the best soloing device ever made. Newer options are the El Mudo and France's Roc Solo, which [Climbing's review](https://climbing.com/gear/a-new-rope-solo-device-replaces-the-legendary-silent-partner) says can catch upside-down falls.
- **GriGri-style methods:** the most widely used approach today, but [Petzl states](https://petzl.com/US/en/Sport/Self-belaying-is-not-authorized-for-GRIGRI--GRIGRI-Plus-and-NEOX) the GriGri is not a self-belay device and does not authorize modifying it for better rope feed. Barghahn, who uses this method, writes in [bergundsteigen](https://bergundsteigen.com/en/artikel/lead-rope-solo-howto): "THIS SYSTEM LIKELY WILL NOT CATCH UPSIDE-DOWN FALLS! Always use backup knots." [Roc Solo's founder](https://climbing.com/gear/a-new-rope-solo-device-replaces-the-legendary-silent-partner) designed his device after a GriGri failed to engage and he decked from low on a route.
- **A clove hitch on a locker:** the cheapest and safest, since the knot is always tied; Kirkpatrick says it works well for big-wall aid, but anything faster than a snail's pace becomes a pain, so it doesn't suit free climbing.
- **Don't lead on progress capture devices.** Kirkpatrick says ascenders, including Traxions, can badly damage the rope in a fall. [Edelrid's tests](https://edelrid.com/int-en/knowledge/knowledge-base/impact-dynamic-loads-self-locking-pulley-spoc) show that a toothed pulley taking a factor-1 fall near the anchor can sever the rope completely.

Attach the device with two carabiners, or a steel maillon to avoid cross-loading. In a Half Dome fatality the AAC published in 2006, the GriGri was found rigged for a rappel from the top anchor and never engaged; the AAC could not say whether he rigged it backwards or re-rigged it to rappel before clipping in, but either way the device was no use ([AAC report](https://publications.americanalpineclub.org/articles/13200604200)). Once rigged, sit back on it on the ground.

### Backup knots and slack

The Silent Partner manual gives concrete numbers: no more than 40–50 feet of rope between the device and a backup knot, and at least two backup knots at a time. Kirkpatrick adds: tie and clip the new knot before removing the old one, and calls the backup knot the one component you should never omit.

Take in slack regularly. [Barghahn](https://bergundsteigen.com/en/artikel/lead-rope-solo-howto) suggests cinching the rope every few protection points, or every six meters or so.

**How a manufacturer sees lead rope soloing.** [EDELRID's risk-analysis video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Q6N1Fav4MaI) runs about 22 minutes. It follows a risk-assessment format, ranking from smallest to largest the seven risks that lead rope soloing adds to partnered leading, and for each one covers what can happen, the countermeasures, and how much risk remains. It is all explanation and diagrams, with no actual climbing. Four segments are worth pausing on:

- **From 4:40, preventing backfeed:** clove hitches on intermediate protection, an elastic prusik, a small elastic loop on the carabiner, or a carabiner insert sold by small makers; each has trade-offs.
- **From 6:02, impact forces:** without a belayer being pulled along to soften the catch, falls near the ground anchor are hard. The countermeasure is shock-absorbing slings (screamers) between the rope end and the ground anchor and on the first few pieces.
- **From 10:20, device function:** in the standard, assisted-braking devices are "braking devices with manually assisted locking." Lead rope soloing has no brake hand, and the cache loop falls along with you, so in their tests the whole cache loop was often pulled through before the device locked. The video gives no countermeasure for this one.
- **From 15:58, the ground anchor:** a locker clipped to a bolt hanger tilts when the rope goes slack, and under load again the gate can be crushed. They recommend tying the rope directly into the anchor, or using a quick link with a positioning element.

The conclusion says lead rope solo accidents are rare because few people do it, mostly on easy routes and slowly, not because it is safe.

### Getting down

Barghahn trails extra rope, enough to run through the top anchor, so he can come down without a big transition at the top. In HowNOT2's episode, [the top-out](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9NxGPzFSHPU&t=4645s) goes like this: clip in direct to the anchor with a PAS, descend on that rope cleaning the draws on the way, then pull the rope as usual. Always knot the tail.

In a 2019 accident in Squamish, Canada, a climber demonstrating his GriGri solo system sat back on a chest-high cam; it pulled out because the load came outward, and his lower pieces were too low to stop a ground fall. He landed on his back and broke a vertebra ([AAC report](https://publications.americanalpineclub.org/articles/13201215648)). If one piece pulling means you hit the ground or a ledge, back that piece up.

### Lead solo, step by step

1. Build the ground anchor, confirm it holds an upward pull, and attach the rope.
2. Put the device on your harness, tie the other end of the rope into your harness, and clip in two backup knots.
3. Sit-test on the ground: the device locks.
4. Climb and clip protection; take in slack every few pieces and move the backup knots up as you go.
5. At the top, clip into the anchor with a PAS first.
6. Run the descent rope through the anchor, rig your descender and weight it, then take the lead-solo device off.
7. Descend, cleaning the draws.
8. At the base, strip the ground anchor and pull the rope.

### Lead solo pre-climb check

- [ ] Ground anchor holds an upward pull (sport: first two bolts clipped inline)
- [ ] Other end of the rope tied into your harness
- [ ] Device the right way round, attached with two carabiners or a steel maillon
- [ ] At least two backup knots clipped to the harness
- [ ] Rope bag or pile flaked so it won't snag
- [ ] Sat on it on the ground: the device locks
- [ ] Decided where you'll take in slack and which pieces must be backed up

### Anchors, ropes and ascending

- **Both anchors must hold in both directions.** The lower anchor is your ground anchor while you lead; the upper one is your anchor while you ascend, and the ground anchor for the next pitch.
- **Manage the rope.** [Kirkpatrick](https://andy-kirkpatrick.com/blog/view/rope_soloing_101_part_1) says rope bags are essential for anything longer than one pitch, and [stacked](https://andy-kirkpatrick.com/blog/view/rope_soloing_101_part_2) so the belay end goes in last. [Barghahn](https://bergundsteigen.com/en/artikel/lead-rope-solo-howto) warns to knot any extra tail: it is easy to start rappelling on the wrong strand.
- **The rope takes more abuse than usual.** You climb on it, rappel it and ascend it again; Kirkpatrick carries a 10 mm dynamic rope as a backup in case the lead rope gets destroyed.
- **Keep redundancy while you ascend.** Use two ascenders plus either backup knots or, as in [Avant's multipitch video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lx0iwchriQ0&t=279s), a third device (a progress capture pulley), so taking one off to pass a piece still leaves two; Avant skips knots below because they snag. For jugging details see [How to Use Aid Climbing Gear](/posts/climbing/2026-10-24-aid-climbing-gear-en).
- **Treat traverses differently.** If the pitch traverses, Kirkpatrick rappels a separate free-hanging rope instead, to avoid a swing.

### Multipitch accidents

- **Zodiac, pitch 1, Yosemite, 2017:** a climber aid-soloing on a Silent Partner had a nut blow about 10 feet below the anchor; in all, six placements pulled or failed, and he fell about 110 feet and stopped level with the first bolt. He later noted that because he was soloing he hadn't extended placements with quickdraws, which may have made a cam more likely to pull ([AAC report](https://publications.americanalpineclub.org/articles/13201214846)).
- **The Shield, El Capitan, 1992:** the climber fed out 10–15 feet of slack for a free section, then switched to aid on a marginal piece without taking it back in; the piece pulled and he fell onto a ledge with an open ankle dislocation. His own conclusion: it is much easier to solo at your limit on aid than free ([AAC report](http://publications.americanalpineclub.org/articles/13199305000/Protection-Pulled-Out-Fall-on-Rock-Inadequate-Self-Belay-Slack-in-Rope-California-Yosemite-Valley-El-Capitan)).
- **After Six (5.7), Yosemite, 2021:** a rope soloist took a pendulum fall on pitch two and hurt his neck, needing a rescue. The AAC wrote that extra gear might have made the fall shorter and less severe ([AAC report](https://publications.americanalpineclub.org/articles/13201216225)).

In all three, nobody was there to say "you haven't taken in that slack" or "put another piece in." Multipitch soloing means being that person yourself.

### Multipitch solo, step by step

1. At the base, build the lower anchor (holding both upward and downward pulls) and attach the rope as your ground anchor.
2. Lead the pitch, build the top anchor and clip in with a PAS first.
3. Fix the rope to the top anchor and knot any extra tail.
4. Rappel to the lower anchor, cleaning as you go; where the rope crosses a bulge, re-fix it below to reduce wear ([HowNOT2's lead solo episode from 56:36](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9NxGPzFSHPU&t=3396s)).
5. At the lower anchor, rig and sit-test your ascending system before stripping the anchor.
6. Ascend to the top, keeping two lines of protection all the way.
7. Restack the rope and start the next pitch.

### Multipitch solo pre-climb check

- [ ] Picked a route you've climbed that is well below your limit
- [ ] Every anchor holds both upward and downward pulls
- [ ] Rope bag stacked, tails knotted
- [ ] Ascending system with two lines of protection at all times; changeovers practiced on the ground
- [ ] A retreat plan: where you can rappel off, and whether the rope is long enough
- [ ] Headlamp; turnaround time set before dark
- [ ] Emergency communication; someone knows your route and return time

## Where to start

1. **Practice each tier with a belay backing you up.** For top-rope soloing, have a partner belay you on a second rope alongside, with a little slack so your solo system takes the load first; for lead soloing, rig a separate top rope above you.
2. **Move to the next tier only when the current one is solid.** Do many clean top-rope solo sessions before thinking about lead.
3. **Learn from someone who does it.** This site's [Self-Rescue Basics](/posts/climbing/2026-10-17-self-rescue-basics-en) and [Multi-Pitch Basics](/posts/climbing/2026-10-17-multi-pitch-basics-en) are prerequisites, not a replacement for hands-on teaching.

**Walk through the system at home first.** [Nitant's video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=peg-HXbh0as), about 11 minutes long, uses a small backyard tree as the anchor to simulate a whole single-pitch lead rope solo, from rigging the ground anchor to lowering and stripping it. The description says it is not a tutorial but how he prepares before going out. The player starts at 5:40 with cache loops: the rope next to the device must carry no weight, or the rope hanging below will pull the GriGri into locking; size them so you can drop one loop at each clipping stance. He shows three ways: a slip knot, a clove hitch, and a progress capture pulley with a knot behind it. Other segments:

- **From 0:21, handing the belayer's jobs to the system:** catching the fall, feeding and taking in rope, and a device that reliably locks.
- **From 4:15, rigging the ground anchor:** clipped with a stick clip, and the rule is "no unnecessary, unattended, unaware slack."
- **From 8:18, an upside-down fall:** the GriGri feeds out the whole cache loop and stops only at the backup knot.
- **From 8:37, lowering off:** how to strip a two-bolt inline ground anchor. Clip your PAS to the quick link before unclipping the bolt carabiner, or you'll slide all the way down.

His GriGri+ hangs upside down on his chest, held by thin cord; Petzl allows neither self-belaying nor the modification. Most of the gear is Avant's, and the description has no sponsored links.

## Practice: three scenarios

Answers are below each one; think first, then look.

**1. Your two Micro Traxions are the same model. Before you top-rope solo today, what do you check?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

That both cams are engaged, not locked open. Petzl recommends two different models precisely so you don't make the same mistake twice. Once rigged, sit on the system on the ground; both must grab before you leave. Keep chest-harness cord and other slings away from the teeth: in the [2023 Valkyrie case](/posts/climbing/2026-10-19-free-solo-rope-solo-en), a sling got pulled into the device and jammed it.

</details>

**2. You want to lead solo a sport route. The first bolt is 3 m up and there are no trees or gear placements at the base.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Stick-clip the first two bolts into an inline anchor and use that as the ground anchor, with a steel locker on the first bolt. Don't rely on one bolt: in the 2015 Owens River Gorge accident, the climber was hanging from one old bolt, and when it snapped there was nothing behind it.

</details>

**3. You are on pitch three of a multipitch solo, it is getting dark, and two pitches remain.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Each pitch is covered three times, so two pitches is six pitches of work, and anchor-building goes wrong more easily when you're tired. [Kirkpatrick](https://andy-kirkpatrick.com/blog/view/rope_soloing_101_part_3) says pushing on while exhausted, especially in the dark, is a reliable way to get into trouble. Keep to the turnaround time you set and retreat by rappelling from the fixed rope.

</details>

## Further viewing: gear-testing and instruction channels

- **[HowNOT2](https://www.youtube.com/@HowNOT2)**: run by Ryan Jenks.
