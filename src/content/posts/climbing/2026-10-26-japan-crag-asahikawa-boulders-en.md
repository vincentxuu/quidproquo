Asahikawa Boulders is a regional group centred on Kamuikotan and extending toward Amekon and Shunshunai. Kenji Komoro and Ryozanpaku are key names. The separate Kamuiiwa guide covers roped cliffs, not another copy of these boulders.

## What kind of crag

**旭川ボルダー** is in Hokkaido. CLIMBING-net identifies a bouldering destination on varied rock. [RockTopo’s public list](https://www.rocktopo.net/areas/50/routes) has 86 records; the base tables retain 86 graded entries after excluding ungraded projects and explicitly restricted or destroyed entries. This is database coverage, not a count of routes certified open.

## When to go

CLIMBING-net highlights April–May and October–early November for Kamuikotan; forest sectors generally begin in late May. Summer brings condensation, tall vegetation and biting insects. Snow and thaw seepage affect individual boulders differently. [CLIMBING-net](https://www.climbing-net.com/iwaba_detail/%E6%97%AD%E5%B7%9D%E3%83%9C%E3%83%AB%E3%83%80%E3%83%BC/)

## Getting there

### Public transport

For Kamuikotan, use the bus connections listed by the geopark. Amekon and Shunshunai are separate destinations, not sectors at the same bus stop; arrange transport before planning a multi-sector day. [Official transport source](https://daisetsuzan-kamuymintar-geopark.jp/geosite/kamui-kotan/)

### Driving

Navigate to a named sector rather than to the regional label. Follow the official one-way visitor road at Kamuikotan; check individual forest-road barriers elsewhere.

### Parking

The geopark provides the primary one-way road information for Kamuikotan. Verify visitor parking and forest-sector parking separately, keeping paths, bridges and cultural sites unobstructed. [Primary source](https://daisetsuzan-kamuymintar-geopark.jp/geosite/kamui-kotan/)

## Rules before you go

Some boulders are tall; inspect the landing and descent before placing pads. Top-rope anchors in historical photographs are not a current equipment guarantee. Native Japanese grades are retained below rather than guessed into V grades. [CLIMBING-net](https://www.climbing-net.com/iwaba_detail/%E6%97%AD%E5%B7%9D%E3%83%9C%E3%83%AB%E3%83%80%E3%83%BC/)

For an ambulance or fire emergency call **119**. Give the location, a recognisable landmark, the injury and a callback number; the [Fire and Disaster Management Agency](https://www.fdma.go.jp/publication/portal/items/portal001_pamphiet_english.pdf) explains the call. Save the Japanese sector and route names offline. Ask your insurer specifically about bouldering, search and rescue, medical treatment and repatriation before travelling.

## Routes by level

RockTopo provides the complete public graded list used here; separately readable local additions and cross-source grade differences are included in the same tables. Publisher image topos and paid guide interiors could not be fully read in the available tools, so use the linked local topo for line identification.

YDS and V-grade conversions follow the [Mountain Project international grade comparison chart](https://www.mountainproject.com/international-climbing-grades) and are approximate; "approx." marks grades the sources give without a/b/c (such as 4 or 5+). The original grade is the one that counts.

The source uses Japanese kyu/dan grades. The four groups below retain that system: cross-checked Font/V values are shown only where a separately cited source supplies them. “—” means unavailable, not an estimated conversion.

Each row cites the public route list. Most entries lack length and pitch information; “not listed” cannot determine your rope length, rack or descent. Native names are retained to match a topo on site.
