> 🌏 [中文版](/posts/climbing/2026-11-01-climbing-radios)

You're halfway up a multi-pitch, the wind is howling, and your partner is round the corner. You shout "Off belay" and they may hear only wind; you tug the rope three times and they can't tell a signal from a snag. A radio means you don't have to shout: hold the side button to talk, let go to listen. It's a tool for talking to your partner, not for calling for help; for that, see [Satellite messengers and personal locator beacons](/posts/climbing/2026-11-01-satellite-messengers-plb-en).

This post answers three questions: do you need one, which type you can legally use in Taiwan and abroad, and which to buy. For the calls themselves, see the communication section of [Outdoor belaying](/posts/climbing/2026-10-17-outdoor-belay-en); for the changeover sequence on multi-pitch, see [Multi-pitch basics](/posts/climbing/2026-10-17-multi-pitch-basics-en). By the end you should be able to decide whether to bring radios on a given trip, which band to bring, and pick a pair from the tables below.

## First: does this trip need radios?

Most of the time, voice is enough. AMGA guide Eli Helmuth says that with proper technique you can almost always communicate from up to 60 m away. AMGA training says to put yourself in voice range whenever in doubt: shorten pitches when it's windy or the route turns a corner, and build a belay before you lose sight of each other ([Climbing.com, 2024](https://www.climbing.com/skills/effective-multipitch-communication/)). He also finds the need for a radio rare, and recommends dialling in other communication techniques first.

The [American Alpine Club's (AAC) communication guide](https://americanalpineclub.org/news/2017/1/19/4xm1fcsag6b7xqf1p1w1qp7vdpp1ha) lists the drawbacks: weight, cost, variable clarity and range, and dependence on batteries; lean on radios too much and, when they die, you may be ill-prepared to communicate any other way. It names the contexts where they help: multi-party climbing, expeditionary climbing and complex ski descents.

Rope tugs are no substitute. The same AAC guide notes that if you agree "three tugs = off belay", a stuck rope or rope drag can produce three similar tugs. An AAC accident report describes a ground fall: two climbers had agreed "two tugs = I'm safe", carried that over to a route that was actually a single pitch, and it went wrong. The analysis says that rope movement from clipping, climbing and building an anchor can be misread as an off-belay signal ([ANAC, 2020](https://publications.americanalpineclub.org/articles/13201215599)).

So here's where radios fit:

- **Single pitch, you can see each other**: just use your voice.
- **Multi-pitch, wind, corners, surf drowning out voices**: radios start to earn their place.
- **Long routes, mountains, alpine-style routes abroad**: bring them, but keep your calls and your backup plan.

Something to do tonight: with your partner, stand 30 m apart in a gym or park, back to back, and call "Off belay" / "Belay off" once. If it isn't clear, that's the distance at which you'll need another method outdoors.

## In Taiwan: only NCC-certified FRS is licence-free

### What Taiwan FRS looks like

Taiwan's licence-free radios are called "low-power two-way radios (FRS)". The spec is section 5.5 of the NCC's Low-power Radio-frequency Devices Technical Regulations (LP0002) ([Executive Yuan Gazette, 2020](https://gazette.nat.gov.tw/EG_FileManager/eguploadpub/eg026132/ch06/type1/gov53/num22/images/Eg01.pdf); the 2024 amendment mainly added Wi-Fi 6E):

- Only 14 frequencies: 467.5125 to 467.675 MHz, one every 12.5 kHz; the radio may not display more than 14 channels.
- Effective radiated power of 1 W or less.
- CTCSS/CDCSS squelch codes (privacy codes) are allowed.
- Any antenna or amplifier must be certified together with the radio.

The [Ministry of Digital Affairs' frequency allocation table](https://www-api.moda.gov.tw/File/Get/moda/zh-tw/H7t5vj9X5VatmmF) likewise only notes, in the 460–470 MHz band, that 467.5125–467.6750 MHz is for low-power radios of 1 W or less. Replying to a public comment on a draft amateur-radio regulation, the NCC wrote that "FRS is a licence-free low-power two-way radio" ([Public Policy Participation Platform, 2017](https://join.gov.tw/policies/detail/96ed6ab5-78a7-4ea6-b31d-8277a82aa074)).

When buying, check two things: is there an NCC certification label on the radio or box, and is the power 1 W or less? Shops do sometimes list 2.5 W radios under "licence-free recommendations"; anything over 1 W isn't Taiwan FRS spec.

### US FRS and EU PMR446: bringing them in is fine, pressing PTT is another matter

**Bringing them in**: you can import low-power radio devices for personal use without a permit, up to 5 units per trip when carried yourself, by signing a declaration. [Taipei Customs](https://web.customs.gov.tw/taipei/singlehtml/3130?cntId=cus2_157031_3130) explicitly lists two-way radios; the legal basis is Article 9 of the [Regulations on Manufacture, Import and Declaration of Controlled Telecommunications Radio-Frequency Devices](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawAll.aspx?pcode=K0060146).

**Using them in Taiwan**: Article 52 of the [Telecommunications Management Act](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawAll.aspx?pcode=K0060111) says radio frequencies may only be used once assigned by the regulator and a usage certificate is issued.

Most US FRS channels sit at 462 MHz, and all EU PMR446 channels are at 446 MHz; none of these fall in the band Taiwan assigns to licence-free radios. Read literally, pressing PTT in Taiwan means using a frequency that hasn't been assigned to you, and Article 74 sets penalties for using unassigned frequencies. But Article 52 itself opens with "except as otherwise provided in this Act", and there is no published interpretation or enforcement case showing whether or how that penalty applies to a traveller's personal radio.

But that's the literal reading of the statute. The NCC hasn't published an interpretation addressing US FRS or EU PMR446 radios brought in by travellers, and we didn't find any case of an individual being fined for using one in the mountains. The closest thing the NCC has said concerned unapproved US DECT 6.0 cordless phones in 2010: they interfere with domestic mobile networks, and selling or using them "violates the Telecommunications Act" ([cnyes, reporting the NCC, 2010](https://news.cnyes.com/news/id/2695248); under the old Telecommunications Act).

There's also a grey zone: five US FRS channels (channels 8 to 12) happen to share frequencies with five of Taiwan's FRS channels.

Whether using only those channels is legal has no public NCC answer either, and the radio itself still isn't NCC-certified.

This post's suggestion: in Taiwan, use only NCC-certified FRS radios, and save US and EU radios for trips abroad.

Something to do tonight: look at the label on the back or under the battery cover of any radio you own. An NCC label and 14 channels means Taiwan FRS; 22 channels and FCC, or PMR446 and CE, means US or EU spec.

### Amateur radio: you need a licence

The "hiking recommendations" section of Taiwanese radio shops is often full of 144/430 MHz dual-band radios such as the BaoFeng UV-5R. These are amateur radios: you need to pass the exam for an amateur operator licence and then get a station licence before operating. Under Article 82 of the Telecommunications Management Act, operating without a licence carries a fine of NT$10,000 to 100,000.

Article 34 of the [Regulations Governing Amateur Radio Operators and Stations](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawAll.aspx?pcode=K0060145) designates 145 MHz and 433 MHz as "calling and emergency rescue frequencies". Using them still assumes you're licensed.

Bringing an amateur radio back from abroad also doesn't get the FRS-style five-unit exemption: you need an import permit and must get a station licence within 6 months, or re-export it or have it destroyed under supervision (Article 24 of the same regulations).

Fire and police frequencies aren't open to the public. Don't tune to them.

## Abroad: every country's licence-free band is different

Looking at the four bands, Taiwan FRS is at 467 MHz, EU PMR446 at 446 MHz, US FRS at 462/467 MHz and Japan's tokutei shodenryoku around 421/422/440 MHz, and they don't overlap. No single radio is licence-free legal in all of these places; take a local-spec radio, or borrow from locals.

| Where | Licence-free option | Don't bring |
|---|---|---|
| Taiwan | NCC-certified FRS (467 MHz, 1 W or less) | US FRS/GMRS, EU PMR446, amateur radios without a licence |
| Europe (incl. the Dolomites) | PMR446 (446.0–446.2 MHz, 0.5 W, integral antenna) | US FRS/GMRS, Taiwan FRS |
| US | FRS; GMRS needs an FCC licence | PMR446 |
| Japan | Tokutei shodenryoku radios with the Giteki mark | US FRS/GMRS |

### Europe and the Dolomites: PMR446

The CEPT decision [ECC/DEC/(15)05](https://docdb.cept.org/download/1491) sets out PMR446: 446.0–446.2 MHz, no individual licence, hand-portable only, integral antenna only, effective radiated power not exceeding 500 mW. It also says member administrations "shall permit free carriage and use of all analogue and digital PMR 446 radio equipment".

Italy's regulator MIMIT (the Dolomites are in Italy) says that under a 2020 law, PMR446 requires no administrative formalities and no fees ([MIMIT](https://ispettorati.mise.gov.it/index.php/servizi/dispositivi-debole-potenza)). But the same page says PMR446 communication is permitted to those meeting the requirements of Article 145 of the Electronic Communications Code.

Article 145, read literally, covers citizens aged 14 or over of EU/EEA countries, citizens of countries with reciprocity agreements, and residents of Italy ([Art. 145](https://www.brocardi.it/codice-delle-comunicazioni-elettroniche/titolo-iii/capo-vii/art145.html)). Whether a Taiwanese tourist falls within that, Italy hasn't said publicly, and there's no official explanation of how it squares with CEPT's "free carriage and use".

So for the Dolomites, PMR446 is the only choice that makes sense, but don't think of it as "no question at all". A US FRS radio is worse in Europe: its band isn't in PMR446 at all.

### US: FRS is licence-free, GMRS needs a licence

The FCC treats FRS as "licensed-by-rule", so the public can use it without applying for a licence; there are 22 channels, and radios must be FCC-certified and unmodified ([FCC FRS](https://www.fcc.gov/wireless/bureau-divisions/mobility-division/family-radio-service-frs)). The FCC also says no FRS channel is assigned for the exclusive use of any user, so someone else may always be on yours.

5 W radios are usually GMRS, which needs an FCC licence ([FCC GMRS](https://www.fcc.gov/wireless/bureau-divisions/mobility-division/general-mobile-radio-service-gmrs)). Rocky Talkie's own Expedition Radio page says: "not legal for use outside the US".

### Japan: tokutei shodenryoku, and US radios are explicitly illegal

Japan's Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications says plainly in its Q&A that FRS and GMRS radios are built to US standards and sold for use in the US, so using them in Japan violates the Radio Act ([MIC Radio Use Portal](https://www.tele.soumu.go.jp/j/adm/monitoring/summary/qa/giteki_mark/)). The penalty is up to 1 year's imprisonment or a fine of up to ¥1 million; interfering with high-priority public radio raises it to up to 5 years. What you can use licence-free in Japan is a "tokutei shodenryoku" (specified low-power) radio with the Giteki mark, at around 10 mW.

### Korea

Korea's licence-free option is a KC-certified digital "saenghwal" (everyday) radio on 424/448 MHz at 0.5 W or less; foreign radios aren't on those bands, and transmitting on one in Korea is punishable under Article 84 of the Radio Waves Act. Analogue everyday radios may only be used until the end of 2026. How to rent or buy in Japan and Korea, and what local climbers use, is in [Climbing Communications for Japan and Korea](/posts/climbing/2026-11-02-climbing-comms-japan-korea-en).

Something to do tonight: write down your next overseas climbing destination, check it against the table above, and confirm whether your radios are that region's spec. If not, put them on the "borrow or buy" list.

## Four criteria

### 1. Attachment: reachable, and hard to drop

Climbing.com's article says to keep the radio somewhere you can reach while climbing, with the volume loud enough to hear over wind and water. The practical problem: most radios have a plastic clip designed for a belt.

- **Rocky Talkie** clips to your harness or pack with a climbing-rated Trango carabiner, plus a coiled backup leash against drops ([Rocky Talkie](https://rockytalkie.com/products/mountain-radio)). The carabiner isn't a locker; the leash is the backup.
- **Other brands** mostly have only a belt clip; add a short leash yourself and clip it to your pack or harness.

Something to do tonight: clip the radio to your pack strap or harness, put on gloves, and with one hand bring it to your mouth, press, say a sentence and put it back. If that's awkward, move it or add a short leash.

### 2. Batteries: how many days, how cold, swappable or not

- **Built-in lithium**: Rocky Talkie quotes 3–5 days for the Mountain Radio, "even in extreme cold". You can buy a spare battery; if it dies on the mountain, though, you can't just drop in ordinary cells. Rocky Talkie also notes that below 15% charge the radio can receive but not transmit ([Rocky Talkie blog](https://rockytalkie.com/blogs/rocky-talkie-blog/get-the-most-out-of-your-rocky-talkies)).
- **AA-capable**: the Motorola T470, T600, T82 Extreme, Cobra ACXT1035R and Taiwan's Motorola T6501 all take dry cells. When they die, you swap in a new set.
- **Runtime varies a lot**: Motorola quotes up to 12 hours for the T470; Rocky Talkie is measured in days.

Something to do tonight: charge the radio fully, leave it on overnight, and check what's left in the morning. That's closer to what you'll see than the manufacturer's day count.

### 3. Waterproofing

- **IP56** (Rocky Talkie Mountain Radio): snow, rain and splash proof, but not submersible. Rocky Talkie suggests blowing on the face of the radio in rain rather than wiping it with your hand.
- **IP67** (Motorola T605 H2O, Cobra ACXT1035R, Rocky Talkie Expedition): survives immersion, and the T605 and ACXT1035R float. Good for canyons and sea cliffs.
- **IPX4, IP54** (most cheap radios): fine in light rain; put them in your pack in heavy rain.

Long Dong is a seaside crag, so spray and salt will reach the radio. I'd pick at least IP56, and make sure the charging and headset port covers are shut.

### 4. Lock, privacy codes, glove-friendly buttons

- **Lock**: the radio scrapes against rock as you climb, and if the channel gets knocked you lose contact. On the Rocky Talkie, hold the channel toggle forward for about two seconds to lock it ([OutdoorGearLab](https://www.outdoorgearlab.com/reviews/camping-and-hiking/walkie-talkies/rocky-talkie-mountain-radio)); the reviewer calls it a "critical feature". The Motorola T470 and Midland XT70 PRO also have keypad locks.
- **Privacy codes aren't encryption**: Motorola's manual describes the code as blocking reception of signals that don't carry the matching code; setting 0 turns it off and everyone on the channel is heard ([Motorola MS350 manual](https://www.motorolasolutions.com/content/dam/msi/docs/business/products/consumertwo-wayradios/talkabout_ms350r/ms350_series_ug_kem-ml36100-15_web_04-11-11.pdf)). It only filters what you hear; anyone with codes off can still hear you.
- **Pairing across brands**: Rocky Talkie channels 23–128 are channels 1–22 with a preset privacy code applied; to pair with another brand, check the manufacturer's table. Different brands' code numbers don't always match, so test the two radios together before the trip.
- **Gloves**: big buttons are easier. Giovanni Saccarola says in his video that the Rocky Talkie's buttons are easy to press with gloves (see the videos below).

Something to do tonight: set two radios to the same channel and privacy code, then switch the code off on one and see whether the other still hears it. You'll see for yourself that privacy codes only affect receiving.

## How to talk on a radio

Your calls don't change; how you say them does. The following is distilled from the AAC communication guide and two Climbing.com guide articles from 2024 and 2025; it isn't anyone's official call set.

- **Name first, then the call**: the AAC recommends putting your partner's name before the call so neighbouring parties don't mishear.
- **One thing at a time**: the 2025 Climbing.com article says "When using radios, don't chat". Its example: the leader's message mixed building an anchor, off belay and a hawk sighting, the belayer replied "You're off belay!", and only minutes later did the leader say the anchor was built and to take him off belay ([Climbing.com, 2025](https://www.climbing.com/skills/pro-tips-for-avoiding-climbing-communication-breakdowns/)).
- **Echo key calls**: the leader says "Off belay", the belayer replies "Belay off" and waits a moment before taking the rope out, giving the leader a last chance to correct (AAC).
- **Press, then pause**: hold PTT for about a second before speaking, so your first word isn't clipped.
- **Test before you leave**: check battery, confirm the same channel and privacy code, do a radio check with each other, and make sure no other party is on your channel; change if there is (Climbing.com 2025).
- **Agree what happens if it fails**: dropped, broken, flat, out of range, you fall back on voice, rope tugs or a time plan. Rope tugs are easy to misread, so keep them as the last resort.

## Choosing by situation

### Single pitch at Long Dong

Don't buy one. Voice, line of sight and agreeing beforehand what happens at the top matter more than a radio.

### Multi-pitch or long mountain routes in Taiwan

- Buy a pair of NCC-certified FRS radios, such as the Motorola T6501. For AA models, carry a spare set.
- Add your own short leash and clip it to your pack strap.
- Don't use a US-spec Rocky Talkie in Taiwan.

### The Dolomites or the Alps

- Take PMR446: the Rocky Talkie EU version (€204 a pair) if you want the carabiner attachment, the Midland XT70 PRO if weight matters.
- Buying in Europe, or from Rocky Talkie's European store, makes it less likely you'll get the wrong version.
- Remember that Italy's statute doesn't spell out the position for non-EU tourists; if you're unsure, ask local guides what they do.
- To call for help, take a satellite messenger; see [Satellite messengers and personal locator beacons](/posts/climbing/2026-11-01-satellite-messengers-plb-en). Radios can't reach rescue services.

### Long routes in the US

The Rocky Talkie Mountain Radio is currently the only benchmark with climbing reviews; on a budget, the Midland T71. 5 W GMRS radios need an FCC licence, and I wouldn't bother with them as a visitor.

### Japan and Korea

Leave your Taiwanese radios at home. In Japan, rent a digital simplified radio (dejikan) or buy a tokutei shodenryoku radio with the Giteki mark locally; in Korea, rent or buy a digital everyday radio. Rental shops, models and prices are in [Climbing Communications for Japan and Korea](/posts/climbing/2026-11-02-climbing-comms-japan-korea-en).

## Inspection and care

- **Before leaving**: charge fully; for AA models, bring a spare set. A Rocky Talkie can't transmit below 15%, so don't wait until it's almost flat to charge.
- **At the crag**: do a radio check between the two units and lock the channel.
- **After rain or sea spray**: dry them at home and store at room temperature; check the charging and headset port covers are closed. In rain, blow on the mic rather than wiping it with your hand.
- **Long-term storage**: Rocky Talkie recommends 50–80% charge, powered off, somewhere warm and dry.

For how to inspect carabiners, slings and other climbing gear, see the last post in this series, [How to inspect and retire climbing gear](/posts/climbing/2026-10-19-climbing-gear-inspection-retirement-en).

## Practice: four scenarios

Answers are under each question; think first, then look.

**1. You bought a pair of Rocky Talkie Mountain Radios from a US website and want to take them to Long Dong for a multi-pitch next week.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Bringing them into Taiwan is fine (up to 5 units for personal use without a permit). But they're US FRS, with most channels at 462 MHz, outside Taiwan's licence-free band; read literally, Article 52 of the Telecommunications Management Act means transmitting with them in Taiwan uses a frequency not assigned to you. The NCC hasn't published an interpretation for this case, but the safest approach is to use NCC-certified FRS radios in Taiwan and save the Rocky Talkies for the US.

</details>

**2. Two parties on the same wall both have radios on channel 5. You've set a privacy code and assume the other party can't hear you.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Wrong. A privacy code only makes your radio block signals without the matching code; if the other party has no code set, they hear everything you say. Worse, you may not hear them, and assume the channel is clear. Switch to an unused channel and put names before your calls.

</details>

**3. The leader says over the radio: "I'm there, building the anchor, you can take me off belay in a bit, it's really windy up here."**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Don't take them off belay. That message holds three things, and none of them is a clear "Off belay". Reply: "(Name), I've still got you on belay. Say 'Off belay' when you're ready." On a radio, say one thing at a time and echo key calls.

</details>

**4. You're heading to the Dolomites for a 12-pitch route, and your partner says: "Radios are enough, an inReach is too expensive, let's skip it."**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Radios only let you talk to each other; they can't reach rescue services, and they aren't satellite devices. To call for help you need a satellite messenger or personal locator beacon; see [Satellite messengers and personal locator beacons](/posts/climbing/2026-11-01-satellite-messengers-plb-en). As for radios, take PMR446, not US or Taiwan FRS.

</details>
