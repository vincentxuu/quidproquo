## Today's Focus

Behavioral questions about failure or missed commitments aren't testing whether your résumé is spotless — they're testing two things: whether you'll honestly own a bad call, and whether that failure actually changed something going forward. Most candidates don't struggle to find a story; they struggle because halfway through, they start defending themselves and quietly shift the blame onto something external, leaving the interviewer with no sense of what the candidate actually learned. Today's question comes straight from Amazon's PM Bar Raiser round, and it's specifically designed to test whether you can admit a failure and still demonstrate sound judgment in the same breath.

## Framework Cheat Sheet

**STAR** is the baseline structure for any behavioral question, but on a "tell me about a failure" prompt, following it mechanically turns into a flat narrative. Adding one extra move — splitting Action into "owning it" and "fixing it" — lets you show honesty and judgment at the same time:

| Step | What to do | Common mistake |
|------|-----------|-----------------|
| Situation | State what was promised and why it mattered | Spending too long on background before getting interrupted |
| Task | Your specific role and the scope you owned | Using "we" to blur your own accountability |
| Action (own it) | State plainly when you realized you'd miss it, and why | Explaining and defending before admitting the outcome |
| Action (split) | Separate the decisions you controlled from the external factors you didn't | Blaming everything external, or over-blaming yourself in a way that hides your actual judgment |
| Action (fix it) | What you did at the time to limit the damage and who you told | Describing feelings instead of concrete mitigation steps |
| Result | A verifiable outcome, with a number if possible | No measurable result or comparison |
| Reflection | A concrete process change or checkpoint you added afterward | Stopping at "I learned to be more careful" with nothing specific |

## Today's Practice Question

### The Question

"Tell me about a time you weren't able to deliver on a commitment. What caused it, what was the impact, and what would you do differently?"

(Source: a commonly reported question from Amazon's PM Bar Raiser interview round)

### How to Break It Down

1. **Frame the commitment**: State what was promised, to whom, and why it mattered — enough for the interviewer to see the stakes, without spending more than about 20% of your answer on setup.
2. **Own the miss directly**: Name the specific moment you realized you'd miss it and what you were thinking at that point — don't build up a pile of justifications before admitting the outcome.
3. **Split what you controlled from what you didn't**: Separate the causes into two buckets — your own judgment errors (underestimating a dependency, not confirming a resource early enough) and genuinely external factors outside your control at the time. This is the core of the answer: the interviewer is checking whether you can honestly isolate your own slice of the failure instead of pinning it all on something external.
4. **Describe the mitigation**: Once you realized you'd miss the deadline, what did you actually do — who did you tell, how did you reprioritize, did you negotiate any buffer.
5. **Define what changed afterward**: Don't stop at "I learned to be more careful." Name a specific process change or checkpoint you added, and whether it's actually held up since.

### Sample Answer (something you could actually say in the interview)

> **Frame the commitment and own the miss.** Last year I led a cross-team feature launch and committed to a six-week delivery, because it was blocking a major customer's renewal timeline. By week four, I discovered a key dependency — an API change owned by another team — was going to land two weeks later than I'd planned for, and that risk was on me: I hadn't proactively confirmed the timeline with that team when I first scoped the project, it wasn't a last-minute change on their end.
>
> **Split the responsibility and describe the fix.** My call at that point wasn't to push the original timeline and hope it worked out. I went straight to my manager and the customer success team and said plainly that the dependency risk went unflagged because I hadn't built cross-team confirmation into my planning process, and I proposed shipping in two phases — the core functionality that didn't depend on the other team's API first, so the customer could see real progress, with the rest following two weeks later. The customer accepted the phased plan and the renewal wasn't affected, but the overall delivery still landed two weeks past my original commitment.
>
> **What changed afterward.** Since then, I've added a fixed step to my own project-planning process: any cross-team dependency needs a written, confirmed date from the owning team before it goes on my timeline — I can't just estimate it from past experience. The three cross-team projects I've run since haven't had a repeat of that same failure mode.

## Today's Case Study

**Netflix's Qwikster split (2011): reversed within three weeks of publicly owning the failure**

In September 2011, Netflix CEO Reed Hastings announced splitting the DVD-by-mail business into a standalone brand, Qwikster, separate from the streaming service, on top of a price increase the company had already pushed through. The market reaction was immediate and severe: according to *The New York Times*, Netflix lost roughly 800,000 subscribers in that quarter alone, and the stock fell nearly 77% from its July peak by November. Hastings didn't hold the line on the original plan — he published a public apology admitting the company had "messed up," and within about three weeks of the Qwikster announcement, reversed the split entirely, folding DVD and streaming back under one Netflix brand (the price increase itself stayed in place).

**Interview angle**: Use this case for questions about owning a failure fast. Hastings didn't blame the market or users for "not understanding" the decision — he directly admitted the call was wrong and reversed it as quickly as possible instead of holding out and hoping sentiment would turn around. For "missed commitment" questions, this case makes a clean point: how fast you admit the miss usually matters more to trust than the miss itself.
