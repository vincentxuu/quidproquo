## Today's Focus

The most common way candidates lose points in Strategy & Execution interviews isn't failing to come up with a plan — it's seeing "the competitor has a better feature" and jumping straight to "so we need to build X," without first stopping to ask what battlefield this fight is actually being fought on. Is a specific feature genuinely weaker, or is the whole category being redefined so that the advantage you've been relying on no longer matters?

This matters because the most common real-world strategy failure isn't picking the wrong tactic — it's fighting a technically impressive battle on the wrong battlefield. A company pours resources into catching up on some feature gap, only to find the market had already changed what it was scoring on. Whether a candidate asks "what is this question actually comparing" the moment they hear the prompt, instead of assuming the stated threat is the real problem, is a direct signal of real strategic judgment rather than a framework reflex that generates plans on autopilot.

## Framework Cheat Sheet

### The Six-Step Strategy Framework

Source: [Aced — Product Strategy Interview Questions](https://www.aced.io/blog/product-strategy-interview-questions). This framework splits a strategy answer into two halves: the first four steps are analysis, and the last two are where the actual score gets decided — the commitment.

| Step | What to do | Common mistake |
|------|----------|----------|
| 1. Clarify the question | Pin down the time horizon (a 6-month tactical move vs. a 3-year strategy), what "winning" means, and any fixed constraints (budget, existing product commitments) | Jumping straight to options without framing the scope first |
| 2. Anchor to business goals | Work out what the company actually makes money from and where it's really putting resources, not what its mission statement says | Treating "what the company says it cares about" as "what the company actually optimizes for" |
| 3. Map the battlefield | Define where this threat or opportunity actually comes from, whether the competitor's posture is offense or defense, and why this is happening now | Generic lines like "AI is disrupting everything" with no specificity |
| 4. Set a constraining principle | Derive at least one principle from steps 2-3 that actually rules options out | A principle like "we should prioritize user trust" that doesn't eliminate anything |
| 5. Generate and filter options | Brainstorm broadly first, then filter using the step-4 principle down to at most three options | Listing seven or eight options, which means no real filtering happened |
| 6. Commit and argue against yourself | Pick one, explain why clearly, then proactively name the strongest counter-argument and explain why you still land there | Stopping at "it depends on the team's priorities," which means never actually committing |

How to use it in interviews: when you get a "company X facing threat Y, what do you do" prompt, use steps 2-3 to question what the threat actually is, use step 4 to narrow options down to three or fewer, and always end by naming the strongest counter-argument — that's the one dividing line between a senior and a junior answer.

## Today's Practice Question

### The Question

"Imagine you're the CPO of Zoom, facing heavy competition from Google Meet. What would you do?"

(Source: a real Google PM interview question, collected in [Aced — Product Strategy Interview Questions](https://www.aced.io/blog/product-strategy-interview-questions))

### Breaking It Down

1. **Clarify the question**: Ask the interviewer which dimension of "competition" they mean — enterprise customers churning to Google Meet, or free/individual users migrating away? Is the time horizon the next two quarters of defensive moves, or Zoom's overall three-year positioning? Different scopes point to completely different tiers of answer.
2. **Anchor to business goals**: Zoom doesn't actually make money from "video calling" as a feature — it makes money from enterprise seat subscriptions, depth of adoption across rooms and departments, and the stickiness that keeps customers in Zoom's ecosystem. Comparing video quality and latency feature-for-feature against Google Meet is competing on the wrong metric.
3. **Map the battlefield**: Google Meet's most dangerous advantage isn't a stronger feature — it's being bundled free into Google Workspace, which for any enterprise that's already paying for Workspace is effectively zero marginal cost. That's a distribution and business-model advantage, not a product advantage. The real threat is that video conferencing itself is becoming a commodity everyone expects for free — closing a specific feature gap doesn't solve that.
4. **Propose a plan**: Set a principle — don't chase "match Google Meet's free video specs," because Google's zero-marginal-cost structure makes that a fight you can't win. Instead, reposition Zoom as a work-intelligence layer that isn't tied to a single meeting platform. The filtered recommendation: open up Zoom's AI features (meeting notes, summaries, action items) so users can use them directly inside Teams and Google Meet, without first switching platforms. The strongest counter-argument: this means voluntarily giving up "keeping users on Zoom video" as a traditional moat, which could accelerate substitution by rivals — but commit anyway, because the video-conferencing moat itself is already being eroded by free bundling, so it's better to build a new point of stickiness on a competitor's turf than defend a position that's already disappearing.
5. **Define success**: Success isn't "did Zoom's video-conferencing market share stop declining" — it's whether usage of cross-platform AI features is growing, and whether enterprise customers who use the cross-platform features renew at a better rate than customers who only use pure video. If stickiness really is shifting from "video quality" to "the intelligence layer," these signals will show up before the market-share number does.

### Sample Answer (how you'd actually say this in an interview)

> **Clarifying and anchoring**: "Before I list out a plan, I want to confirm the scope of this question — are we talking about enterprise customers churning, or free-user attrition? I'll assume it's enterprise renewal defense, since that's Zoom's real revenue engine. And before going further, I want to flag something: Zoom doesn't actually make money from 'better video quality' — it makes money from enterprise seat subscriptions and depth of cross-department adoption. So if I just compare feature checklists against Google Meet, I'm fighting a battle I've already designed myself to lose."
>
> **Mapping the threat and the plan**: "What makes Google Meet genuinely dangerous isn't that any one feature beats ours — it's that it's bundled free into Google Workspace, so for any enterprise that already pays for Workspace, using Meet is effectively zero cost. That means video conferencing as a whole category is becoming something everyone expects for free, and we can't out-feature a structural advantage like that. So I'd set a principle: don't chase free video specs, and instead reposition Zoom as a work-intelligence layer that isn't locked to one meeting platform — letting our AI meeting assistant work directly inside Teams and Google Meet, without requiring users to switch to Zoom first. The biggest counter-argument is that this means voluntarily giving up the traditional video moat, but I think that moat is already being eroded by free bundling anyway — rather than defending a position that's disappearing, it's better to build a new point of stickiness on the competitor's own turf."
>
> **Defining success**: "I wouldn't measure this by 'did Zoom's video market share stop falling,' because that's a lagging indicator, and it's entirely possible to be directionally right and still see it decline for a while longer. I'd watch growth in cross-platform AI feature usage, and whether enterprise customers using the cross-platform features renew at a meaningfully better rate than customers using only pure video. If stickiness really is shifting from 'video quality' to 'the intelligence layer,' those signals will show up before the market-share number does."

## Today's Case

**Zoom: opening AI Companion up to Microsoft Teams and Google Meet users, without requiring a platform switch**

In September 2025, Zoom announced it was extending its AI meeting assistant, AI Companion, to Microsoft Teams, Google Meet, and in-person meetings — letting users get Zoom's AI note-taking, summaries, and action items on a competitor's platform, with no need to move the meeting to Zoom first. By early 2026, this expanded further with AI Companion 3.0, with coverage describing Zoom as positioning itself as a universal work-intelligence layer that works no matter which video platform a meeting happens to run on. That's the real answer behind today's practice question: instead of fighting to win video conferencing specs — a battlefield becoming a free commodity — Zoom chose to voluntarily give up "keeping users on Zoom video" as its traditional moat, and build a new point of stickiness on its competitors' own turf.

**Interview relevance**: This case is direct evidence for the "strongest counter-argument" section of the answer above — opening AI features up to Teams and Google Meet users is effectively admitting the video-conferencing moat itself can no longer be defended, which is exactly the kind of "there's a real trade-off, and it hurts" judgment interviewers want to hear a candidate say out loud, rather than a safe answer nobody would object to. When answering this type of question, you can cite this case to show that mature strategic judgment sometimes means admitting a moat is no longer worth defending, and rebuilding advantage on a new battlefield instead.
