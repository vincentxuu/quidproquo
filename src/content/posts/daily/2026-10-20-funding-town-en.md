## What this company does

Town builds a team version of the AI work assistant. Every employee gets a personal assistant called a Townie, but a Townie no longer works only for the one person it belongs to — it can now hand off work across team boundaries too.

The core product connects a Townie to its owner's email, documents, calendar, and day-to-day work systems, learning that person's relationships, active projects, and voice, then proactively taking work off their plate. The capability announced in this round lets different employees' Townies talk to each other: asking questions, connecting the dots, and handing off tasks — while following three rules: share only what the person would share themselves, ask for permission when it's needed, and leave the decisions that genuinely require human judgment to the human. Teams can also set up shared workflows — when sales ops updates the competitive battle cards once, every rep's Townie picks up the change before their next call. Town says each customer owns the context its Townies build and can take it with them if they leave, that customer data is not used to train models by default, and that the underlying model can be swapped per task so no customer is locked into a single AI vendor.

The company was co-founded in late 2024 by former Plaid CTO Jean-Denis Greze and former Google AI / Dropbox product design lead Tony Vincent. It only launched publicly in June 2026, reached seven figures of annualized recurring revenue within weeks, and has since grown paying customers 4x and paying teams 9x since July 2026. Thousands of businesses now use Townies daily.

## What this round signals

### What it means for the agent ecosystem

The headline here isn't "how smart is one assistant" — it's "can assistants work together." Town frames the problem directly: AI has already made individuals faster on their own, but most work depends on other people — waiting on finance to update a model, waiting on an ops lead to grant access, waiting on an external sales contact to reply. As individuals get faster, teams can end up slower, stuck waiting on each other. Today's AI assistants split into two camps: a personal assistant knows its user but can't see the team; a team bot can see shared channels but doesn't really know any one person. Town's bet is to keep the "knows this person" advantage of a personal assistant while adding the "can work with other assistants" layer on top — and extend that collaboration outside the company, to customers, partners, and candidates.

### What the investors are betting on

Shawn Carolan, a partner at co-lead Menlo Ventures, has publicly noted that his firm backed Siri back in 2008 — framing this investment as the same bet, except Town chose business over consumer. That's a signal Menlo thinks personal assistants only truly scale commercially inside work, not everyday life. The other co-lead, Forerunner Ventures, is known for consumer-brand investing; its move into enterprise software here is itself notable — a sign that a well-known consumer VC is now treating "personal assistant" as a category that straddles consumer-grade product experience and enterprise-grade business model. Hitting a $1B valuation just four months after public launch means these investors aren't betting on whether the concept works — they're betting on whether the growth rate can keep justifying the valuation.

### Numbers worth tracking

- A $1B valuation four months after launch, up from an undisclosed valuation at the June 2026 Series A — a sharp acceleration in a very short window
- Paying teams grew 9x since July 2026, outpacing the 4x growth in paying customers — suggesting adoption inside existing customers is spreading from individual trial use to whole-team rollout
- At least $160M raised against a headcount of just 11–50 (per Crunchbase) — capital per employee far above most SaaS companies at this stage, a bet on a lean, fast-growing model

## Today's takeaway

Most AI assistant products still compete on how much one assistant can do for one person. Town moved the battleground to whether assistants can hand off work to each other — which is actually where enterprise efficiency gets stuck: it's not that individuals are too slow, it's that they're waiting on everyone else. A consumer-brand VC (Forerunner) choosing to back enterprise software here, instead of another lifestyle assistant app, is itself a hint about where the next round of personal-assistant valuation premiums will come from — not from "knowing you better," but from "making an entire team faster together."
