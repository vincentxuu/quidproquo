## Today's Focus

Strategy questions have always been the hardest PM interview question to answer well, and the easiest to answer adequately. Aced (formerly Exponent) flags a clear shift in its 2026 guide: a structured answer that names the right frameworks used to be the bar for passing. Now it's the bar for rejection — because AI tools let every candidate produce a structurally polished answer in seconds, so structure alone is no longer a threshold, it's the starting line.

What actually separates candidates is whether they land on a point of view that five other prepared candidates couldn't have given — knowing what a company actually optimizes for (not what its mission statement says), naming a threat down to its real mechanism instead of vague "AI is disrupting everything" talk, and ultimately making a call, naming the strongest counter-argument, and explaining why they still land there. Today's framework and practice question are both built around that convergence skill.

## Framework Cheat Sheet

### Framework One: the six-step strategy framework (Aced, 2026)

Strategy questions span a wide range — a market threat, a new category expansion, an acquisition call, a build-vs-buy decision — but these six steps apply across all of them. They're scaffolding, not the answer: two candidates running the same six steps can land on completely different responses, because one brings an opinion and the other brings a template.

| Step | What to do | Common mistake |
|------|------------|-----------------|
| 1. Clarify | Pin down the time horizon, what success looks like, and existing constraints, using 2-3 clarifying questions that do double duty — narrowing scope while also revealing a hypothesis forming | Asking only generic clarifying questions without surfacing any point of view |
| 2. Anchor to business goals | Work out the company's real revenue streams and where money actually flows (revealed preference), not what the mission statement says | Treating the mission statement as what the company actually optimizes for |
| 3. Map the landscape | Define the threat's actual mechanism and source, competitors' theory of winning (offense/defense, broad/deep), and why it's a question now | Producing a feature comparison table instead of describing strategic posture |
| 4. Set guiding principles | Pull one principle each from steps 2 and 3 that can actually eliminate options | Writing principles that sound like slogans but don't rule anything out |
| 5. Generate and filter options | Brainstorm without judgment for 60-90 seconds, then filter with the principles from step 4 down to at most three survivors | Still having 5-7 options left after "filtering," meaning filtering never actually happened |
| 6. Argue and close | State in two sentences which option and why, name the strongest counter-argument, explain why you still land there, and flag the signal that would change your mind | Spending the whole answer getting here and running out of room to actually converge |

### Framework Two: the stupidity flip + counter-positioning (Roger Martin / Hamilton Helmer)

When setting guiding principles in step 4, or judging whether a differentiator is strong enough, use Roger Martin's "stupidity flip" test — state the opposite of your strategic choice out loud. If the opposite sounds obviously absurd, you haven't actually made a choice. For example, "make sales more efficient" flipped is "make sales slower," which is absurd, so it isn't a real choice. But "make sales more efficient through in-person client visits" flipped is "through remote interactions," a real existing alternative — that's an actual strategic choice.

For differentiation itself, check it against Hamilton Helmer's 7 Powers categories: can you actually execute it, and does it actually create distance from competitors? Counter-positioning is worth special attention — a new entrant picks a move that an incumbent knows it should match but can't, because matching it would gut its own existing business model. Today's case study is the textbook example of exactly this move.

## Today's Practice Question

### The Question

"You're Zoom's CPO facing heavy competition from Google Meet. What do you do?"

(Source: a real Google PM interview question, collected in Aced's "Product Strategy Interview Questions (2026 Guide)" under the "strategic analysis" question type)

### How to Break It Down

1. **Clarify**: First ask the interviewer whether this is about a 6-month defensive move or a 3-year strategic direction, and whether success is measured by enterprise renewal rate or overall install base. Surface a hypothesis at the same time: "I'll assume the goal is defending our position in the enterprise market rather than chasing consumer free-tier usage, since those two goals point to completely different option sets."
2. **Anchor to business goals**: Zoom's real revenue engine is enterprise subscriptions and add-on services — Zoom Phone, Zoom Workspace, hardware certification ecosystem — not free consumer meeting volume. Any analysis that stays at the level of "the video conferencing market" is analyzing the wrong company.
3. **Map the landscape**: Google Meet's threat isn't feature parity, it's distribution — Meet comes almost free through Workspace bundling, so Google doesn't need the meeting product itself to make money; it can subsidize it indefinitely to retain Workspace customers. Naming the threat precisely as "bundled distribution, not technology" is the level of precision interviewers are looking for.
4. **Set guiding principles**: Pull one principle from each of the previous two steps that can actually eliminate options — don't compete with Google on a free-bundling war, since the resources aren't comparable (this kills any price-war option); focus on the integration depth enterprise customers already depend on — calendar systems, phone systems, conference-room hardware certification — rather than any single point feature (this kills options like "add a few more AI widgets").
5. **Argue and close**: Choose "deepen enterprise workflow integration and the hardware certification ecosystem" over a price war or a single AI feature, because switching cost is the moat Zoom actually holds right now. The strongest counter-argument is that Google can use free pricing to squeeze out any enterprise bargaining power; the reason to proceed anyway is that enterprise procurement cycles are long and IT has already invested in existing integrations, so this moat won't be wiped out by free pricing in the short term. The signal to watch is enterprise logo churn — if it starts climbing, that's a sign this switching-cost moat is eroding, and the whole direction needs to be reassessed.

### Sample Answer (how to actually say it in an interview)

> **Anchor first, don't jump straight to tactics**: "I wouldn't jump straight into 'how do we beat Google Meet.' I'd first confirm the time horizon — if this is a 3-year strategy, my starting point is what Zoom actually makes money from: enterprise subscriptions, add-on services like Zoom Phone, not free consumer meeting volume. This step matters, because anyone who treats this as a 'video conferencing market share war' is analyzing the wrong company."
>
> **Name the threat at the mechanism level**: "Google Meet's real threat isn't features, it's distribution — it comes almost free through Workspace bundling, and Google doesn't need the meeting product itself to make money; it can subsidize it indefinitely to retain enterprise customers. That means we can't compete on free pricing — the resource structures aren't comparable — and that single point eliminates any option built around a price war."
>
> **Converge on one option and own the counter-argument**: "I'd prioritize deepening the workflow integration and hardware certification ecosystem enterprise customers already depend on, because switching cost is the moat we actually hold right now, not bolting on a few more AI widgets. The strongest counter-argument is that Google can use free pricing to crush our bargaining power entirely. I'd still hold this direction, because enterprise procurement cycles are long and existing integrations don't get replaced overnight — but I'd watch enterprise logo churn closely, and if it starts climbing, that's a sign this moat is eroding and needs to be reassessed."

## Today's Case Study

**Netflix vs. Blockbuster: the textbook counter-positioning battle**

In 2000, Blockbuster collected nearly $800 million in late fees — 16% of company revenue, not a side business, a core part of its primary cash flow. Netflix launched a no-late-fee subscription model that hit members' single biggest pain point directly. Blockbuster knew it should match this. It couldn't. Dropping late fees meant gutting a revenue stream worth a sixth of the company's top line — no public company can tell shareholders that story. This is the textbook case of counter-positioning from Hamilton Helmer's 7 Powers: the new entrant's move isn't one the incumbent can't technically copy — it's one the incumbent's own business model makes it structurally unable to match.

**Interview angle**: use this case directly for any question like "a competitor just launched a new move, how do you respond" or "how do you build a moat." It demonstrates the test for counter-positioning — ask whether the competitor isn't matching you because they technically can't, or because matching you would gut their own existing cash flow. If it's the latter, that moat is usually more durable than a simple feature lead, because the competitor isn't unwilling to match — it's structurally unable to.
