## What this company does

Legora builds legal AI agents — instead of just helping lawyers read contracts faster, it lets legal teams hand off multi-step work like research, tracking regulatory changes, and triaging incoming requests directly to an agent.

Founded in 2023, Legora launched its platform in October 2024. Its core product is Legora aOS, a legal AI "agent operating system" built around a clear repositioning: from AI that assists a person reading documents, to agents that take over an entire case workflow. The platform now serves more than 140,000 legal professionals across 80 markets, with over 2,400 law firms and in-house legal teams as customers. In the US — where Legora opened its first office only in spring 2025 — it already counts more than half of the AmLaw 50 firms as customers.

Growth speed is the centerpiece of the company's pitch. Annual recurring revenue (ARR) went from $1 million to $100 million in under 18 months, and the next $100 million took less than six months — ARR now sits above $200M. In-house legal departments, rather than traditional law firms, have grown from a small slice of new customers a year ago to 40% of new customers this quarter, signaling that Legora's buyer base is expanding from law firms to corporate legal departments.

## What this round signals

### What it means for the agent ecosystem

This is the second $550M-scale raise by a legal AI startup in six months — Legora's biggest rival, Harvey, closed its own $550M round at a $15.5B valuation just six weeks earlier. Two companies landing the same check size almost back-to-back suggests investors see legal services as entering an early, decisive phase of agent-driven disruption, with capital rapidly concentrating around the handful of companies that can prove agents can own a complete workflow. Legora plans to use the money to expand across North America, Europe, and Asia-Pacific, while continuing to invest in aOS's agent capabilities.

### What the investors are betting on

Lead investor Atreides Management isn't betting on growth speed — it's betting on growth durability. Managing partner Gavin Baker put it this way: "Legal is a sophisticated market with demanding buyers, and the bar for software is high... Legora has become the platform that law firms and in-house legal teams adopt and then expand across their organizations." CFO David Eckstein added that the business has "healthy gross margins, high retention, and a cost structure that gets stronger as we scale" — a different lens than the typical SaaS growth-rate pitch; Atreides is underwriting whether this ARR survives a downturn, not just how fast it's growing.

### Numbers worth tracking

- The valuation climbed 1.57x in seven months ($5.6B to $8.8B) — a gentler, steadier curve than pure-infrastructure agent plays like Daytona (3.3x in nine months), suggesting vertical legal AI's valuation growth is more measured but also more durable
- ARR took under 18 months to go from $1M to $100M, but less than six months to add the next $100M — the acceleration itself matters more than the absolute dollar figure
- Compared to rival Harvey, which already passed $200M+ ARR at a $15.5B valuation back in March: Legora only caught up on ARR in October, yet is valued at just $8.8B (about 57% of Harvey's). That gap in how the market prices otherwise-similar "agent execution" could narrow or widen depending on each company's next earnings update

## Watchlist status

Legora is not yet on the watchlist. Recommend adding it to section D4 (Legal AI) — and adding rival Harvey to the same section at the same time. The two companies landed near-identical funding within months of each other, making them the most direct comparison available for tracking competitive intensity in the legal AI agent vertical.

## Today's takeaway

Legal work has long been considered one of the hardest professional services for AI to penetrate, on the theory that "judgment" can't be automated. What Legora and Harvey are both proving is different: capital isn't betting that agents can replace a lawyer's judgment — it's betting that agents can fully absorb everything *besides* judgment: research, tracking regulatory changes, triaging incoming requests. What's left for humans is judgment and sign-off. When two companies each raise $550M within six months, and in-house legal departments jump from single digits to 40% of new customers, that split is already showing up in how buyers actually purchase — not just in venture pitch decks.
