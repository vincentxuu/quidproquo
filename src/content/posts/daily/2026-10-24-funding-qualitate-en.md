## What this company does

Qualitate builds what it calls a continuous intelligence system for primary research — turning expert-interview-style market research, which traditionally takes weeks and tens of thousands of dollars per project, into always-on infrastructure that can be queried at any time, rather than a one-off engagement that has to be re-commissioned each time.

Its core product is an AI Interviewer that proactively conducts thousands of expert conversations each month, turning every conversation into structured evidence that accumulates into a research library covering tens of thousands of companies and markets. Each new conversation draws on prior research and a customer's own context to ask sharper questions. Customers can query that library through Qualitate's own agentic research application, or access it directly through Claude or Codex via MCP (Model Context Protocol) — turning the database into a service layer other agents can call directly, rather than just a web interface.

Current customers include investment firms like Alger, Stone Point Capital, and Squarepoint, along with enterprises such as UiPath, Collibra, Alteryx, and Commvault. Revenue run-rate has grown more than 10x this year, the company has logged more than a million minutes of AI-moderated interviews, and 12-month net revenue retention exceeds 300%.

## What this round signals

### What it means for the agent ecosystem

Qualitate isn't wrapping market research in a chatbot — it's decomposing the task of "doing primary research" into a process agents can run continuously: an AI Interviewer asks the questions, structures the answers, accumulates them into a database, and lets other agents query that database directly via MCP. That's a sign that "research-as-infrastructure" is becoming its own category within the agent ecosystem — not an end-user product, but a backend service other agents call, in the same category logic as agent memory layers or agent observability tools.

### What the investors are betting on

The most unusual detail in this round: Insight Partners was already a paying Qualitate customer before it decided to lead. That's not the usual "review the deck, then do diligence" sequence — it's "use the product first, confirm it actually works, then lead the round." Insight has a long track record backing enterprise software infrastructure, and its thesis here is that primary research is shifting from an episodic model to an infrastructure model — the same transition narrative Insight has applied to SaaS moving from licensing to subscription, just applied to the research industry this time.

### Numbers worth tracking

- Only six months separate the $7M seed (April 2026) from the $25M Series A (October 2026) — the fundraising cadence was clearly pulled forward by growth metrics, not planned on a fixed schedule
- 12-month net revenue retention above 300% means existing customers aren't just renewing — they're continuously moving more of their research spend onto Qualitate, a figure that would be considered extreme in any SaaS business
- More than a million minutes of AI-moderated interviews — roughly 19,000 hours — is currently the only concrete number available to gauge database scale, since the company hasn't disclosed the exact number of companies its library covers

## Watchlist status

Qualitate is not yet on the watchlist. Recommend adding it to a watch list for "agent-native research/data infrastructure," tracking whether its MCP distribution model extends to agent ecosystems beyond Claude and Codex, and whether that 300% net revenue retention holds as the business scales.

## Today's takeaway

The most memorable detail here isn't the $25M figure — it's the order of operations: the VC became a customer before it decided to lead. Most B2B AI SaaS fundraising still runs on pitching a market-size slide deck and hoping investors buy in. Qualitate flipped that — let a prospective investor become a real paying customer first, confirm the product actually solves the problem, then negotiate valuation. That may be more persuasive to early-stage founders than any pitch deck, but only if the product is genuinely good enough that a VC wants to pay for it on its own.
