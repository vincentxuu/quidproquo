## Today's Focus

Sunday's rotation is Behavioral & Weekly Review. The trap in this category is answering in abstractions — "I communicated proactively," "I rebuilt trust" — without a single sentence that lets the interviewer actually picture what happened. The consistent theme in 2026 interview guides is that a well-structured STAR story is now table stakes; what separates candidates is whether the "pivotal moment" in the story — usually one conversation, one piece of feedback, one decision — is concrete enough that the interviewer can see it.

## Framework Cheat Sheet

**STAR for the outer timeline, SBI for the pivotal moment.** Most candidates know STAR (Situation → Task → Action → Result), but they narrate the whole thing at the same altitude, and when they reach "I had a conversation with them and things got better afterward," the interviewer loses the thread entirely. SBI (Situation-Behavior-Impact) is a smaller tool built specifically for describing one concrete interaction — you drop it into the Action step of STAR and re-tell that one moment at a finer grain.

| Framework | Purpose | Structure | Common failure |
|------|------|------|-----------|
| STAR | Carries the overall timeline | Situation (context) → Task (your responsibility) → Action (what you did) → Result (a verifiable outcome) | The Action step stays vague, skipping past the one conversation that actually mattered |
| SBI | Makes one interaction inside STAR's Action step concrete | Situation (the specific setting) → Behavior (an observable action someone took — not a character judgment) → Impact (what that action did to the team, the outcome, or you) | Behavior gets written as a judgment ("he was irresponsible") instead of an observable action ("he missed the same committed deliverable in three straight standups") |

Used together: STAR carries the causal arc of the whole story; SBI makes the moment where trust broke, or the moment it was repaired, specific enough to be checked — not just a feeling you're reporting.

## Today's Practice Question

### The question

"Tell me about a time the team's trust was damaged — by someone else or by you — and how you fixed it. What was the result?"

(Source: Amazon PM/TPM interview question bank, as collected by igotanoffer.com)

### How to break it down

1. **Clarify the problem (pick your protagonist)**: This question splits into two branches — trust damaged by *someone else*, or by *you*. Pick a real story with a concrete, verifiable outcome up front; don't default to "I was the one who messed up" just to sound humble if you can't actually back it with detail.
2. **Set the scene**: One or two sentences on what the team and project were, and what things looked like before trust broke — e.g., "This cross-functional group had been working smoothly until..."
3. **Use SBI to make the "damage" moment concrete**: Don't say "he wasn't transparent enough." Say "he committed to delivering a data cut in the weekly sync, missed it two weeks in a row, and never flagged it in advance." That's an observable behavior, so the interviewer can actually hear what "trust" meant in this story.
4. **Use SBI again for the "repair" moment**: What specific action did you take? A one-on-one, a redesigned status-reporting cadence, a public acknowledgment of your own mistake in a meeting? The point is what you *did*, not "things felt better afterward."
5. **Define success**: Close with a verifiable signal that trust actually came back — the person started proactively flagging risk, they were handed a bigger scope, the team started covering for each other without you checking in — not an unfalsifiable line like "everyone felt better about it."

### Sample answer (how this could actually sound in the room)

> **Set the scene**: "I want to walk through a time I damaged trust myself and had to repair it, because I think it says more about how I handle my own mistakes. I was leading a project spanning three teams, and at kickoff I committed to a timeline that, it turned out, I hadn't actually validated with engineering."
>
> **Use SBI for the damage**: "Two weeks later, the engineering lead called it out publicly in standup — the timeline wasn't achievable because of a dependency I hadn't accounted for. I could feel the other two teams start looking at my commitments differently after that. It wasn't just my read on the room — one of the other PMs later told me their team had started quietly padding any date I gave them."
>
> **Use SBI for the repair**: "I didn't wait for someone to come to me. In the next standup, I publicly owned the miscalculation, proposed a revised timeline I'd already validated with engineering beforehand, and added a standing rule: any timeline touching another team would get written sign-off before I committed to it externally. I kept that confirmation process visible for the next two months."
>
> **Close on the result**: "Three months later, the same engineering lead who'd called out the original miss handed me the scoping work for a new project on his own initiative. That was a more credible signal that trust had actually come back than anyone telling me 'it's fine now.'"

### Self-check

| Check | Covered? |
|---------|---------|
| Picked a real story with a concrete outcome, not a vague one | |
| The "damage" moment uses SBI to describe an observable behavior, not a character judgment | |
| The "repair" moment describes a specific action, not "things got better" | |
| Success is defined by a verifiable change in behavior, not a feeling | |

## Today's Case

**Kim Scott at Google: how "you sounded dumb" didn't break trust — it repaired it**

Early in her time at Google, in her first big leadership moment, Kim Scott presented a marketing campaign's results to co-founder Sergey Brin and then-CEO Eric Schmidt with her team. The presentation itself landed well, but afterward her manager, Sheryl Sandberg (later COO of Facebook/Meta), pulled her aside and told her directly: she'd said "um" too many times, it made her sound unsure of herself, and if she didn't fix it, people would start thinking she wasn't competent. Scott has said that in the moment, it stung.

But that blunt feedback didn't damage the trust between them — it became the origin story of Radical Candor: Be a Kick-Ass Boss Without Losing Your Humanity, the management book Scott later wrote. The reason wasn't that Sandberg delivered the feedback gently — she didn't. It was that, before that conversation, Sandberg had already proven through a string of concrete actions that she genuinely cared about Scott as a person: inviting her into her book club, supporting her through a family member's illness. Scott's own explanation: Sandberg could say something as blunt as "you sounded stupid" specifically because Scott already knew Sandberg cared about her — she'd proven it in a thousand small ways. That's the core claim of the Radical Candor framework: "Challenge Directly" only lands as repair, rather than as an attack, when it's stacked on top of "Care Personally."

**Interview angle**: this case fits best on a behavioral question about repairing or building trust through direct feedback. The point isn't whether you should tell people the truth — it's showing a specific causal chain: small, consistent actions establish "this person actually cares about me" first, and only then does a sharp piece of feedback get received as help instead of harm. When answering "how did you repair trust," it's worth checking your own story against this: before the repair moment, is there any evidence you'd already laid down that said "I care about you"?
