## What the company does

Cohere is a Canadian AI company building "sovereign AI" — helping enterprises and governments run large language models on infrastructure they fully control, whether public cloud, private cloud, on-premises, or fully air-gapped environments, without handing data to a third-party cloud. It was founded in Toronto in 2019 by Aidan Gomez, Nick Frosst, and Ivan Zhang; Gomez and Frosst were both Google Brain researchers, and Gomez co-authored the Transformer paper "Attention Is All You Need."

Its core products include the Command family of language models, Embed/Rerank for search ranking, and North 2, this year's update to its agentic AI platform — letting enterprises build agents in their own environments that call internal systems and execute multi-step tasks. Unlike OpenAI or Anthropic, which mainly sell public-cloud APIs, Cohere targets regulated industries (finance, telecom, government, healthcare) whose data can't leave their own infrastructure.

Customers include Aston Martin Aramco, Bell Canada, Fujitsu, RBC, RWS, PwC, TD Bank, and TKMS; the company says its customer count grew 20x over the past year to 300, with 165% year-over-year growth in annual recurring revenue (no absolute ARR figure disclosed). This year it completed a strategic combination with Germany's Aleph Alpha and acquired healthcare-AI company Reliant AI and security company AISLE, continuing its push into vertical-specific AI.

## What this round signals

### What it means for the agent ecosystem

What makes this round distinctive is that "sovereignty" is now formally written into AI infrastructure's capital structure — the lead, Schwarz Group (the German retail giant that owns STACKIT, one of Europe's largest cloud infrastructures), and the Canadian government are betting side by side, meaning "AI sovereignty" is no longer just a policy talking point but an actual equity-investment thesis. Part of the money will go toward training frontier-level enterprise models and expanding long-term compute infrastructure, showing Cohere is charting a different path than competing head-on with OpenAI or Anthropic on raw model capability — betting instead on "data sovereignty plus vertical specialization."

### What investors are betting on

Schwarz Digits CEO Christian Müller's logic is direct: hook Cohere's AI capabilities into Schwarz's own STACKIT sovereign cloud and sell it straight to European enterprises and governments bound by GDPR and local data-residency rules that can't outsource their models to US cloud giants. The Canadian government's equity stake (amount undisclosed, though Bloomberg estimates up to US $1.14B) is a symbolic investment in Cohere as "national AI capability," in exchange for commitments to keep investing in domestic compute infrastructure and jobs. NVIDIA, returning as an investor, is extending its strategy of betting across a diverse AI ecosystem rather than putting all its chips on a single model provider.

### Numbers worth watching

- This is an "initial close" — the company has only disclosed the target ceiling of CAD $7B and a proposed CAD $35.7B valuation (~US $25.6B), with the amount actually secured undisclosed. That's different from most funding stories where the amount is already settled, meaning the final close could end up smaller than the target
- Customer count grew 20x in a year, from 15 to 300, with 165% year-over-year ARR growth — one of the fastest publicly disclosed growth figures in the sovereign-AI race right now
- If the CAD $35.7B valuation holds, it would be the largest private financing round ever for a Canadian tech company, a sign that the AI-sovereignty narrative outside North America's usual hubs is now pulling in sovereign-fund-scale capital

## Today's takeaway

Most AI funding headlines report money that's already in the bank, but Cohere's announcement is an "initial close" — the target ceiling and the amount actually secured are two different things. That's a reminder to read funding news carefully for the difference between "the target announced" and "the cash banked," especially when the lead investor is a sovereign fund or a government equity stake rather than a traditional VC — those closings tend to run on a much longer, less transparent timeline than a typical VC round.
