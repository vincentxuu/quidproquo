## What the company does

Broccoli AI builds front-desk automation agents for home-service businesses — plumbing, HVAC, and electrical contractors. It isn't selling a chatbot; its agents answer inbound calls, book jobs directly into the scheduling software a contractor already uses, follow up on open estimates, and remind customers to renew service memberships.

Its differentiator is not asking owners to change their habits: Broccoli plugs its agents into existing scheduling and bookkeeping systems rather than requiring a switch, handling the chores owners complain about most — nobody picking up the phone after 5 p.m., or no one following up on estimates technicians wrote. More than 1,500 businesses now use it, including Ace Hardware Home Services, Mr. Electric, and Any Hour Group, and in the past seven months it has expanded from plumbing/HVAC/electrical into roofing, pool care, garage doors, and home improvement.

Co-founders AJ Jain and Aditya Ketkar met as students at BITS Pilani in India. Before arriving in San Francisco in early 2024, they had tried several ventures that never scaled — a campus delivery app, an interview-prep bootcamp, a universal EV API in Europe. In May and June 2024, the pair walked into roughly 140 contractors' shops across Northern California carrying notebooks instead of laptops, eventually landing their first customer at Armstrong Plumbing in Sacramento — owner Marcelle Flowers later became Broccoli's COO. The company says its AI now handles about 80% of tasks, equivalent to roughly 30% of a typical contractor office's staff workload, with the remaining 20% — emergencies like a flood or a fire — still routed to humans.

## What this round signals

### What it means for the agent ecosystem

Broccoli shows a vertical agent doesn't need new infrastructure to win — it plugs directly into the scheduling and bookkeeping software owners already run, acting as a "ghost office employee" rather than replacing the whole stack. More notable is the founders' approach: instead of writing code first and finding customers later, they spent months embedded inside a plumbing shop, trading direct observation for domain knowledge before building anything. That suggests the next edge for vertical agents may not be the model itself, but whether founders are willing to spend the time to actually understand how a traditional industry runs.

### What investors are betting on

Khosla Ventures personally led both rounds — Series A and Series B — just nine months apart, signaling its conviction in the growth curve never wavered even after the first round. Khosla has long favored markets where "the long tail of small businesses is large but software penetration is low" — home services in the US is a market worth hundreds of billions of dollars, and most owners haven't even adopted cloud scheduling software yet, leaving far more room for an AI agent to break in than in enterprise software markets SaaS has already saturated.

## Today's takeaway

Both Broccoli founders had tried and failed at several ventures before this one, each time betting on "smarter technology" to win. It wasn't until they set their laptops aside and spent three months embedded in a plumbing shop that they found something that could actually scale. That's a reminder that a vertical agent's real moat usually isn't model capability — anyone can call the same APIs — it's whether anyone is willing to spend the time to truly understand the daily grind of a traditional industry. That can't be bought with more compute; it only comes from time spent.
