## What the company does

Larridin audits how much companies' AI spending is actually paying off. As companies pour money into a growing stack of AI tools and models, Larridin's job is to answer the question nobody else is answering: is this money being well spent?

Its core product is a measurement platform that plugs into a company's internal systems, analyzes how employees interact with various AI tools (including the prompts they write), and determines who's using AI effectively, who needs more training, and how much time is actually being saved. Co-founder and CTO Ameya Kanitkar is emphatic that the platform analyzes interactions without storing their contents — it generates scores and high-level insights instead, to protect employee privacy.

Customer growth is the key evidence behind this round: Larridin went from a single enterprise customer last December to 50 today. CEO Russell Fradin says the business really started accelerating in the second quarter of this year, once companies began taking AI costs seriously.

## What this round signals

### What it means for the agent ecosystem

Corporate AI spending behavior took a sharp turn in 2026. Early in the year, "tokenmaxxing" was the dominant mindset — companies pushed employees to burn through tokens and treated AI usage itself as a performance metric (Duolingo reportedly made AI use part of employee evaluations, and Uber's CTO famously admitted the company had already blown through its annual Claude Code budget). By mid-year, that had flipped to "modelmaxxing" — dynamically routing prompts to cheaper models, including more open-source models out of Chinese labs, to cut costs. Larridin is built for exactly the gap that opened up after that pivot: once saving money and proving ROI became the priority, companies needed a third-party auditing layer that wasn't tied to any single model vendor.

### What investors are betting on

Pelion Ventures partner Jeff Kearl, who led the round, comes from a consumer-brand background (he founded and ran Skullcandy and Stance), not a typical enterprise-software pedigree. His logic for this bet is closer to a pattern-match: whenever a lot of money suddenly flows into a new category, someone builds a third-party auditing platform for it. That's a pattern Fradin knows from experience — before Larridin, he worked at Comscore, the media-and-advertising measurement company, and he explicitly compares Larridin to Comscore, Nielsen, and Datadog for the AI era. a16z partner Alex Rampell frames it as the need for an "AI boss" — something that tells employees what they should and shouldn't be doing with AI, instead of letting everyone use it however they like with nobody tracking whether it's actually working.

### Numbers worth watching

- Larridin's enterprise customer count grew from 1 to 50 in eleven months — the main evidence behind this round's size
- The jump from a $17M seed to a $30M Series A is about 1.76x, a steady step-up rather than a frothy re-up
- A 20-person team currently serves 50 enterprise customers — a high leverage ratio, and the main reason the company plans to roughly double headcount to 40 after this raise
- The existing AI cost-management market is already split into two layers: infrastructure (CloudZero, Vantage, which track GPU and cloud compute spend) and API usage (LangSmith, Helicone, which track token consumption). Larridin sits a layer above both — auditing AI-driven productivity inside the enterprise — a niche with no direct competitor yet

## Watchlist status

Larridin isn't on the watchlist yet. Recommend adding it under section B6 (Agent Observability / Evaluation), with a caveat: the companies currently in B6 (Arize, Braintrust, Langfuse, AgentOps, etc.) all serve engineers doing development and debugging observability. Larridin's audience is management and HR, and what it measures is the productivity of AI usage by people and agents inside a company — a distinct sub-category within the same ring, one worth splitting out on its own if more companies like it show up.

## Today's takeaway

Larridin's growth curve lines up almost exactly with the inflection point in corporate AI-spending attitudes — and the sequence is the same one cloud spending went through: buy first, audit later. What's really valuable here isn't the auditing tool itself, but the timing of when companies finally became willing to pay for it. Before Q2, this kind of demand barely existed; after Q2, the business took off — a reminder that for infrastructure-layer startups like this, raising at the right moment often matters more than the product itself.
