## Region: Latin America

This series last covered Latin America on September 25 — a seven-week gap that happens to straddle three clear storylines this week: Chile producing both a "policy in force" milestone and a still-pending "law under review" at the same time, AWS putting real money on the ground in Chile, and Argentina grabbing headlines with tax incentives and legal innovation. Lined up together, the gap between "who's talking" and "who's building" in Latin America's AI landscape comes into sharp focus.

## Key Developments This Week

### Chile's National AI Policy Takes Effect, But the Binding AI Bill Just Entered Its Amendment Deadline

Chile's Ministry of Science-led National Artificial Intelligence Policy (Política Nacional de Inteligencia Artificial) was confirmed this week by AI-governance tracker Regulations.ai as officially "in force," giving public bodies and businesses a national framework for responsible AI adoption. ([Regulations.ai](https://regulations.ai/news/chiles-ai-policy-is-in-force-what-teams-should-do))

But this is a non-binding strategic document, not a law. Chile's actually binding, risk-tiered AI bill modeled on the EU AI Act (Boletín 16821-19) is still in its second reading in the Senate; the governing coalition did not submit the key amendment expected by observers, so the committee set December 4 as the deadline for amendments — and if the text changes, it still has to go through a third reading. In other words, "policy in force" and "law passed" are two different things, and the latter's timeline remains uncertain. ([Parvel bill tracker](https://parlamento.ai/cl/bill/16821-19) · [Duplos](https://duplos.cl/actualidad/inteligencia-artificial-propuesta-ley-comision-futuro-sala-15/284441/2026/11/04/) · [Fundación Progresa](https://fundacionprogresa.cl/ley-inteligencia-artificial-chile/))

### AWS Commits $4 Billion to Open Latin America's Third Data-Center Region, in Chile

AWS announced on November 11 that its "AWS South America (Chile) Region" has opened to select customers — the company's first infrastructure region in Chile and its third in Latin America (after São Paulo and Mexico), with general availability expected by the end of 2026. The investment, first announced in May 2025, totals more than $4 billion; AWS estimates it will contribute over $7.5 billion to Chile's GDP and support an average of about 4,100 full-time jobs a year. ([Amazon's official announcement](https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/aws/aws-south-america-chile-region-launch) · [PPC Land](https://ppc.land/aws-opens-chile-cloud-region-to-select-customers-backed-by-4bn/))

### Argentina Pitches a 'Non-Human Company' Tax Break — With a Tenth of Chile's Data-Center Capacity

Argentine President Javier Milei reiterated his AI investment pitch this week: a corporate tax cut from 25% to 15% for AI and other emerging-industry projects investing at least $1 billion, branded around a "no AI regulation" pledge aimed at turning Patagonia into a data-center hub. The tax break has not yet passed the Senate and isn't law; it extends a bill Milei submitted to Congress back in May that would let fully AI-agent-run "non-human companies" operate without a human board. ([Euronews](https://www.euronews.com/2026/11/02/argentina-courts-ai-data-centres-with-no-regulation-and-tax-breaks) · [Superpower Daily](https://superpowerdaily.com/posts/milei-courts-ai-data-centers-with-a-no-regulation-pledge-and-proposed-tax-cuts))

But there's a visible gap between the pitch and the actual infrastructure: per the Financial Times, Argentina currently has only about 40MW of data-center capacity, versus roughly 400MW in Chile — a tenfold difference. International investors in Latin America have so far more often picked Uruguay, Mexico, or Brazil over Argentina. ([Traders Union](https://tradersunion.com/news/companies/show/4148731-patagonia-data-centres-ai-argentina/))

### Medellín Hosts the Horasis Global Summit, Putting Latin America's Talent Story on the Table

More than 1,000 business, government, and investment figures gathered in Medellín, Colombia from November 10–13 for the Horasis Global Summit, themed "Building Tomorrow," with an agenda spanning AI governance and the debate over "where humans fit as AI agents gain more autonomy." Medellín itself had already drawn $216 million in startup funding in 2026, up 38% year over year — the city's choice as host is itself part of Latin America's talent and urban-transformation narrative. ([IBTimes](https://www.ibtimes.com/horasis-summit-medellin-ai-risks-autonomy-3809656) · [Sociable](https://sociable.co/business/horasis-global-summit-to-bring-ai-governance-and-startup-investment-debates-to-medellin/))

## Deep Dive

I think the "factor conditions" dimension of Porter's Diamond Model is the best lens for comparing Chile and Argentina this week, because both countries are courting the same pool of AI infrastructure investors — but with completely different factor bundles.

Chile's factor conditions combine real physical infrastructure with emerging regulatory certainty: roughly 400MW of existing data-center capacity, AWS putting a real $4 billion behind Latin America's third regional data center, plus a national AI policy already in force and an AI bill that, while not yet passed, has a clear timeline (the December 4 amendment deadline). Even without a final law, investors at least know roughly when and in which direction the rules will settle.

Argentina's factor conditions, by contrast, are mostly "policy rhetoric plus legal innovation, layered over scarce physical conditions": Milei's proposed tax cut (25% to 15%) and the "non-human company" legal concept are bold, but the infrastructure underneath is only a tenth of Chile's capacity, and neither the tax bill nor the corporate-law amendment has passed Congress yet. For a cloud provider ready to commit capital to infrastructure today, Chile's "factor conditions already in place" is clearly more compelling than Argentina's "factor conditions still being talked about" — which helps explain why Latin America's third data-center region landed in Chile, not Argentina.

Colombia's Medellín, meanwhile, occupies a different corner of the Diamond Model — "related and supporting industries." It doesn't lead the region in data-center capacity or policy headlines, but hosting the Horasis summit, its $216 million in 2026 startup funding, and its young-talent narrative fill a gap that neither Chile nor Argentina covers: ecosystem visibility. Together, these three countries sketch out three distinct paths currently dividing up Latin America's AI landscape: hard infrastructure (Chile), policy rhetoric first (Argentina), and talent-and-visibility building (Colombia).

## Takeaways for Taiwanese Entrepreneurs

- **If you're building AI compliance or regtech tools**: Chile is the real date to watch — once the December 4 amendment deadline passes, the actual direction of Boletín 16821-19's text will become clearer, which is a far more actionable signal for positioning compliance-consulting or content-review services than a headline about a "national AI policy" taking effect. Don't let "policy in force" get mistaken for "AI law passed."
- **If you're in data-center supply chains (servers, racks, cooling systems)**: Taiwanese suppliers already serving global cloud providers — TSMC, Quanta, QCT — can treat AWS's Chile region buildout (targeting full availability by end of 2026) as a leading indicator of Latin American cloud procurement demand, rather than waiting for Argentina's investment pitch to turn into actual tenders.
- **If you're sequencing Latin American market entry**: Argentina's tax numbers look attractive on paper, but with a tenth of Chile's data-center capacity and no passed legislation, the execution risk is far higher than the headlines suggest. Entering infrastructure-ready markets like Chile or Brazil first, then watching Argentina's bills progress, is the more prudent order.
- **If you're considering distributed teams or an overseas talent hub**: Medellín's 38% year-over-year jump in 2026 startup funding signals a growing young AI talent pool — worth adding to Chile and Brazil as a third option when Taiwanese teams evaluate remote collaboration or a regional talent base.

## Key Insight

I used to assume a headline like "Chile's national AI policy takes effect" meant Chile's AI regulatory environment was settled. Cross-checking Regulations.ai's "in force" announcement against Boletín 16821-19's Senate review status showed otherwise: "policy in force" refers to a non-binding strategic framework, while the actually binding AI bill will only become clearer after the December 4 amendment deadline. That gap is itself a reminder: verbs like "in force" or "passed" in Latin American AI news need to be checked against whether the subject is a policy, a bill, or an executive decree — the three carry entirely different weight. The bigger surprise is that Argentina's tax-cut and "non-human company" rhetoric generated far more noise this week than Chile's, yet it was Chile that actually landed AWS's real capital. Right now, the true dividing line in Latin America's AI landscape is factor conditions like data-center capacity and regulatory certainty — not whose policy headline is louder.
