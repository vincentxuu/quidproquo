## What the company does

Numa builds an AI operating system for car dealerships — its argument is that the traditional DMS (Dealer Management System) was only ever built to *record* what happens at a dealership, never to *run* it. Numa wants to be the layer that runs it.

The product started as customer-service automation: answering calls, texts and chats, auto-booking service appointments, and chasing down missed calls. This year it expanded across the whole store — Numa acquired SocketTime in August to fold service scheduling into the same system; Numa for Sales, launched this month, is the first agent with what the company calls "Inventory Fluency," knowing every car on the lot down to trim and mileage and pivoting to another vehicle the moment the one a buyer asked about sells; Operator, launched in September, works like a smart switchboard for the dealership and has climbed above 95% routing accuracy; LiveCSI monitors customer interactions in real time and flags satisfaction risks before they show up in a survey.

Numa now serves more than 1,500 dealerships across the US and Canada — up from 600 in October 2024, a 2.5x increase in just over two years — and has handled over a billion calls, texts and chats, booking more than 4 million appointments. More notably, it has won OEM-level vendor status from all three major Detroit automakers: GM named Numa an iMR Approved Turnkey Vendor, and more than 240 GM dealerships now run on it, where reply times dropped from 23 hours to 24 minutes; FordDirect picked Numa as its strategic AI vendor, opening access to more than 3,000 Ford and Lincoln dealerships; and Stellantis approved Numa on its MarketCenter, opening the door to 2,600 US and 440 Canadian dealerships.

## What this funding signals

### What it means for the agent ecosystem

This is another data point for vertical AI agents moving from customer-service point solutions toward end-to-end operating systems. The new capital is mostly going toward hiring more engineers to accelerate pulling customer service, sales, repair scheduling and customer intelligence into a single system — which suggests the next phase of competition in vertical agents will shift from "who answers the phone faster" to "who can consolidate an entire store's workflow into one system."

### What investors are betting on

Blue Cloud Ventures, a growth-stage fund focused on AI software, infrastructure and cybersecurity with a portfolio of more than 55 companies, led the round on the thesis that Numa's deep understanding of dealership culture and workflow is a moat that's hard to replicate by bolting generic AI features onto existing tools. The more unusual name in the syndicate is Tokio Marine Future Fund, an insurer's strategic fund — its executive explicitly frames the dealership as a critical touchpoint in the vehicle-ownership journey, betting that a more efficient AI-driven touchpoint also means better risk data and less friction on the insurance side. Google's Gradient Ventures joining the round extends its existing pattern of backing sales-oriented agents (such as Rox).

## Today's takeaway

Most vertical AI agents grow bottom-up — pilot a handful of stores, then expand gradually. This round shows Numa doing the opposite: lock in OEM-level vendor status first, then use that credential to batch-convert thousands of dealerships in one move. For vertical-agent founders, the supplier relationship itself may end up deciding who scales faster than product experience at the individual-store level does.
