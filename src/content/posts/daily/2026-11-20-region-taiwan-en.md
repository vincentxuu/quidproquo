## Region: Taiwan

This series last covered Taiwan on October 2 — a seven-week gap, the largest coverage gap of any region this week. That window happens to straddle a consequential shift in export controls affecting Taiwanese semiconductor firms. Three storylines this week, read together: who the US drew inside its "trusted" line, who's putting real capital behind Taiwan's supply chain, and whether agentic AI adoption is actually showing up in the numbers.

## Key Developments This Week

### New US Rules Split Taiwan's Chipmakers: MediaTek and Realtek Make the Approved List, TSMC Told to Tighten China Orders

New US Commerce Department rules took effect on November 19, and Reuters reports TSMC has begun tightening advanced-process orders from Chinese chip designers, with 16nm and more advanced nodes now a hard line — some previously permitted orders have been suspended. Notably, the US government also published a list of approved chip designers that includes Nvidia, AMD, Apple, and Taiwan's MediaTek and Realtek — but no mainland Chinese firm. ([Reuters via Yahoo Finance](https://ca.finance.yahoo.com/news/tsmc-tightens-china-ai-chip-124653283.html) · [Taipei Times](https://www.taipeitimes.com/News/biz/archives/2026/11/20/2003868041))

That means Taiwanese companies on the same supply chain are now being explicitly sorted by the US's screening regime: MediaTek and Realtek's chip-design businesses are deemed "trusted," while TSMC, as a foundry, bears the added cost of stricter order screening over uncertainty about whether its capacity could flow to restricted customers. The dividing line isn't nationality — it's a company's role (designer vs. foundry) in the chain.

### Nvidia and Alphabet Subscribe to MediaTek's $3.9 Billion Convertible Bond, Funding AI ASIC Expansion

Reuters and multiple outlets report MediaTek priced a $3.9 billion zero-interest overseas convertible bond on August 31, with Nvidia subscribing $3.5 billion of it; Google parent Alphabet also participated (amount undisclosed). The proceeds fund wafer and component purchases as MediaTek expands from mobile chips into custom AI accelerators (ASICs), along with the data-center capacity that business requires. ([Yahoo Finance](https://finance.yahoo.com/technology/ai/articles/tsmc-reportedly-setting-credit-limits-133700118.html) · [ShiftDelete](https://shiftdelete.net/en/tsmc-ai-chip-customers-pay-upfront) · [respawnfirst](https://respawnfirst.com/jensen-huang-circular-financing-tsmc-demands-cash-upfront-ai-startups/))

What's notable about the structure: Nvidia didn't just place an order — it became a bondholder (convertible to equity), effectively locking in a key supply-chain partner with capital rather than relying solely on traditional purchase agreements. The same week, Taiwan's Commercial Times reported that committed and planned funding from Nvidia, Google, Amazon, Micron, and other global tech giants into Taiwan's supply chain now totals more than NT$350 billion (roughly $11 billion).

### Appier's Q3 Earnings: Agentic AI Adoption Lifts Quarterly Gross Profit 38% Year Over Year

Appier (TSE: 4180) reported Q3 2026 results on November 13: revenue of JPY 13.9 billion, up 21.8% year over year, a record high; operating income rose 104.7% to JPY 2.1 billion, with operating margin up 6.2 points to 15.2%. The company's own announcement attributed the growth to "agentic AI adoption" — quarterly gross profit per employee reached a record JPY 11.3 million, up 45% year over year. ([PR Newswire](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/appier-doubles-operating-income-on-record-revenue-and-margins-raises-fy26-guidance-and-dividend-forecast-302938814.html) · [Yahoo Finance](https://finance.yahoo.com/technology/ai/articles/appier-doubles-operating-income-record-084900743.html))

The company also raised its full-year guidance and dividend forecast, noting the profit growth came mainly from operating-efficiency gains — gross profit rose 38% while operating expenses grew only 26% — rather than cuts to R&D spending. It's one of the few Taiwan cases where a public earnings report, rather than a vendor's own claimed efficiency figure, verifies that agentic AI is actually improving gross-margin structure.

## Deep Dive

I think the "bargaining power" and "barriers to entry" dimensions of Porter's Five Forces are the best lens for this week's three stories together, because what they describe is the same underlying shift: bargaining power across Taiwan's semiconductor supply chain is moving in two directions at once.

On barriers to entry: the US export-control whitelist is, in effect, an artificially constructed entry threshold via state screening — MediaTek and Realtek cleared it because of their "chip designer" role, while TSMC, as a foundry, now bears extra screening costs. This barrier wasn't built by technology or capital; it was drawn by geopolitics, and the line it draws isn't "Taiwan" as a whole — it's each company's specific role in the chain.

On buyer bargaining power: Nvidia and Alphabet directly subscribing to MediaTek's convertible bond is the buyer (cloud hyperscalers) reaching upstream to lock in capacity with capital, rather than passively waiting on order lead times. That actually narrows MediaTek's own bargaining room as a supplier — the money came in, but the cost is that its biggest customer is now also a creditor, adding a layer of capital entanglement to every future capacity-allocation and pricing conversation.

Appier's case is a different story entirely: it's not in the semiconductor supply chain at all, and what it's experiencing is agentic AI acting directly as a productivity tool on its own cost structure — not an external force from geopolitics or capital structure. Put the three together, and Taiwan experienced three entirely different layers of AI impact this week, simultaneously and unrelated to each other: being re-sorted by a screening regime, having its bargaining power locked in by capital, and improving its own margins through AI tooling. That's Taiwan's actual position in this round of AI supply-chain realignment — not a single unified narrative, but fragmented impacts happening at different companies and different layers at once.

## Takeaways for Taiwanese Entrepreneurs

- **If your product depends on a specific chip-design partner or foundry**: check what role your partner holds on the US whitelist — "designer" or "foundry." This week shows that two Taiwanese companies can face very different levels of screening scrutiny depending on their role; don't assume "Taiwan supply chain" is a single, uniform risk tier.
- **If you're in data-center or AI-infrastructure supply chains**: Nvidia and Alphabet subscribing to a convertible bond, rather than simply placing orders, is a leading indicator — hyperscalers are now willing to lock in upstream capacity with capital. Taiwan's secondary supply-chain vendors (servers, thermal, substrates) should watch for similar capital-lock-in moves spreading downstream, rather than waiting for order volumes alone to shift.
- **If you're doing enterprise SaaS or AI-transformation consulting**: Appier's public earnings — not a vendor's self-reported efficiency claim — verify that "agentic AI adoption" can produce measurable gross-margin improvement. It's a rare local reference case you can cite with specific numbers (38% gross-profit growth, 45% gross-profit-per-employee growth) the next time you're pitching a Taiwanese enterprise client, instead of a vague "AI can boost efficiency."

## Key Insight

I used to assume US export controls would hit Taiwanese companies roughly uniformly — they're all Taiwanese, so they should move together. Seeing MediaTek and Realtek clear the whitelist while TSMC was told to tighten supply made clear that the screening regime actually draws its line by a company's role in the supply chain, not by nationality — two Taiwanese firms can face very different levels of scrutiny depending on whether they design chips or fabricate them. The other surprise was Nvidia's convertible-bond subscription to MediaTek: at first glance it looked like a routine supply-chain financing arrangement, but cross-referencing it against the Commercial Times' total figure for funds flowing into Taiwan's supply chain revealed it as a deliberate move by hyperscalers to inject capital upstream in exchange for capacity assurance — quietly tilting the bargaining-power balance toward the buyer side.
