## What the company does

nextesy automates bookkeeping and back-office administration for small and midsize businesses — the goal is to stop Swiss SMEs, freelancers, and fiduciary firms from manually handling invoices, expense reports, and closings.

The core product is a bookkeeping platform built on AI from the ground up: it automatically classifies receipts, matches bank transactions, and generates reports, with the company claiming automated classification already covers more than 60% of entries. The next step is handing increasingly complex work to AI agents, with the stated end goal being a fully automated year-end close. nextesy positions itself directly against bexio, Switzerland's dominant incumbent accounting software — its site even runs a head-to-head comparison page and offers a CHF 1/month switching tariff to cover the notice period customers owe their previous provider.

The company spent roughly two years in stealth after its September 2023 founding before launching publicly at the end of April 2026. Growth since then has been fast: it now counts more than 2,000 customers and made the top 20 of Slush 100 at Slush in Helsinki, one of the world's major startup conferences.

## What this round signals

### What it means for the agent ecosystem

Bookkeeping is one of the rare back-office jobs where the rules are clear but the steps are tedious — a decade of SaaS tools (bexio among them) has automated parts of it, yet the judgment-heavy steps (classification, reconciliation, closing) have still needed a human to sign off. nextesy's round signals that agentizing financial back-office work isn't a rip-and-replace move: it starts from "60% auto-classification" and gradually hands judgment-heavy steps to agents, saving the year-end close — the step that needs the most human review — for last. That mirrors the pattern seen in legal and customer-service agents: handle high-frequency small tasks first, then expand into harder ones.

### What investors are betting on

Swisscom Ventures is the corporate venture arm of Swiss telecom incumbent Swisscom, and it has long invested in Swiss enterprise software with strategic intent as much as financial return. The logic behind leading nextesy's round is straightforward: managing partner Stefan Kuentz's public comment was that SMEs spend many times more running their bookkeeping than they spend on the software for it — in other words, the bet is on how much of that spend nextesy can shift from labor to software subscriptions. Going from a $3.03M pre-seed in February 2025 to $10.4M just 21 months later is a 3.4x jump, suggesting both existing backers and new entrant Swisscom Ventures are underwriting the signal from 2,000+ paying customers, not just an early-stage concept.

### Numbers worth watching

- $3.03M pre-seed (February 2025) to $10.4M seed (November 2026): a 3.4x jump in 21 months, faster than the typical fundraising cadence for bookkeeping SaaS.
- Dealroom puts this round in the 97th percentile of all-time seed rounds for Swiss enterprise software, out of 305 comparable deals — a notably large seed round for a Swiss AI startup. EY data shows roughly a third of 2025 Swiss startup funding went to AI companies, but the first half of 2026 lacked large AI rounds, making this one of the exceptions in the second half.
- Crossing 2,000 customers about seven months after public launch with a 40+ person team — customer growth clearly outpacing headcount growth, which is concrete evidence for an automation story rather than a disguised outsourcing play.

## Watchlist status

nextesy is not yet on the watchlist. Recommend adding it to section D6 (Financial Services / Insurance), alongside Taktile, Aveni, and Gradient Labs, with tracking focused on how far its agents progress into complex judgment calls, and whether it can expand beyond Switzerland into other European markets.

## Today's takeaway

Most bookkeeping SaaS products sell "automation" as the headline. nextesy's pitch is more precise: it openly admits it's only at 60% auto-classification today, then sets "fully automated year-end close" as an explicit endpoint, with agents filling the gap step by step. Honestly marking the current limits of automation and then using agents to push the boundary forward — rather than claiming "fully AI-powered bookkeeping" outright — is an easier sell to business customers (especially cautious Swiss SMEs) weighing the cost of switching.
