On October 15, CommonWealth Magazine (《天下雜誌》) ran [a piece](https://www.cw.com.tw/article/5143302) titled "Charlie Munger: To Get Rich, First Save $100,000," adapted from [TheStreet](https://www.thestreet.com/investing/charlie-munger-investing-advice-save-100000-then-ease-off-the-gas) and Yahoo Finance. Its claim: Munger thought the first $100,000 is the hardest, and after that compounding starts doing the work.

If you want to quote this in your own writing or a presentation, this post answers three questions: what Munger actually said, whether the best-known line has a source, and what $100,000 is worth today.

## Summary: what holds up and what doesn't

| Claim in the article | Status |
|---|---|
| Munger said the first $100,000 is the hardest | Primary source: 1999 shareholder meeting transcript |
| People who get there fast are rational, opportunistic, and spend far less than they earn | Primary source: same passage |
| "Walk everywhere, buy food only with coupons" | No primary source found; treat as folklore |
| The remarks came from a late-1990s meeting | Only the 1999 passage is sourced |
| $100,000 in 1995 ≈ $210,000 in 2025 | Matches official CPI data |
| 2025 US median household income was $87,460 | Matches the [Census Bureau release](https://www.census.gov/library/publications/2026/demo/p60-289.html) |

## The 1999 original

The source is the [1999 Berkshire Hathaway annual meeting, afternoon session transcript](https://buffett.cnbc.com/video/1999/05/03/afternoon-session---1999-berkshire-hathaway-annual-meeting.html). A shareholder asked Buffett how, starting out in his early 30s today, he could make $30 billion. Buffett answered "Start young," talked about thinking for yourself, and then handed over to Munger:

> The hard part of the process for most people is the first $100,000. If you have a standing start at zero, getting together $100,000 is a long struggle for most people. And I would argue that the people who get there relatively quickly are helped if they're passionate about being rational, very eager and opportunistic, and steadily underspend their income grossly.

There is no "ease off the gas" here, and nothing about walking or coupons.

## Where the viral version comes from

The version that circulates widely reads:

> The first $100,000 is a b****, but you gotta do it. I don't care what you have to do — if it means walking everywhere and not eating anything that wasn't purchased with a coupon, find a way to get your hands on $100,000. After that, you can ease off the gas a little bit.

I read the CNBC archive transcripts for the 1995–1999 meetings (10 sessions; for the 1995 morning session only the first half was retrievable). None contains this passage. The earliest version I could trace is a 2017 personal-finance blog post. The author writes that Munger said "The first $100,000 is a b\*tch, but you gotta do it," then admits:

> I couldn't find the exact words he used to follow up this statement, but it was something like: …

So "walking, coupons, ease off the gas" is the blogger's recollection of roughly what was said. It was later presented as a verbatim quote by [Wikiquote](https://en.wikiquote.org/wiki/Charlie_Munger) (dated 1998), Yahoo Finance, and TheStreet. My guess is that the viral text is the 1999 remark made punchier, but I have no evidence that Munger never said something similar elsewhere.

## What $100,000 is worth today

The CommonWealth figures follow TheStreet. I recomputed them with the Minneapolis Fed's [annual average CPI table](https://www.minneapolisfed.org/about-us/monetary-policy/inflation-calculator/consumer-price-index-1913-) and they match:

| Base year | CPI that year | In 2025 dollars (CPI 321.9) |
|---|---|---|
| 1995 | 152.4 | about $211,000 |
| 1998 | 163.0 | about $197,000 |
| 1999 | 166.6 | about $193,000 |

The sourced remark is from 1999, so "about $193,000" is the closer figure than $210,000. With 2025 US median household income at $87,460, that is a little over two years of pre-tax household income.

The compounding illustrations in the article also check out: saving $10,000 at each year-end takes 10 years with no return, about 9 years at 4%, and about 8 years at 7%. A $100,000 portfolio at 7% grows to about $197,000, $387,000 and $761,000 after 10, 20 and 30 years. These are fixed-return arithmetic, not forecasts.

## Limits

- I did not read the 1994 meeting, meetings after 1999, Wesco or Daily Journal annual meetings, or interviews. The viral line could come from one of those; I found no evidence either way.
- The 1999 point is to underspend your income steadily, not to live at the extreme. The CommonWealth piece also notes that income, rent and debt change how fast anyone can save.
- CPI conversion only tracks prices. It does not mean $100,000 in 1999 and $193,000 today buy the same life.

## How to quote it

1. To quote Munger, use the 1999 passage and link the [transcript](https://buffett.cnbc.com/video/1999/05/03/afternoon-session---1999-berkshire-hathaway-annual-meeting.html).
2. To use the walking-and-coupons line, call it "a widely circulated saying," without quotation marks or a year.
3. When you convert the amount, name the base year: "$100,000 in 1999 is about $193,000 in 2025 dollars."
