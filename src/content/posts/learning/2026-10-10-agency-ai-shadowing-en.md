You have tools that can turn an idea into a prototype, but you are still waiting for someone to tell you whether it belongs to your job. [Max Schoening’s conversation on Lenny’s Podcast](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/why-cultivating-agency-matters-more) raises a useful question: as skills become easier to access, what makes someone actually start?

His answer is agency: the capacity to take action and influence a situation. This guide explores that idea, then offers three 30–60-second clips with bilingual comprehension notes. Listen to the original audio, practice each complete clip, and use the idea to describe something you could change at work.

## Access to skills still leaves a decision to act

In the [discussion beginning at 10:32](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mCO-D3pkviM&t=632s), Max argues that AI assistance makes another difference more visible: whether people see their surroundings as changeable and take initiative.

Imagine a team manually assembling the same report every week. You could ask whose responsibility it is. You could also identify the slowest step, make a small example, and discuss it with the person who owns the report.

That is my application of the idea. You do not have to take over the entire process. You can turn a vague complaint into a proposal people can inspect, test, and discuss.

## A prototype helps you understand the thing you are designing

In the [discussion beginning at 8:24](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mCO-D3pkviM&t=504s), Max connects prototyping in code with understanding the medium of a product.

A static picture of an AI conversation leaves many questions unanswered. What happens while the system is waiting? How does it handle a retry? Can a user interrupt a tool call? A working prototype exposes behaviors that a picture cannot show.

This is why I connect agency with small experiments. Building a small piece can turn uncertainty into specific questions: what information is missing, what behavior needs testing, and who should help decide the next step?

## Initiative needs room for review and handoff

The [chapter beginning at 13:52](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mCO-D3pkviM&t=832s) raises the question of what merging roles might cost us. That prompts a practical question of my own: after a prototype works, who will address reliability, scale, and design details?

My takeaway is to move a problem forward while keeping validation and handoff in view. For the report example, does the prototype use sensitive data? Who will notice an error? Is the existing owner willing to maintain it? Those questions belong in the proposal.

Try this tonight: pick one recurring problem, describe the current process, and make an alternative small enough to compare. Show both versions and ask which is closer to what the other person needs.

**Short quotation from the original captions:**

> the thing that matters is agency and I don't think agency is very evenly distributed in the world

The point is that initiative matters and varies between people. This quotation locates the argument; the exercise covers all 40 seconds, including the explanation and contrast around it.

## Put it into your own work

The following are **original practice sentences written for this article, not quotes from Max**. Use them for reading aloud, substitution, and describing your situation. Use the previous section to practice with the speaker’s audio.

| English practice sentence | Chinese meaning |
|---|---|
| I can build a small prototype and get feedback. | 我可以做一個小原型，再請人給回饋。 |
| The thing that matters is whether this solves the problem. | 真正重要的是，這能不能解決問題。 |
| We can change the process without replacing the whole system. | 我們可以調整流程，不用把整套系統換掉。 |
| I want to understand how this works before we ship it. | 我想先了解它怎麼運作，再把它上線。 |
| Let's try a small change and see what happens. | 我們先試一個小改動，看看結果。 |

Choose a real problem. Replace *a small prototype* with something concrete, such as *a report template*. Add what you want to check: *I want to know whether it saves the team time.*（我想知道它能不能替團隊省時間。）

## Choosing a passage to practice

Start by trying a coherent 30–60-second passage. This is a practical starting suggestion, not a research-established optimum. If it feels overwhelming, reduce it to one sentence.

Look for four things:

- **A complete idea:** choose natural pauses at both ends.
- **Meaning you can follow:** after listening and checking the captions, you can explain the passage in Chinese. Skip passages that require looking up almost every word.
- **Useful language:** you can adapt at least one expression to your own work or life.
- **Clear audio:** begin with one speaker, few interruptions, and no music obscuring the voice.

This article already selects three continuous clips lasting 40, 48, and 40 seconds. Begin with the first, then choose the workplace tradeoff or concept-and-analogy clip. If necessary, imitate individual sentences before returning to the complete passage.

Finally, check whether you can explain the meaning without the text and adapt an expression into your own sentence. If you can only chase the sounds, shorten the passage or choose something easier.

## Practice with the audio

Choose one clip above and listen to its full 30–60 seconds without captions. Listen again with English CC and the comprehension notes. Then pause and imitate individual sentences. Once comfortable, play the complete clip and speak slightly behind the original audio.

If listening and speaking simultaneously feels overwhelming, stay with phrase-by-phrase imitation. See the site’s [guide to shadowing and echo practice](/posts/learning/2026-10-08-shadowing-evidence-en) for more context.

Record one sentence, listen for where you hesitate, and practice that part again. Finally, close the video and answer in your own English: what would you like to change this week, and what could your first step be?

The useful question to carry into your day is concrete: with the tools already available to you, what could you begin improving?
