## What this series covers

**Do examples, diagrams, and questions necessarily make a teaching article work better?** Most writing advice is experience: give more examples, draw more diagrams, add practice questions. This series takes a different angle and looks at learning research from the writer's side: it takes the concrete decisions you face when writing a teaching article and checks what studies actually compared, what the comparison group did, whether they measured understanding or preference, how large the effects were, and under what conditions they held.

The sentence you meet most often in the series is "no study has tested this." Most evidence comes from classrooms, labs, and online courses (MOOCs); very few studies look directly at one person reading a web article alone. So the series is useful not as a list of techniques guaranteed to work, but as a way to see how much evidence sits behind each technique and how far its conditions are from your readers.

## How the series is arranged

The series has 30 articles, numbered 0 to 29. Articles 1 to 28 move outward from the page in seven parts, and article 29 uses this site as a case:

1. **What is on the page** (articles 1–4): how to write examples, diagrams, code, and terminology.
2. **Media readers must click, watch, or operate** (articles 5–7): whether video and interactive figures are worth including.
3. **Asking readers to act** (articles 8–11): practice, questions, and projects, and whether readers actually do them.
4. **Who the readers are** (articles 12–15): prior knowledge, misconceptions, motivation, and whether readers know when they have understood.
5. **Time** (articles 16–19): what remains days or weeks later, and whether readers come back.
6. **Readers are not alone** (articles 20–24): peers, comments, writing for others, and who cannot read the article at all.
7. **How to know it worked** (articles 25–28): what the writer's signals, experiments, re-explaining existing courses, and AI-written materials can and cannot show.
8. **This site as a case** (article 29): what each check in this site's writing and review process can and cannot show.

The answer to one part usually raises the next part's question: once the page is written, will readers look at the media? If they look, will they act? Who are the readers who act? How much do they remember afterward? Is anyone else with them? And finally, how does the writer know whether any of it worked?

## 1. What is on the page (articles 1–4)

This part asks how to write the four kinds of representation on a static page, and how far the evidence goes.

The best-supported findings are the three techniques in article 1: a worked example readers can follow to the end (meta-analysis g = 0.48, mathematics only), asking readers to explain it themselves (0.55), and stating common misconceptions in order to refute them (about 0.4, an effect the authors call "modest"). Adding a diagram gives 0.39 for reading comprehension in a meta-analysis, 0.28 after correcting for possible publication bias, and the diagrams in those studies were each designed specifically for one text.

- [Article 1: Examples and openings](/posts/learning/2026-10-13-teaching-text-evidence-en): how to write examples, explanations, and openings with evidence behind them.
- [Article 2: When to draw a diagram](/posts/learning/2026-10-13-diagrams-in-teaching-articles-en): what content deserves a diagram, and which kind.
- [Article 3: How much code to write](/posts/learning/2026-10-14-code-in-teaching-articles-en): how to break up and practice code in an article, and what happens when it doesn't run.
- [Article 4: Terminology, notation, and formulas](/posts/learning/2026-10-14-notation-jargon-terminology-en): how to handle jargon, symbols, and English terms without stalling readers.

## 2. Media readers must click, watch, or operate (articles 5–7)

This part asks whether video and interactive figures, which ask readers for extra effort, are worth including.

All three articles point the same way: what helps is readers doing something related to the content, not the medium itself. In an online experiment with about two thousand people, adding five kinds of interaction to the same figure did not beat the static figure on any of them for answering on the spot (article 7). A separate review of algorithm visualization research found that once the comparison group was also given an activity, the difference disappeared.

- [Article 5: Video in teaching articles](/posts/learning/2026-10-13-instructional-video-in-articles-en): where video goes in an article, and how the article should work with it.
- [Article 6: Laying out video beside text](/posts/learning/2026-10-13-video-beside-text-layout-en): how to arrange video and text after a reader clicks a timestamp.
- [Article 7: Interactive figures](/posts/learning/2026-10-14-interactive-figures-in-teaching-articles-en): whether interactive figures or simulations are worth the effort.

## 3. Asking readers to act (articles 8–11)

This part asks how much of the benefit of practice, questions, and projects, all measured in settings with teachers, classmates, and course credit, survives when one person reads a web article, and whether readers actually do them.

In a meta-analysis of university classes, classes with active learning beat lecture classes by about 0.47 standard deviations, but what was compared was a whole change of teaching method. One experiment speaks directly to whether readers do the tasks: in an online course, the number of people who wrote a three-sentence summary was about half the number who read a ready-made summary and ticked "read" (article 10).

- [Article 8: What "learning by doing" evidence measures](/posts/learning/2026-10-14-active-learning-evidence-en): whether "active learning works better" justifies asking readers to act.
- [Article 9: Questions and feedback](/posts/learning/2026-10-13-teaching-text-questions-feedback-en): how to set questions in the text and how to give answers.
- [Article 10: Do readers answer embedded questions?](/posts/learning/2026-10-14-do-readers-answer-embedded-questions-en): whether readers actually answer, and whether they peek at answers first.
- [Article 11: How much guidance projects need](/posts/learning/2026-10-14-projects-struggle-scaffolding-en): how much to explain before a project, and whether to remove the support.

## 4. Who the readers are (articles 12–15)

This part asks: readers arrive with different prior knowledge, misconceptions, motivations, and judgments of whether they understand, and the writer cannot see them, so who does the same text help or harm?

Materials with more support help readers with low prior knowledge (d = 0.51) and hurt those with high prior knowledge (−0.43) (article 12). Readers also don't know well whether they have understood: without any intervention, the correlation between their judgment of which texts they understood and their actual test scores averages only 0.18 (article 15).

- [Article 12: Writing for readers at different levels](/posts/learning/2026-10-14-prior-knowledge-reader-level-en): what a writer can do without seeing the reader's level.
- [Article 13: Writing about common misconceptions](/posts/learning/2026-10-14-misconceptions-in-teaching-articles-en): who benefits when misconceptions are written out, and whether that plants them.
- [Article 14: Stating usefulness and adding anecdotes](/posts/learning/2026-10-14-utility-interest-seductive-details-en): who benefits from stated usefulness, choices, and interesting asides.
- [Article 15: Do readers know they understood?](/posts/learning/2026-10-14-metacomprehension-in-teaching-articles-en): how accurate readers' judgments of understanding are, and how to improve them.

## 5. Time (articles 16–19)

This part asks what remains days or weeks later: how much of the spacing effect survives outside the lab, whether it can be built into articles, how to order a series, and whether readers are still there. What the articles share is that the writer cannot control when readers return.

In a meta-analysis restricted to real classrooms, spacing practice across days gives about d = 0.54, but most studies are from US schools and they vary widely (article 16). When review questions were built into an online course, about eight in ten learners in one MOOC's experimental group never touched them (article 17).

- [Article 16: Spaced practice in real courses](/posts/learning/2026-10-14-spaced-practice-in-real-courses-en): how much of the lab spacing effect survives in real courses.
- [Article 17: Building spaced review into articles](/posts/learning/2026-10-14-spaced-review-in-teaching-articles-en): whether spaced review can be built into an article, and why it hasn't caught on.
- [Article 18: Ordering a series](/posts/learning/2026-10-14-series-order-in-teaching-articles-en): how to order a series, and whether later articles should reuse earlier ones.
- [Article 19: Why readers don't finish](/posts/learning/2026-10-14-why-readers-dont-finish-en): when readers leave, whether they come back, and how much a writer can change.

## 6. Readers are not alone (articles 20–24)

This part opens up "one reader and one article": how learning together differs from reading alone, and who cannot read the article, or reads it at a disadvantage.

Peer discussion does not necessarily lead to the right answer: in one course, 91% of students who answered wrong and found their neighbor had chosen the same wrong answer stayed wrong (article 20). Screen reader users answered questions about online charts correctly 34% of the time, against 87% for the comparison group; with a data table attached to the chart, they reached 73% (article 23).

- [Article 20: What reading alone misses](/posts/learning/2026-10-14-learning-alone-vs-together-en): what is missing when you read alone, and how much an article can make up.
- [Article 21: Comment sections](/posts/learning/2026-10-14-forums-comments-community-learning-en): whether people who join communities learn better because of the community or because of who chooses to join.
- [Article 22: Writing for others](/posts/learning/2026-10-14-learning-by-teaching-without-audience-en): how much of the benefit of teaching remains without a live audience.
- [Article 23: Who can't read teaching articles](/posts/learning/2026-10-14-accessibility-in-teaching-articles-en): which readers cannot access an article's diagrams, code, and formulas.
- [Article 24: Non-native and mobile readers](/posts/learning/2026-10-14-language-translation-mobile-reading-en): what non-native readers, readers of translations, and phone readers miss.

## 7. How to know it worked (articles 25–28)

This part asks, when the writer has no exam to give: how far reader feedback, platform experiments, rubrics, re-explaining existing courses, and AI drafting can show that an article works. All four articles answer: none of them can show that readers actually learned.

In a crossover experiment in a university physics course, the class where students scored 0.46 standard deviations higher on a test was the one they rated 0.56 lower in a questionnaire that included how much they liked it (article 25). When GPT-3.5 wrote solution hints for algebra and statistics problems directly, about 32% of the problems had wrong answers and working (article 28).

- [Article 26: Testing writing with experiments](/posts/learning/2026-10-14-platform-experiments-rubrics-design-research-en): whether writing choices can be validated with experiments, rubrics, or design research.
- [Article 27: Re-explaining existing courses](/posts/learning/2026-10-14-re-explaining-existing-courses-en): whether it is worth re-explaining a course that already exists.
- [Article 28: AI-written materials and AI tutors](/posts/learning/2026-10-14-ai-written-materials-and-ai-tutors-en): how often AI-written materials are wrong, and how much AI tutors help.

## 8. This site as a case (article 29)

- [Article 29: This site's writing and review process](/posts/learning/2026-10-14-site-case-agents-en): which checks a post goes through before publication, and what each can show. Its conclusion: "passing checks," "fit to publish," and "helps people learn" each need their own evidence; formal compliance is not the same as helping people learn.

## What you can do after reading

- When you meet a piece of writing advice, say how much research supports it: a meta-analysis, a single experiment, or just experience.
- When you read a study, check first what the comparison group did and whether it measured understanding or preference, and judge how far its conditions are from your readers.
- When you revise a draft, know which changes are worth making first (worked examples, asking readers to explain, putting diagrams next to their text) and which demand a lot of effort for little evidence (interactive figures, review schedules built into articles).
- Know that readers saying an article was well written is not learning, and check with a test reader and a task.

## Five conclusions

1. **Few techniques have meta-analytic support, and all come with conditions.** Worked examples (mathematics only), asking readers to explain, refuting misconceptions, adding a diagram (about 0.28 after correction), spacing practice (real classrooms, mostly schools).
2. **Interactive figures by themselves did not beat static ones.** What helps is readers doing something with the content; when the comparison group also had something to do, the difference disappeared. These two points come from different studies but point the same way.
3. **Readers often don't follow your design.** In one interactive article, about 55% of readers never opened the collapsed explanations; people who wrote summaries were about half the number who ticked "read"; about eight in ten never touched the review questions pushed to them. Each figure has only one source, but all point the same way.
4. **Feeling that you understood, or saying it was well written, is not learning.** Judgments of understanding are inaccurate, and ratings can even run opposite to test scores.
5. **Almost all the evidence comes from classrooms, labs, and MOOCs.** One person reading a public article alone, doing a project alone, and deciding alone whether to come back for review: these studies tested none of these. So for every recommendation, check whether the study's conditions resemble your readers.

## Read the comparisons, not just the conclusion

Ask who did what, what the comparison group did, and whether the study measured understanding, memory, task performance, or preference. Operating a simulation and clicking to reveal an answer can both be called interaction while being entirely different activities; test scores and reader ratings also measure different things. Each article lists the original studies at the end, and articles 1 to 28 also record study designs and numbers in an appendix.

## Use one draft as your working example

Write one sentence stating what readers should be able to do afterward, then pick the most relevant article from the eight parts above. Change one concrete element at a time, such as replacing an abstract explanation with a worked example readers can follow to the end, or asking readers to answer before revealing the answer. Then ask a reader at the intended level to attempt your task and record where they get stuck; to judge whether they learned, look at whether they can explain, answer, or complete the task, not at whether they found it easy to read.
