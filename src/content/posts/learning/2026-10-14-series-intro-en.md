## What this series asks

**Do examples, diagrams, and questions necessarily make a teaching article work better?** This series takes the concrete decisions you face when writing one, such as how to write an example, whether to draw a diagram, how much help to give with a question, and how to order a series, and checks them against what research actually compared, how large the effects were, and under what conditions they held, then turns that into practices you can use in an article.

The series has 30 articles, numbered 0 to 29: this one is the entry point, articles 1 to 28 each cover one kind of writing decision, and article 29 is a case study of how this site uses AI in writing and review.

## Five conclusions from the 29 articles

1. **Few techniques have meta-analytic support, but they are concrete.** Give a worked example readers can follow to the end, ask readers to explain it themselves, and state common misconceptions in order to refute them ([article 1](/posts/learning/2026-10-13-teaching-text-evidence-en)); adding a diagram raises reading comprehension by about 0.39 standard deviations on average ([article 2](/posts/learning/2026-10-13-diagrams-in-teaching-articles-en)); spacing practice across days gives about d = 0.54 in a meta-analysis of real classrooms ([article 16](/posts/learning/2026-10-14-spaced-practice-in-real-courses-en)).
2. **What helps is readers doing something with the material, not components that move.** Adding interactive features to the same figure did not beat the static version in one set of experiments; what worked was readers doing something with the figure, and the gap disappeared when the comparison group also had something to do ([article 7](/posts/learning/2026-10-14-interactive-figures-in-teaching-articles-en)).
3. **Readers often don't follow your design.** About half of desktop readers never opened content placed behind a click (article 7); tasks requiring typed answers were done by about half as many people as tasks requiring a tick ([article 10](/posts/learning/2026-10-14-do-readers-answer-embedded-questions-en)); about eight in ten MOOC learners never touched review questions pushed to them ([article 17](/posts/learning/2026-10-14-spaced-review-in-teaching-articles-en)).
4. **Feeling that you understood, or saying it was well written, is not learning.** Readers' ability to tell which texts they understood correlates only 0.18 on average ([article 15](/posts/learning/2026-10-14-metacomprehension-in-teaching-articles-en)); in a physics course, the class where students scored 0.46 standard deviations higher on a test was the one they rated lower ([article 25](/posts/learning/2026-10-14-reader-signals-vs-learning-en)).
5. **Almost all the evidence comes from classrooms, labs, and MOOCs.** An adult reading a public article alone, doing a project alone, and deciding alone whether to come back for review has barely been studied directly ([article 8](/posts/learning/2026-10-14-active-learning-evidence-en), [article 11](/posts/learning/2026-10-14-projects-struggle-scaffolding-en), [article 20](/posts/learning/2026-10-14-learning-alone-vs-together-en)). So for every writing recommendation, check whether the study's conditions resemble your readers.

## Read the comparisons, not just the conclusion

Ask who did what, what the comparison group did, and whether the study measured understanding, memory, task performance, or preference. Operating a simulation and clicking to reveal an answer can both be called interaction while being entirely different activities; test scores and reader ratings also measure different things.

Each article lists the original studies at the end, and articles 1 to 28 also record study designs and numbers in an appendix. A classroom finding may not apply directly to someone reading selected sections alone; when studies disagree, compare their participants, comparison groups, and measures before deciding what to change.

## Use one draft as your working example

Write one sentence stating what readers should be able to do afterward, then pick the most relevant article from the table. Change one concrete element at a time, such as replacing an abstract explanation with a worked example readers can follow to the end, or asking readers to answer before revealing the answer.

Then ask a reader at the intended level to attempt your task and record where they get stuck. Readability feedback helps improve presentation; evidence of learning also requires an explanation, an answer, or successful task performance (which is what the fourth conclusion is about).
