You enjoy a speaking lesson and the teacher improves a few sentences. The next day, a colleague asks something similar and you return to the original wording. One place to intervene is the review process: connect the lesson to your next conversation instead of simply filing the notes away.

This adjustable routine is intended for working adults in Taiwan. You need a way to record yourself, a real task and someone or something to respond: a teacher, partner or practice tool. Apply it to lessons you already use. If you are choosing a channel, start with the [English Speaking Practice overview](/posts/learning/2026-10-19-english-speaking-practice-overview-en).

The days and durations below are an author-designed starting point, rather than a scientifically validated dose or a promise of a level increase. The approach draws on British Council guidance about [speaking practice](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/level/improve-your-english-level/how-improve-your-english-speaking), [correction](https://www.teachingenglish.org.uk/professional-development/teachers/assessing-learning/error-correction) and a [role-play cycle](https://www.teachingenglish.org.uk/professional-development/teachers/knowing-subject/strategic-repetition-accelerated-speaking-fluency), organised into preparation, classroom work and review.

## Work on one recurring task each week

Name something you need to accomplish, such as explaining a delay to a colleague and proposing an alternative. That is easier to prepare and assess than “improve my business English”.

Write down **who you are talking to, what you need to accomplish and where you tend to get stuck**. If introductions are manageable but follow-up questions are difficult, narrow the task to explaining one piece of work and answering a question about the reason. You do not need to memorise an entire new introduction.

Every lesson need not have a new topic. Use the same situation for a first attempt, a revised attempt and a changed condition. This is a practice arrangement; the decision to move on comes from the checks below, rather than a required number of repetitions.

### A starting weekly schedule

| When | Action | Keep |
|---|---|---|
| Monday, about ten minutes | Record the original attempt and identify one priority | Baseline recording and a one-sentence task goal |
| Tuesday, your scheduled lesson length | Bring the task, answer a follow-up, use feedback and try again | One checked feedback item and a revised recording or summary |
| Wednesday, about ten minutes | Repeat the task without full notes | A second independent attempt and the remaining difficulty |
| Friday, about ten minutes | Change one condition or listener and handle an unprepared follow-up | New-situation recording and whether the task was completed |
| Sunday, about five minutes | Compare the records and decide whether to continue or change tasks | One sentence describing the result and next step |

Move the days or shorten the sessions as needed. If you can practise only twice, keep one attempt with feedback and one changed-condition task, and simplify the records. Missing a day does not require restarting the week.

## Before class: find out what you currently say

Speak once before looking for materials. Record your own explanation and keep the difficult parts. If you save only the polished attempt, there is little baseline to compare later.

Listen for whether the other person could understand your intended action, which necessary information is missing and which follow-up would be difficult. Select one priority rather than transcribing the entire recording.

Prepare a few prompts, such as reason, impact and alternative, instead of a full script. If sentence-building is still difficult, first use a suitable model and gradually reduce support. [LearnEnglish speaking materials](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/speaking) are organised by level and situation. After understanding a model, return to your own task and speak again.

Tell your teacher what you want from the lesson: listen to the attempt, ask a follow-up, identify the point that most affects understanding and let you try again. A message saying only “I want speaking practice” leaves the goal to be guessed from scratch.

## During class: communicate, then select a problem to work on

The [British Council's correction guidance](https://www.teachingenglish.org.uk/professional-development/teachers/assessing-learning/error-correction) distinguishes fluency work from accuracy work and recommends discussing correction preferences. For a round focused on completing a message, ask the teacher to note problems for later. When working on a particular sound or construction, stopping to correct that target can be appropriate.

A lesson can include three actions:

1. **Do the task.** Ask the other person to respond naturally rather than supplying every answer. You need to discover whether you can handle a real question.
2. **Examine feedback.** Establish what caused misunderstanding. Try correcting it yourself before requesting a demonstration.
3. **Do it again.** Apply the correction in another attempt, checking that you understood rather than merely copied the teacher's sentence.

Avoid collecting every small error for immediate correction. A useful selection heuristic is whether a problem causes misunderstanding, recurs or matters to the current task. This is a way to choose a focus, rather than a universal ranking of all errors.

In a group class, a teacher may not be able to restart your whole task. Use the revised expression during pair work or record your own attempt afterwards, then ask about the unresolved part next time.

## After class: write notes that lead to another attempt

Notes should tell you what to practise next. The [official correction article](https://www.teachingenglish.org.uk/professional-development/teachers/assessing-learning/error-correction) suggests recording a mistake, correction and note. Here, situation and next check are added to connect the entry to speaking.

| Situation | Original wording or difficulty | Checked correction | Next check |
|---|---|---|---|
| Discussing an outdoor activity | `It depends of the weather.` | `It depends on the weather.` | Without notes, explain a different arrangement affected by weather |
| Explaining an activity's backup plan | Mentioning a backup without saying when the decision will be made | State the decision time, then the alternative location | Answer a question about when everyone will be informed |

The first row uses a correction from the official article. The second is an author-designed information-structure example. Some difficulties concern missing information rather than grammar.

Keep your actual original wording where possible and record where the feedback came from. If an AI suggestion is something you cannot judge, mark it for checking and consult a teacher or reliable material before practising it. A more polished alternative does not automatically establish that the original was wrong.

### Put the full answer away before repeating the task

The [role-play cycle guidance](https://www.teachingenglish.org.uk/professional-development/teachers/knowing-subject/strategic-repetition-accelerated-speaking-fluency) moves from supported practice towards unscripted performance and comparison. Keep the task prompt and keywords available, cover the corrected script, speak, then compare afterwards.

If you still need keywords, record that the task was completed with prompts. Do not label it independent. Try with one less prompt next time. When nothing comes out, restore a little support instead of forcing repeated failure.

Your own voice is enough for the recording. Obtain agreement and check course rules before recording teachers, partners or classmates. If there is no classroom recording, make a separate solo recording afterwards. This routine does not require a platform to provide transcripts or video.

## Complete example: an outdoor gathering with uncertain weather

This is an original task. You are organising a weekend gathering with friends. A picnic in the park was planned, but the weather is uncertain. Explain a backup so everyone knows when the decision will be made and where they would go instead.

### First attempt: preserve the problem

Your initial recording might reveal two issues: the preposition error in the notes table and an explanation that ends after mentioning an indoor alternative, without saying when it will be confirmed or checking agreement.

Set the goal as **explain how weather affects the arrangement, state a decision time and check whether the listener agrees**. Faster speech, no pauses and more advanced vocabulary do not all need to be targets at once.

### In class: make the listener's response necessary

Have the teacher or partner play a friend. Explain the original plan and alternative, then answer a follow-up such as how everyone will find out whether the plan changes. Ask the listener to summarise what they understood.

If they know the location might change but cannot tell when a decision will be made, supply that information. Check the weather-related expression and explain the whole arrangement again. At the end, the listener should be able to identify the original destination, the condition for changing it and when the result will be known.

### The next day: retell without a script

Keep five prompts: original plan, weather, decision time, backup and confirmation. Record again. If you can complete it, try without the prompts. If one part is missing, review that entry rather than rereading every piece of material.

### Friday: introduce an unprepared condition

Have the listener add that one friend cannot stay out late. Check whether the backup still works, ask about the restriction and propose a feasible arrangement.

Finally, change the setting to an outdoor workshop with colleagues. Keep the communication demands: explain the condition, offer a backup, establish the decision time and check agreement. You can choose different wording and pause to think. You do not need to reproduce the picnic version word for word.

### What counts as completion?

| Result | Observation | Next step |
|---|---|---|
| The familiar task still requires a full script | Without it, essential information disappears | Reduce the message to two or three points and use keywords |
| The familiar task works, but a new condition stops you | The script is familiar but a question breaks the response | Keep the situation, add one question and allow time to answer |
| New conditions are manageable, but the target correction keeps disappearing | The message succeeds while the chosen language point is unstable | Practise that point briefly, then return it to the whole conversation |
| The task works without a full script and you handle a new condition | The listener can summarise and you answer an unprepared question | Record the result and choose a related task |

This is a criterion for the specific task. It does not convert into a formal proficiency level or establish overall fluency from one recording.

## When practice fails, adjust the relevant layer

**You go blank immediately:** shrink the task to one clear need and allow keywords. If more language is needed, find a suitable model or ask the teacher for one, then try the same task.

**Corrections prevent you from finishing:** agree to finish the message before addressing one point. Clarify immediately if the listener no longer understands; maintaining flow is not a reason to preserve an actual misunderstanding.

**A memorised answer does not survive a new topic:** postpone expanding the phrase list. Change the listener, reason or restriction and test the same communication pattern elsewhere. Start with one changed condition in the familiar setting if necessary.

**The notes grow but you never speak again:** select one entry you might use tomorrow, fill in its next check and record an attempt now. The rest of the notebook can wait.

**You have only ten minutes:** do one task, listen to one point and try it again after correction. Add a new condition at the next opportunity. There is no need to make large numbers of recordings to catch up with the table.

## Finish the week with three useful records

Keep a baseline recording, one checked feedback item and one changed-condition attempt. Add a sentence stating what you can do now and what you will work on next. The [British Council guidance on recording and feedback](https://www.teachingenglish.org.uk/professional-development/teachers/knowing-subject/error-correction-2) provides a way to revisit speech and errors; the comparison still needs to concern your own task.

If clarifying a colleague's request is what you need this week, start with that conversation. Connect the lesson, notes and review to the next thing you need to say.
