You want to tell a colleague that you have a cold, your throat hurts, and your voice is fading. At lunch, you also want to ask about the food they brought. Practise one message at a time, then add a question.

Start with the illness group and save lunch for another session. Cover the English, use the Chinese prompt to recall a sentence, then reveal it and try again.

## A cold, a sore throat, and a fading voice

When a colleague asks what is wrong, start with **I have a cold.** [Cambridge's voice entry](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/dictionary/english/voice) uses a cold and losing your voice in the same example. Here, a cold means an illness, rather than feeling cold.

To name the symptom, say **I have a sore throat.** This is an example in [Cambridge's throat entry](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/dictionary/english/throat). [Oxford Online English's illness lesson](https://www.oxfordonlineenglish.com/talk-about-illness-medicine) also includes sore throat in a conversation about symptoms.

You can also say **My throat hurts.** This adapts the body-part-plus-hurts pattern in [Cambridge's hurt entry](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/dictionary/english/hurt). Both sentences identify the discomfort; neither adds a specific degree of pain.

If your voice is fading, say **I have a cold and I think I'm losing my voice.** This comes from the voice entry above. It describes a developing loss of voice and can explain why speaking is difficult.

## Strong pain, barely speaking, and a lost voice

To express strong pain, use **My throat really hurts.** The [throat-symptom lesson from b Watashi no Eikaiwa](https://www.b-cafe.net/newsletter/2026/08/sore-throat-english.php) provides this full sentence.

The same lesson also gives **I can barely speak.** and **I've lost my voice.** The first means speaking is still possible, but only just; the second describes a lost voice. [Oxford Learner's Dictionaries: voice](https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/definition/english/voice_1) also explains losing your voice as being unable to speak.

When a colleague asks what is wrong, choose the sentences that match your condition: I have a cold. My throat really hurts. I can barely speak. You can express the three ideas separately.

## Ask about today's lunch, then usual habits

Start with **What have you got for lunch today?** Follow up with **What do you usually have for lunch?** Both appear in [BBC's Talking about lunch](https://www.bbc.co.uk/learningenglish/features/real-easy-english/250801). Today asks about this meal; usually asks about a habit.

Once your colleague has described their food, check with **You brought it from home?** This confirmation question appears in [ELLLO's What's for Lunch?](https://www.elllo.org/english/0601/T625-Tom-Lunch.htm). It refers to the lunch already mentioned. Bringing food from home does not necessarily mean making it yourself.

To share your own habit, use **Sometimes I have leftovers that I bring in.** This also comes from the BBC conversation. Use it only if it matches your life.

To ask whether your colleague made the food, say **That looks delicious! Did you make it?** [All Ears English's lunch-at-work lesson](https://www.allearsenglish.com/american-culture-and-food-at-work/) teaches this exact situation. Use it when the lunch looks homemade. When it is clearly from a restaurant, ask where they bought it instead.

## Move from lunch to cooking

To ask whether your colleague cooks dinner, say **What do you do for dinner, do you cook?** The same ELLLO lunch lesson uses this question to move the conversation to dinner.

If they cook and you want to learn, ask **Can you recommend something that is very easy to cook?** This adapts a sentence in [ELLLO's Cooking Tips](https://elllo.org/english/0451/474-Akane-Cooking.htm) by removing a repeated very.

## Similar expressions with different purposes

Choose a sentence that matches what you mean:

| Expression | When to use it | Difference |
| --- | --- | --- |
| I have a sore throat. / My throat hurts. | Describing throat discomfort | Both fit the same situation; the second uses a body part followed by hurts |
| I'm losing my voice (full sentence above) | Your voice is fading | A sore throat does not necessarily mean losing your voice |
| What have you got for lunch today? | Asking about this meal | It does not ask about a routine |
| What do you usually have for lunch? | Asking about a habit | The answer may describe a usual food or routine |
| You brought it from home? | Confirming where the food came from | It does not directly ask who cooked it |

## Two short practice conversations

These are original practice examples, rather than transcripts from the sources. The colleague's answers are fictional.

**Colleague:** What's wrong?

**You:** I have a sore throat.

**Colleague:** Is it a cold?

**You:** I have a cold and I think I'm losing my voice.

**You:** What have you got for lunch today?

**Colleague:** Rice and vegetables.

**You:** That looks delicious! Did you make it?

**Colleague:** Yes.

**You:** What do you usually have for lunch?

**Colleague:** Usually rice or noodles.
