You often hear "that's an aerobic sport" or "that's an anaerobic one". Is that split right? Where does the energy for muscle actually come from?

The conclusion first: muscle has three ways to resupply energy, and during exercise they all run at once; only the mix changes. As for how much one sport uses of each, the most complete summary I found comes from running and cycling studies, its estimates carry real uncertainty, and it covers only single maximal efforts.

This post follows on from [How Muscles Contract](/posts/learning/2026-10-24-how-muscles-contract-en): that post showed that every round of contraction needs ATP, and this one covers where the ATP comes from and how the mix changes with time. After it you can say why the "aerobic" or "anaerobic" label for one sport shifts with how long the effort lasts, and check any energy claim you read the same way. This post gives no training advice.

## Three ways to resupply ATP

[The ATP stored in muscle is very small, enough for only a few seconds of contraction](https://openstax.org/books/anatomy-and-physiology-2e/pages/10-3-muscle-fiber-contraction-and-relaxation), so it has to be resupplied as it is used. The textbook lists three ways:

- **Phosphocreatine:** quickly hands its stored energy to ADP, turning it back into ATP. It is the fastest, but lasts only about 15 seconds.
- **Glycolysis** (no oxygen needed): breaks down glucose to make ATP. It is slower than phosphocreatine and can be sustained for only about 1 minute of muscle activity.
- **Aerobic respiration** (oxygen needed): breaks down glucose or fatty acids in the mitochondria. It is much more efficient than glycolysis but slow, and needs a steady oxygen supply. In resting or moderately active muscle, about 95% of ATP comes from it.

These times are approximations; within the same section phosphocreatine is also described as "the first few seconds".

## All three run at the same time

So during exercise, which one is supplying the energy? The introduction of a [systematic review](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-026-02414-7) says that beyond a few contractions all three ways take part at once. Depending on the activity, one becomes the main source, but no single one does the job alone.

The authors use this to criticize a habit: labeling activities "anaerobic" or "aerobic" by physiology rather than describing the activity itself (explosive, high intensity, endurance). That is their view, not a result of the study; but it leads to an inference: since all three run together, a label can only reflect which one is in the majority, and which one that is changes with how long the effort lasts.

That review pooled 102 studies and 311 data points, asking: in a single maximal effort lasting a given time, how much comes from anaerobic and how much from aerobic sources?

## The longer the effort, the larger the aerobic share

From those data, the broad picture is:

- When the effort is very short, anaerobic supply dominates. At about 20 seconds the estimate is roughly 82% anaerobic and 18% aerobic.
- For a single maximal effort lasting about 75 to 80 seconds, anaerobic and aerobic each account for half (the precise estimate is 78.6 seconds, with a 95% confidence interval of plus or minus 1.1 seconds). This means the two are equal over the whole effort taken together, not that the body switches to aerobic at second 78.6.
- For longer efforts, the aerobic share keeps growing.

These are only two shares, anaerobic and aerobic. How much phosphocreatine and glycolysis each contribute within the anaerobic share, I did not read, so this post does not say.

## How reliable are these numbers

Limits the authors list:

- **The prediction error is not small.** The model's prediction error is about 12 to 14%, and the authors say the estimates should be interpreted cautiously. The plus or minus 1.1 seconds above is the confidence interval for the 78.6-second estimate, and it measures something different from the 12 to 14%.
- **There is no accepted measurement method.** There is no universally accepted way to measure anaerobic energy release in whole-body exercise, and every method relies on estimates or assumptions.
- **They are average trends.** The numbers average across different exercise modes, pacing strategies and athlete populations, and cannot precisely predict any one person.

The design of the review, and a few points I read from it:

- **Only single maximal efforts.** Intermittent exercise, repeated sprints and submaximal exercise are all in the exclusion criteria.
- **The included studies lean male.** The samples across the included studies are about 78% male, 12% female and 11% mixed, and the exercise is mostly cycling and running.
- **The data are older.** Included studies run to January 2020, and 27% of them come from the first author's own 2001 review of the same question, so part of why the two sets of estimates are close is that they share data.

## Does this apply to climbing

Not directly. This review includes just one 6-person indoor climbing study and does not analyze climbing separately. And what it studies is single maximal efforts, while climbing is intermittent: grab, move, release, grab again, and usually not all-out. So "how much of a route is anaerobic and how much aerobic" cannot be answered from it.

What carries over is the mechanism: different ways of resupplying ATP run at the same time, and their mix changes with intensity and duration. For the climbing angle, read [Energy Systems in Climbing: What the Pump Is, and Why Climbing to Failure Isn't Endurance Training](/posts/climbing/2026-10-18-climbing-energy-systems-en). Note that it draws on sports-training sources, which are weaker evidence than this systematic review.

## Two things to try tonight

1. **Estimate a duration.** Pick an all-out movement whose time you can estimate, such as a hard sprint over a short stretch, and guess how many seconds it lasts. Compare with the numbers above: if it is under about 75 seconds, this review's estimate has anaerobic supply in the majority; if over, aerobic. Then think about whether you would call it "aerobic" or "anaerobic", and whether that label fits the duration.
2. **Confirm who the participants were.** Open [the systematic review above](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-026-02414-7), read the sentence in the abstract with “78% male, 12% female, 11% mixed adult samples”, then find the exclusion criteria in the methods. You should read that intermittent exercise, repeated sprints and submaximal exercise were excluded.

## What this post leaves out

How long or how hard to train to improve one of the ways is not covered. The authors give training prescriptions in their practical-applications section, but those are their own inferences from the research, and I did not use them.
