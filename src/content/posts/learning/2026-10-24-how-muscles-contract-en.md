Clench your fist, then let go. It takes less than a second, but every working fiber in your forearm just went through the same chain of events.

This post answers one question: what actually happens inside a muscle when it produces force? Why does it depend on a nerve, and why does it get tired? By the end you can name the three things a contraction needs, and explain how the same muscle can pick up a feather or lift a heavy weight. Later posts in this series (strength, energy, fatigue, recovery) all build on this.

## A muscle does not move on its own

Skeletal muscle, the kind that moves your limbs, [depends completely on signals from the nervous system](https://openstax.org/books/anatomy-and-physiology-2e/pages/10-1-overview-of-muscle-tissues). Most of the motor neurons that drive it start in the spinal cord; a smaller number sit in the brainstem and serve the face, head and neck. Cardiac and smooth muscle can also respond to hormones and local signals. Skeletal muscle has no such backup, so no nerve signal means no contraction.

## Six steps in one contraction

A muscle fiber contains thousands of [sarcomeres](https://openstax.org/books/anatomy-and-physiology-2e/pages/10-2-skeletal-muscle) lined up end to end. Each sarcomere shortens a little, and the whole fiber shortens with it. These six steps happen in every fiber that gets the call:

1. **The nerve sends a signal.** At the neuromuscular junction, the motor neuron's terminal releases acetylcholine (ACh), a chemical messenger.
2. **The fiber fires.** ACh binds to receptors on the fiber's surface and triggers an electrical signal (an action potential). It spreads along the whole fiber and dives inside through the T-tubules.
3. **Calcium is released.** The signal makes the sarcoplasmic reticulum, the fiber's internal calcium store, release calcium ions.
4. **The binding sites open.** Calcium binds to troponin, and the protein that was covering the binding sites on actin moves aside.
5. **The pull.** The head of a myosin molecule grabs actin and pulls it toward the center of the sarcomere, shortening it. The textbook compares this grab, pull, release and reset cycle to the strokes of an oar: in, pull, out, back.
6. **ATP lets go, so it can repeat.** A fresh ATP molecule has to attach to the myosin head before it can release actin and reset. As long as calcium is present and ATP is available, the cycle keeps running and the fiber keeps shortening.

Relaxation runs the other way. The nerve stops signaling, calcium is pumped back into the reticulum, the binding sites are covered again, and myosin has nothing to pull on. One phenomenon shows what ATP does: after death the body stops making it, myosin cannot release actin, and the muscles lock in place. That is [rigor mortis](https://openstax.org/books/anatomy-and-physiology-2e/pages/10-3-muscle-fiber-contraction-and-relaxation).

## ATP is the bottleneck

ATP is the molecule cells use to supply energy. Steps 5 and 6, and the pumping of calcium back, all consume it. But muscle [stores very little ATP](https://openstax.org/books/anatomy-and-physiology-2e/pages/10-3-muscle-fiber-contraction-and-relaxation), only enough for a few seconds of contraction, so it has to be rebuilt as fast as it is used. There are three ways:

| Source | Character |
|---|---|
| Creatine phosphate | Fastest, but only lasts a very short time |
| Glycolysis (no oxygen) | Slower than creatine phosphate and cannot be sustained long; suited to short bursts of high output |
| Aerobic respiration | Slowest, but can run for a long time; at rest or moderate activity, about 95% of ATP comes from here |

In real exercise these three do not take turns. For how they run together on the wall, read [Energy Systems in Climbing: What the Pump Is, and Why Climbing to Failure Isn't Endurance Training](/posts/climbing/2026-10-18-climbing-energy-systems-en).

Why does a muscle tire? The textbook says the exact causes are not fully known. Several factors are linked to fatigue: falling ATP reserves, lactic acid lowering the pH inside the cell, sodium and potassium imbalance disrupting calcium release, and long exercise possibly damaging the reticulum and fiber membrane. So do not blame fatigue on lactic acid alone.

## Grading force: motor units and recruitment

A motor neuron branches many times, and each branch connects to one muscle fiber. That neuron plus every fiber it supplies is one **motor unit**. A fiber answers to only one neuron.

Motor units come in different sizes. The muscles that move your eyes use small units, with roughly six fibers per neuron, which gives very fine control. Thigh and back muscles use large units, where one neuron can supply thousands of fibers, built for big force.

The nervous system grades force in two ways:

- **Recruit more motor units.** Small, easily excited units fire first. When more force is needed, larger units join. In some muscles the largest unit can produce 50 times the force of the smallest. That is why the same biceps can lift a feather or a heavy weight.
- **Fire faster.** If a second signal arrives before the first twitch is over, the two add together and force rises. At a high enough rate the muscle never relaxes between signals, which is sustained contraction (tetanus).

The muscle does not use every unit at once either. Some rest while others work, which lets contraction last longer.

Contractions come in three kinds, depending on what happens to muscle length:

- **Concentric:** the muscle shortens and moves a load, like lifting a weight.
- **Eccentric:** the muscle lengthens while still producing force, like lowering a weight slowly.
- **Isometric:** the muscle produces force but its length does not change, like pushing on something that will not move, or holding a posture.

Sarcomere length matters too. Force is greatest near resting length and drops when the sarcomere is stretched too far or shortened too far.

## Two things to try tonight

These are observations, not experiments. The point is to match the terms to what you feel.

1. **Pinch a sheet of paper.** Hold it between thumb and forefinger, starting from "just enough not to drop it", then squeeze slowly up to your maximum and slowly back down. Notice that force is not an on/off switch but climbs in steps. That matches recruiting more motor units and firing them faster.
2. **Lift a water bottle.** Curl it up to chest height (concentric), hold it in mid-air for a few seconds (isometric), then lower it slowly (eccentric). Notice that the muscle is still working on the way down; only the direction changed.

## What this post leaves out

This post covers the mechanism only. How heavy to lift, how many sets, and why muscles grow come in later posts. What you take away is a map of contraction: nerve, calcium and ATP. Any one of the three can be the source of fatigue, injury or progress.
