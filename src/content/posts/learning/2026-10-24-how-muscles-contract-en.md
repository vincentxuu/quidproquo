Clench your fist, then let go. It takes less than a second, but every muscle cell (muscle fiber) in your forearm that got the call went through the same chain of events.

One contraction needs three things: a nerve signal, calcium, and ATP. By the end of this post you can say what each one does, and explain how the same muscle can pick up a feather or lift a heavy weight. The later posts on training, energy and recovery all build on this map.

## A muscle does not move on its own

Skeletal muscle, the kind that moves your limbs, [depends completely on signals from the nervous system](https://openstax.org/books/anatomy-and-physiology-2e/pages/10-1-overview-of-muscle-tissues). The [motor neurons that drive it mostly start in the spinal cord](https://openstax.org/books/anatomy-and-physiology-2e/pages/10-2-skeletal-muscle). Cardiac and smooth muscle can also respond to signals such as hormones. Skeletal muscle has no such backup, so no nerve signal means no contraction.

## Six steps in one contraction

Inside a muscle fiber are thousands of small units joined end to end, called [sarcomeres](https://openstax.org/books/anatomy-and-physiology-2e/pages/10-2-skeletal-muscle). Each sarcomere holds two kinds of protein filament: actin is the line that gets pulled, and myosin is the one that reaches out and grabs. When a sarcomere shortens a little, the whole fiber shortens a little.

These six steps happen in every fiber that gets the call:

1. **The nerve sends a signal.** At the junction between nerve and fiber (the neuromuscular junction), the motor neuron's terminal releases acetylcholine, a chemical messenger.
2. **The fiber fires.** Acetylcholine makes an electrical signal (an action potential) appear on the fiber's surface. It spreads along the fiber and into its interior.
3. **Calcium is released.** The signal makes the fiber's internal calcium store (the sarcoplasmic reticulum) release calcium ions.
4. **The binding sites open.** Calcium attaches to a regulatory protein next to actin, and the protein that was blocking the binding sites moves aside. The spots on actin that can be grabbed are now exposed.
5. **The pull.** The head of a myosin molecule grabs actin and pulls it toward the center of the sarcomere, shortening it.
6. **ATP lets go, so it can grab again.** ATP is the molecule cells use to supply energy. A fresh ATP has to attach to the myosin head before it can release actin, reset, and grab the next spot. The textbook compares this grab, pull, release and reset cycle to rowing: oar in, pull, oar out, back. As long as calcium is present and ATP is available, the cycle keeps running and the fiber keeps shortening.

All six steps in one sentence: the nerve signal flips the switch, calcium exposes the binding sites, and ATP lets the pulling repeat round after round.

Relaxation runs the other way. The nerve stops signaling and calcium is pumped back into the reticulum. The binding sites are covered again, myosin has nothing to pull on, and the muscle relaxes.

## ATP is scarce, so it has to be rebuilt as it is used

Steps 5 and 6, and the return of calcium to the reticulum, all consume ATP. But muscle [stores very little ATP](https://openstax.org/books/anatomy-and-physiology-2e/pages/10-3-muscle-fiber-contraction-and-relaxation), only enough for a few seconds of contraction, so it has to be rebuilt as it is used. There are three ways, from fast to slow: creatine phosphate, glycolysis (no oxygen), and aerobic respiration. The fast ones do not last; the slow ones do. How the three divide the work is a topic for a later post in this series. If you want to see how they combine in climbing first, read [Energy Systems in Climbing: What the Pump Is, and Why Climbing to Failure Isn't Endurance Training](/posts/climbing/2026-10-18-climbing-energy-systems-en).

Why does a muscle tire? [The textbook says the exact causes are not fully known](https://openstax.org/books/anatomy-and-physiology-2e/pages/10-3-muscle-fiber-contraction-and-relaxation). Factors linked to fatigue include falling ATP, the inside of the cell turning acidic, ion imbalance disrupting calcium release, and long exercise possibly damaging the fiber. Lactic acid is just one of the factors.

One more example shows what ATP does: after death the body stops making ATP, myosin cannot release actin, and the muscles lock in place. That is rigor mortis.

## Grading force: motor units and recruitment

A motor neuron branches many times, and each branch connects to one muscle fiber. That neuron plus every fiber it supplies is one [motor unit](https://openstax.org/books/anatomy-and-physiology-2e/pages/10-4-nervous-system-control-of-muscle-tension). A fiber answers to only one neuron.

Motor units come in different sizes. The muscles that move your eyes use small units, with roughly six fibers per neuron, which gives fine control. Thigh and back muscles use large units, where one neuron can supply thousands of fibers, built for big force.

The nervous system grades force in two ways:

- **Recruit more motor units.** Small, easily excited units fire first. When more force is needed, larger ones join. In some muscles the largest motor unit can produce 50 times the force of the smallest.
- **Send signals more densely.** If the next signal arrives before the previous contraction is over, the two add together and force rises. When signals come so densely that the muscle cannot relax between them, it stays tense.

That is how the same biceps can pick up a feather or lift a heavy weight.

## Three kinds of contraction

By what happens to muscle length, contractions come in three kinds:

- **Concentric:** the muscle shortens and moves a load, like lifting a weight.
- **Eccentric:** the muscle lengthens while still producing force, like lowering a weight slowly.
- **Isometric:** the muscle produces force but its length does not change, like trying to lift something too heavy, or holding a posture.

## Two things to try tonight

These are only to help you feel the terms in your body.

1. **Paper pull.** Pinch a sheet of paper between thumb and forefinger and slowly pull it outward with your other hand. Over about 10 seconds, start from "just enough that the paper is not pulled away" and add force bit by bit to your maximum, then ease off over another 10 seconds. Notice that force climbs in steps: that is the nervous system calling in more motor units and sending signals more densely.
2. **Water-bottle lift.** Hold a 600 ml bottle of water, with your other hand resting lightly on the front of your upper arm. Curl the bottle up to chest height over about 3 seconds (concentric), hold it in mid-air for about 3 seconds (isometric), then lower it over about 3 seconds (eccentric). Notice that on the way down the muscle is still working; it is just producing force while being lengthened.

Afterwards, clench your fist once more and think of the motor units being called in your forearm.

## What this post leaves out

This post covers the mechanism only. How heavy to lift, how many sets, and why muscles grow come in later posts. What you have now is a map of contraction: the nerve signal flips the switch, calcium exposes the binding sites, and ATP lets the cycle repeat. Some of the known causes of fatigue relate to ATP, and some to how calcium is released.
