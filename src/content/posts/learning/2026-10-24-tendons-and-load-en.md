When you hang from a small hold, force travels from your forearm to your fingers, and it has to pass through tendons. A common saying is that "load makes tendons stronger". What about the tendons in your fingers?

The bottom line first: for the lower limb, a meta-analysis pooling many studies measured tendons getting stiffer after load. But stiffer is not the same as stronger, and that analysis did not measure the fingers. For how finger tendons adapt, I found no study that tracks them before and after training, within what I searched.

This post sorts the evidence into four parts: what a tendon does in the body, what was measured in the lower limb, what turned up for the fingers, and the cost of a lot of load. After it you can say what "tendon gets stiffer" actually measures and why it cannot be applied to the fingers directly. This post gives no training advice.

## Where the tendon sits

[The tension made when a muscle contracts passes through the connective tissue around the muscle to the tendon, and then pulls on the bone so the body moves](https://openstax.org/books/anatomy-and-physiology-2e/pages/10-2-skeletal-muscle). So the tendon is the stretch between muscle and bone that carries force. Whatever force the muscle makes has to get through this stretch.

## Lower-limb tendons: stiffer after load

[A meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-022-01695-y) pooled 61 studies with 763 participants. It looked at what happens to healthy lower-limb tendons, the Achilles tendon at the back of the heel and the patellar tendon below the knee, after loading, mostly resistance training. It measured three things:

- **Stiffness:** how much the tendon lengthens when pulled by the same force; the stiffer, the less it lengthens.
- **Modulus:** how stiff the tissue itself is once thickness is accounted for.
- **Cross-sectional area:** the area of the tendon cut across, to see whether it got thicker.

After loading, stiffness rose moderately, modulus rose a lot, and cross-sectional area rose slightly. "Moderately", "a lot" and "slightly" here are standardized effect sizes, not percentages. Using 20 of the studies, the authors then ran a regression and found the rise in stiffness was linked to modulus and not significantly to cross-sectional area. So their reading is that a tendon gets stiffer mainly because the tissue itself gets stiffer, with thickening contributing less.

Two more details: only resistance training raised all three clearly; and the authors point out that resistance training in which the tendon is stretched more (high strain) worked better, but only 3 studies measured strain, so treat it as a lead.

Note that what was measured is stiffness, not strength. Stiffness only says how much a tendon lengthens when pulled; the source does not say it means stronger or less likely to be injured.

## Whom does this result apply to

Some of the limits below were listed by the authors, and some come from the design and scope of the analysis. All of them affect whom the result can be applied to.

Limits the authors list:

- **The studies are small.** Apart from one, every study had 15 participants or fewer.
- **Most participants were untrained.** Most had no consistent history of resistance training or were only recreationally active. Only 3 studies included well-trained people, and the authors say none of the 3 showed a change in patellar-tendon stiffness.
- **Reporting is incomplete.** The authors judged overall quality moderate to high, but also write that only 7% of studies reported allocation concealment and only 18% reported assessor blinding.

Design and scope of the analysis:

- **It compares before and after training.** It did not extract control-group data, so it cannot compare with people who did not train.
- **Lower limb only.** Tendons in the fingers, shoulder and elbow are outside its scope.

So the more accurate statement is: in healthy people's lower limbs, stiffer tendons were measured after resistance training; the evidence comes from many small studies, it may not apply to well-trained people, and the fingers were not measured.

## Finger tendons: what turned up

This is the part climbers most want answered, and it is the emptiest. What I found is the three kinds of material below, none of them a study that tracks finger tendons before and after training. This conclusion holds only within what I searched; it does not mean all literature was searched.

- The fingers carry large loads. A [narrative review of resistance training in climbing](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40798-024-00677-w) points to high mechanical stress on the finger flexors and says fingers and shoulders are the most frequent chronic injury sites in climbing. The authors suggest resistance training may reduce tendon injuries, but that is an inference based on other literature they cite. They also write that intervention studies of this kind are few and the evidence for improved climbing performance is limited.
- A [case report with a literature review](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijerph192417050) examined the fingers of one elite climber and saw changes in soft tissue and bone, with signs of osteoarthritis at a late stage. It is one person, whom the authors suggest belongs to a small group with the heaviest loads, so he cannot stand for climbers in general.
- The lower-limb meta-analysis above, whose scope does not include fingers.

So what can be said is: the fingers carry large loads and are a common injury site, but for "load makes finger tendons stronger" I did not find reliable research that supports it.

## A lot of load also has costs

Another [systematic review](https://doi.org/10.3389/fspor.2023.1269870) pooled 34 studies with 10,049 climbers and looked at risk factors for overuse injuries, not limited to tendons. The "intensity" it looked at is climbing difficulty grade, not the force a tendon actually bears. Of 21 studies that looked at intensity, 19 found that climbers at higher difficulty also had higher injury risk; for climbing volume (hours), the relationship was unclear and studies contradicted each other.

The 34 included studies are of low quality: scored on the Downs and Black scale out of 32, they range from 8 to 20 with an average of 14.4, and nearly two thirds are cross-sectional (a single snapshot in time that cannot show what came first). And what it reports is "associated with", not "causes". It supports this much: people who climb at higher difficulty also have higher injury risk. How much load is low enough to avoid injury, these studies cannot answer.

## Two things to try tonight

1. **Check a claim with three questions.** Pick a tendon or finger-training claim you read recently and write down: which tendon does it talk about? Who were the participants: trained or not, healthy or injured? What is its source? This extends question 4 in [the series overview](/posts/learning/2026-10-24-human-body-overview-en), "do the people studied resemble you". Hold any unanswered one loosely; if you cannot find a source, that is also an answer, so write "not found".
2. **Read one limit.** Open [the meta-analysis above](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-022-01695-y), find the Limitations in the discussion, and search for “well-trained”. You should read that only 3 studies included well-trained people, and none of them showed a change in patellar-tendon stiffness.
