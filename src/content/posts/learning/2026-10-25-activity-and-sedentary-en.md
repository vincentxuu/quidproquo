How much do you need to move each week? Is sitting really that bad? Should you walk 10,000 steps a day? These numbers are everywhere online, and few sources say how strong the evidence behind them is.

The conclusion first, in three parts. First, WHO gives adults a clear set of activity benchmarks, and the two core ones are strong recommendations. Second, on sitting, WHO itself admits there is not enough evidence to set a quantitative limit. Third, 10,000 steps a day is not a threshold the research summary sets. And most of this evidence can only say that two things tend to go together, not that moving more necessarily brings a benefit.

This post looks at the activity benchmarks, sitting and steps in turn, and marks which layer of evidence each sits on. After it you can say which WHO recommendations are strong and which one is only conditional, and check any activity claim you read in the same way. It gives no exercise prescription; if something is wrong with your body, or new symptoms appear after you increase your activity, ask a doctor first.

## WHO's benchmarks for adults

The [WHO 2020 guidelines](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2020-102955) recommend for adults aged 18 to 64:

- **Aerobic activity:** at least 150 to 300 minutes of moderate-intensity activity a week, or at least 75 to 150 minutes of vigorous-intensity, or an equivalent combination.
- **Muscle-strengthening activity:** on 2 or more days a week, at moderate or greater intensity, involving all major muscle groups.

What do moderate and vigorous feel like? WHO defines them on a 0 to 10 scale of perceived exertion, where 0 is no effort and 10 is maximum effort: moderate is usually 5 to 6, and vigorous usually 7 to 8. People 65 and over have a separate set of recommendations, which this post does not cover.

A few things that are easy to miss:

- **Activity of any length counts.** The older version required bouts of at least 10 minutes; the new one does not.
- **Every setting counts.** Activity at work, in leisure, at home and in getting around all counts toward the recommended amount.
- **There are benefits below the recommended amount.** Start small and build up gradually.
- **"Generally no medical clearance" comes with a condition.** What WHO says is that people without contraindications generally do not need pre-exercise medical clearance before starting light- or moderate-intensity activity that does not exceed the demands of brisk walking or everyday living. If new symptoms appear after you increase your activity, see a doctor.

## Strength and evidence for each recommendation

The [series overview](/posts/learning/2026-10-24-human-body-overview-en) explained that WHO sorts recommendations into strong and conditional, and rates the certainty of evidence separately. Here are this post's recommendations side by side:

| Recommendation | Strength | What WHO says about the evidence |
|---|---|---|
| 150 to 300 minutes of moderate-intensity a week (or 75 to 150 of vigorous) | Strong | For some outcomes (all-cause and cardiovascular mortality, cancer, diabetes), the dose-response is curved with moderate certainty: more is better, but the benefit diminishes, and it cannot be said where the diminishing begins |
| Muscle-strengthening on 2 or more days a week | Strong | Moderate certainty; no evidence that higher amounts bring more benefit |
| More than 300 minutes a week may add benefit | Conditional | Because the point where diminishing begins cannot be specified |
| Limit sitting and replace it with activity of any intensity | Strong | The link between sitting and mortality varies with activity level, with moderate certainty; evidence is insufficient for a quantitative threshold on how much sitting is too much |

So within one guideline, the two core items are strong recommendations, going beyond them is only conditional, and for sitting the direction is clear but the numbers are not.

WHO's guidelines do not address training athletes do to improve competition performance.

## Sitting: the association varies with activity

WHO's recommendation for adults is to limit the time spent sedentary, replace it with activity of any intensity, and, if you sit a lot, do more than the recommended moderate-to-vigorous amount.

A [meta-analysis that measured activity with accelerometers](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2020-103270) adds detail. An accelerometer is a small device worn on the body that records the size of movement, and this study used it to measure daily activity and sitting. It pooled 9 prospective cohort studies (activity measured first, then people followed for years to see who died first), with more than 44,000 people aged 40 or older, followed for up to 14.5 years.

The result: more sitting went with higher mortality, most clearly in the least active people. Among the most active third, how long people sat showed no statistically clear difference in mortality. That group's median activity was roughly 30 to 40 minutes a day of moderate-to-vigorous activity. The authors say the association was weakened but not completely removed.

Limits the authors list include:

- It is observational and cannot rule out reverse causation; in other words, people who were already ill may simply have moved less.
- All participants were middle-aged or older adults aged 40 and over, so it may not apply to younger, more active people, nor necessarily to low- and middle-income countries.
- The accelerometer judges sitting from the size of movement, which may count standing as sitting.

## Daily steps

What about 10,000 steps a day? A 2024 [umbrella review](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjopen-2024-088524) (a study that gathers many systematic reviews into one summary) pooled 10 systematic reviews, all observational. It cites one analysis in which mortality risk was lowest at roughly 8,000 to 10,000 steps a day for people under 60 and roughly 6,000 to 8,000 for people 60 and over, with no further clear drop beyond that.

This is an average picture seen across populations, not a step target for you. And 10,000 is not a threshold this summary sets. The authors also say they did not grade the evidence. It mentions as well a study finding that more than 10,000 steps a day went with a higher risk of meniscal pathology, but that comes from just one study, not enough to conclude anything.

## Does this apply to climbers

WHO's benchmark is a health benchmark for ordinary adults, not a prescription for competition training. Recreational climbers mostly count as ordinary adults and can compare their total activity with these numbers; training to improve climbing performance is not covered by this evidence. If you want to start from climbing, begin with [Plateaued in Climbing, Should You Start Training?](/posts/climbing/2026-10-18-climbing-training-diagnosis-en).

On muscle-strengthening, WHO's conclusion is that there is no evidence that higher amounts bring more health benefit; a different question, how to make strength and muscle improve more, has different evidence and conclusions, in [How Heavy, How Many Sets, To Failure or Not](/posts/learning/2026-10-25-strength-and-hypertrophy-en).

## Two things to try tonight

1. **Add up last week's moderate activity.** Looking back over last week, list the activities that felt about 5 to 6 on the effort scale (0 is no effort, 10 is maximum effort), write down the minutes for each, and add them up. Work, housework and getting around all count, with no minimum bout. Compare the total with WHO: below 150 minutes, WHO says there is still benefit, so start small and build up gradually; above 300, the extra benefit is only a conditional recommendation. Do not suddenly add a lot because you fell short.
2. **Label the strength of the recommendations.** Take the first three rows of the table above, copy each into one sentence of your own, and label it strong or conditional. You should end up with two strong recommendations (150 to 300 minutes, strengthening on 2 days) and one conditional (more than 300 minutes).

## Questions this post cannot answer

The physiology of cardiorespiratory fitness (how the heart pumps blood and the lungs exchange oxygen), and recommendations for people 65 and over, children, pregnancy and chronic conditions, are not covered. Whether different kinds of activity are equivalent, and whether steps can be converted to minutes, I did not read evidence on and draw no conclusion. How to plan training or improve sports performance is outside this post.
