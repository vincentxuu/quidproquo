The evidence in earlier posts mostly comes from healthy adults, for example [How Heavy, How Many Sets, To Failure or Not](/posts/learning/2026-10-25-strength-and-hypertrophy-en). Whether those patterns still hold in older age has to be looked at separately.

The conclusion first: muscle mass and strength decline with age, but current studies can hardly separate how much comes from age and how much from becoming less active. When randomized trials in older adults with sarcopenia are pooled (a meta-analysis), the resistance-training groups do better than controls on grip strength and walking speed. The average gains mostly stay below the threshold people can clinically notice, though, and muscle mass does not clearly increase. These exercise results cannot be applied directly to healthy older adults. For bone, I could read only a meta-analysis in postmenopausal women and the abstract of one newer study. The certainty of evidence (see [Understanding the Human Body: Overview](/posts/learning/2026-10-24-human-body-overview-en)) is not high in either.

This post gives no program and no protein or supplement advice; ask a doctor or dietitian about diet and supplements, and see [Bones and Joints](/posts/learning/2026-10-25-bones-and-joints-en) for the basic structure of bone. If you have a chronic condition, a history of falls, or a diagnosis of osteoporosis, ask a doctor or physical therapist before starting new exercise.

## How muscle changes with age

The source for this section is a 2021 review paper by Shur and colleagues. The text I read gives no description of a search method, and the evidence level is lower than a meta-analysis.

The authors say there is no agreement on how fast muscle mass declines with age, because few studies follow the same people over a long time. Most data come from cross-sectional studies, which compare people of different ages at one point in time; compared with young adults, people aged 65 to 90 showed muscle-mass differences anywhere from 8% to 45%, a very wide range. Strength falls consistently more than muscle mass does.

Age and activity level are mixed together. They know of no study that measured participants' usual activity precisely and used it to match young and old groups. In one small cross-sectional study, highly trained older athletes showed muscle and strength differences that did not appear to be related to age. Without precise measurement of activity, it is hard to say these differences come wholly from ageing.

The authors also speculate that muscle ageing caused by lifestyle may be at least partly reversible; that is speculation, not a result of the review.

## What the two sarcopenia studies compared

Sarcopenia is an age-related condition in which muscle mass and function fall to a certain degree. Both papers I read are meta-analyses that pool only randomized controlled trials, in older adults diagnosed with sarcopenia.

- **First paper (Yan and colleagues, 2025):** 24 trials, 951 people, aged 60 or older, average about 73, roughly 20% men. Control groups received usual care, health education, or no intervention. Sarcopenia caused by diseases such as cancer, diabetes or stroke was excluded.
- **Second paper (Ran and colleagues, 2025):** 12 trials, 538 people, only pure resistance training, with trials combining nutritional supplements left out.

At least some trials overlap: for example, Vasconcelos 2016 and Rufino 2023 appear in both (I matched by author and year and did not check the supplementary trial lists). So the two results are not two independent bodies of evidence.

## Results: improvement, but how much needs care

The first paper found that the resistance-training groups did better than controls on grip strength, walking speed, knee-extension strength, the timed up-and-go test (TUG) and the five-times sit-to-stand test (5STS), and the differences were statistically significant. There was no significant difference on the short physical performance battery (SPPB) or the muscle-mass measures. The second paper found a medium-sized effect on grip strength and no significant difference in the muscle-mass index. On SPPB the two differ: no effect in the first (only 3 trials), a medium effect in the second.

"Statistically significant" only means the difference is unlikely to be chance; it does not mean the difference is big enough to notice. The first paper set a "minimal important difference" for some outcomes. For grip strength the threshold is 5.0 kg and the training group averaged 2.3 kg more. For walking speed the threshold is 0.1 m/s and the group averaged 0.08 m/s more.

The conclusion of that paper has two sentences that seem to pull in different directions:

> "Resistance training probably improves muscle strength and physical function in older adults with sarcopenia."

> "improvements in grip strength, gait speed, TUG, 5STS, and SPPB did not exceed their MID thresholds, indicating little to no clinical benefit"

In plain terms: training probably improves strength and performance, but the gain does not exceed the threshold people can feel. The second sentence lists SPPB among the "improvements", yet the same paper's results section says SPPB showed no significant difference; that is an inconsistency in the original. The clinical-implications section adds that any clinically perceptible benefit over usual care is "uncertain".

Neither paper found a significant increase in muscle mass. The second paper's authors speculate the reason may be that they included only pure resistance training, without nutrition; that is speculation. Adverse events were reported by only four trials, all mild and reversible; I did not read whether the other trials recorded any.

## The two papers rate evidence quality differently

Both used GRADE to rate certainty of evidence. The first says most outcomes fall at moderate to high; the second says overall quality is low and calls for caution. Of the first paper's own 24 trials, only 8 had low risk of bias (trial designs less likely to distort results). I did not compare the ratings, so I do not judge which is right, only note that both assessments exist.

## Bone: the part I could read

**Postmenopausal women (full text read).** Mohebbi and colleagues' 2023 meta-analysis pooled 80 controlled trials of at least 6 months, with 5,581 postmenopausal women. Exercise groups had higher bone density than controls, which the authors rate as moderate in size at best. The effect size (standardized mean difference, which puts results from different instruments on one scale) was 0.29 at the lumbar spine, 0.27 at the femoral neck and 0.41 at the total hip.

The exercise types varied widely across trials, including walking, jumping, resistance training and tai chi. No significant difference showed up by whether bone mass was already low, how long since menopause, or whether sessions were supervised; finding no difference is not proof there is none. The outcome is bone density, not fracture.

If you have been diagnosed with osteoporosis or have had a fracture, ask a doctor which exercises you can do.

**Age 40 and over (abstract only).** Lu and colleagues' 2026 meta-analysis in the BMJ pooled 124 trials. It is not open access, and I read only the abstract. The abstract says brisk walking or jogging and combined aerobic-resistance exercise "probably" improve lumbar-spine bone density, with low to moderate certainty; mixed aerobic exercise and mind-body exercise "may" reduce fracture risk, with low certainty. The authors conclude with "cautious support". Whether "other types" includes pure resistance training I cannot confirm without the full text, so this post cannot answer whether resistance training helps bone.

## Limits of these conclusions

- **Population and comparison.** Participants were diagnosed older adults, and 10 of the second paper's 12 trials came from Western populations; its authors warn against extending the results to other groups. The comparison was usual care, so these studies can only answer "how much better than no intervention".
- **Time and outcomes.** The first paper's authors say most trials lasted no more than 26 weeks at 2 to 3 sessions per week, which limits how far the dose-response results can be extended; they recommend future trials include falls and quality of life, which are not available now.
- **Counter-evidence.** Within the range I searched, I found no systematic review with the opposite conclusion; that does not mean none exists.

## Does this apply to climbing or daily activity

There are no climbing data. A grip dynamometer measures the grip of the whole hand, which differs from finger strength on a hold; for finger training see [Hangboard Training](/posts/climbing/2026-10-18-hangboard-training-en).

Walking and sit-to-stand tests are fairly close to daily life (this is my analogy). On fall prevention, the WHO 2020 guideline says for adults aged 65 and older that balance and functional exercises reduce the rate of falls, and rates this as high-certainty evidence. That is for older adults in general; for people with chronic conditions, WHO says direct evidence is lacking. See the original for specific recommendations, which this post does not restate; balance has its own post: [Balance and Fall Prevention](/posts/learning/2026-10-25-balance-and-fall-prevention-en). (The first paper's participants were aged 60 and older.)

## Two things to try tonight

Both only involve reading; neither asks you to test your own strength.

1. **Tell "significant" from "noticeable".** Open [the abstract of Yan et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40520-025-03235-w) and find the conclusion. You should read two sentences in the same passage: the first says resistance training "probably improves" strength and function, and the second says the improvements "did not exceed their MID thresholds".
2. **See who the study asked about.** In the full text of the same paper, find the eligibility criteria in the Methods and copy the participant conditions as three lines. You should write down: aged 60 or older, diagnosed with sarcopenia, and not caused by diseases such as cancer, diabetes or stroke. Then ask whether the older person you have in mind fits; if not, treat the results as background only.
