Earlier posts covered muscle, energy and the heart and lungs. This one asks: can balance be trained, and if you train it, do you actually fall less?

The conclusion comes with a split between two kinds of result. One is how many times people fall in real life. The other is the score on a balance test in a clinic or a study. The evidence I read says exercise lowers the number of falls in community-dwelling older adults, with high certainty of evidence (the levels are explained in [Understanding the Human Body: Overview](/posts/learning/2026-10-24-human-body-overview-en)). The average gains in test scores are small, and the certainty is low.

After reading, you should be able to say how sure we can be about each kind of result, and to tell which one a claim like "balance training works" is about.

The evidence is about people aged 60 and over who mostly walk on their own. This post gives no exercise program. If you have just fallen and been hurt, hit your head, or have new dizziness, get medical care. If you want to be more active but have fallen before or have a chronic condition, ask a doctor or physical therapist first.

## Does exercise mean fewer falls?

The first source is a 2019 [Cochrane review](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD012424.pub2). Cochrane is an international organization that produces systematic reviews, which means setting the rules first and then gathering the studies that meet them. I did not read the full text of this review; everything below comes from its abstract.

It included 108 randomized controlled trials (researchers randomly assign participants to an exercise group or a control group and compare them) with 23,407 community-dwelling people aged 60 or older, 76 years old on average. The abstract says trials focused on particular conditions, such as stroke, were excluded.

The outcome is the rate of falls, meaning how many falls happened during follow-up. The abstract's conclusion is:

> Exercise programmes reduce the rate of falls and the number of people experiencing falls in older people living in the community (high-certainty evidence).

For balance and functional exercises the result is:

> balance and functional exercises reduce the rate of falls by 24%

The rate ratio is 0.76: the exercise group had about 76% as many falls as the control group. It does not mean each person fell 24% less.

The abstract also lists limits: most trials had design problems that could bias the result, or it was unclear whether they did; and the authors are uncertain about exercise that is mainly resistance training, dance or walking.

## How much did balance test scores improve?

Studies often use test scores because they are easier to measure in the short term. A 2025 [meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1007/s41999-025-01328-3) does this. A meta-analysis pools the numbers from several studies. I read this paper in full.

It looks only at dual-task training, which means doing two things at once, such as walking while counting. It included 44 randomized controlled trials with 2,782 people aged over 60, mostly community-dwelling, with a few trials in care facilities that the paper does not break out by setting. People with stroke, Parkinson's disease or dementia were excluded. The authors also write that most participants could walk and live independently, so the results cannot be applied directly to frail older adults.

The pooled results are the exercise group's average minus the control group's average:

- **Berg Balance Scale:** 1.78 points higher, certainty very low.
- **Timed Up and Go:** 0.73 seconds shorter, certainty low.
- **Number of falls:** 6 trials, fewer in the exercise group, certainty moderate.

First, the size of the gains. In the discussion the authors write that the average difference did not reach the minimal clinically important difference thresholds they cite: 3 points for the scale and 2.2 seconds for the walking test. So the differences are statistically significant, but on average not large enough to be noticeable. The authors speculate the gap would be larger if training fell in the best range their analysis found. That is their hypothesis, not a result.

Second, how consistent the studies were. I² is above 90% for the scale and the walking test (the higher it is, the more the trials' results differ), so the pooled average does not describe any single trial well.

Third, the fall data. The authors say all six studies relied on participants reporting their own falls, which is prone to recall bias. The paper does not clearly state the unit of this average difference, so I can only say the exercise group fell less, not give a percentage.

For the number of falls, Cochrane rates the certainty high and this review rates it moderate. The two included different studies and exercise types, and I did not look further into why. The conclusion also applies only to dual-task training.

## What the WHO says

The [WHO 2020 physical activity guidelines](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2020-102955) define older adults as aged 65 and over. Their older-adult section recommends varied activity that emphasizes balance and strength, and says high-certainty evidence shows balance and functional exercises reduce the rate of falls. That points the same way as the Cochrane abstract.

The older-adult recommendation also covers people with chronic conditions or disabilities, but the guidelines admit direct evidence for these groups is lacking and much of it is extended by inference. For the specific frequency and intensity, see the original; this post does not restate them. For overall weekly activity see [How Much Activity a Week](/posts/learning/2026-10-25-activity-and-sedentary-en), and for muscle and bone after 60 see [How Muscle Strength Changes With Age](/posts/learning/2026-10-25-aging-muscle-and-bone-en).

## Does this apply to young adults or climbers?

I did not read evidence on falls in young adults or climbers, so I do not apply it to them.

## Two things you can do tonight

1. **Read one abstract and find one sentence.** Open the [Europe PMC page of the Cochrane review](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/30703272) and look in the abstract's Main results for “balance and functional exercises reduce the rate of falls”. You should read: 24% fewer, followed by high-certainty evidence.
2. **Sort the three results.** Write down the three results above: the scale, the walking test and the number of falls. Mark each "test" or "real falls", and add its certainty. You should write: the first two are tests (very low, low) and the third is real falls (moderate). Then think about this: Cochrane rates falls "high", while the "moderate" here is this one review's own rating. The two numbers come from different reviews.

Both actions are done sitting down and reading. Do not try standing on one leg without support just to see.
