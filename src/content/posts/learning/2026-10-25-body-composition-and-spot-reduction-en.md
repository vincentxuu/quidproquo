"Do crunches and the fat on your belly goes away" is an old claim. It says that wherever you train, the fat there shrinks more, which is called spot reduction. What does the evidence say?

The short answer depends on the body part. For limbs, the most complete summary I read compared the trained side with the untrained side of the same person and did not see a local effect. For the abdomen and trunk, that summary does not cover them; there are only two small trials, both saw less fat in the abdomen or trunk, but they had few participants and measured differently, so the evidence is weak. You cannot say spot reduction is proven, and you cannot say it is ruled out.

This post goes through three studies in turn, then their limits. By the end you should be able to tell whether a claim that spot reduction "works" or "doesn't" rests on a pooled set of studies or on a trial of a dozen people. It says nothing about what your weight or body fat should be, gives no diet advice, and is not recommending that anyone lose fat. If you notice you are eating less or exercising excessively because of your weight or body fat, or you are very concerned about your body shape or want to lose a lot of weight, please talk to a doctor or dietitian first.

## The most complete source: the trained side versus the untrained side

The [systematic review and meta-analysis by Ramírez-Campillo and colleagues (2022)](https://doi.org/10.5114/hm.2022.110373) included only unilateral limb training: one side is trained, the other serves as the control, and local fat change is compared between the two sides. What a systematic review and a meta-analysis are is explained in [Understanding the Human Body: A Map of How It Works](/posts/learning/2026-10-24-human-body-overview-en).

It pooled 13 studies with over a thousand healthy men and women. The pooled effect size was −0.03 with a 95% confidence interval of −0.10 to 0.05. You can think of effect size as "how different the two sides are", where 0 means no difference; a confidence interval that crosses 0 means these data cannot tell the two sides apart. The authors conclude that localized muscle training had no effect on localized fat.

This design compares the two sides of a limb. It did not test the abdomen or trunk, so its conclusion cannot be applied directly to "do crunches reduce belly fat".

I read only the abstract, not the full text, so the limitations the authors list in the body are outside what I saw. The abstract says the included studies had high methodological quality and fairly consistent results; that is the authors' own assessment and I did not check it.

## Two small randomized trials, both seeing changes in the abdomen or trunk

A randomized trial assigns participants to groups at random and then compares the groups. Both trials below are small.

**Paoli and colleagues, 2021.** Eighteen healthy adults enrolled, 4 were excluded for attending too few sessions, and 14 were analyzed (7 per group). They trained for 8 weeks: one group did a circuit program emphasizing the abdomen and triceps, the other did conventional resistance training, with similar exercises in a different order. Fat thickness was measured three ways: skinfold calipers (pinching the skin and measuring its thickness), ultrasound, and bioimpedance, the method many body-fat scales use.

The paper reports that abdominal subcutaneous fat got thinner in the circuit group while the triceps site did not change, which the authors call unexpected. Bioimpedance showed no significant change in total fat mass. The trial compares two ways of arranging training, not training against no training. The paper lists each group's own before-and-after change; I did not see a test comparing the two groups with each other.

**Brobakken and colleagues, 2023.** Sixteen men with overweight (mean age 43) were randomized to 10 weeks of training. One group did running plus abdominal endurance exercise, the other did running only. Before the trial, pilot measurements were used to match the two programs' estimated total energy expenditure. By DEXA, total fat mass and body weight fell in both groups with no difference between them; trunk fat fell about 700 grams more in the abdominal group than in the running-only group. The authors therefore argue that spot reduction shows up in these men with overweight.

That conclusion has limits, and the authors list several: participants were only asked not to change their diet, and intake was not measured; DEXA cannot tell which layer of fat was lost; and on whether the findings apply to women they write "it is unclear if the findings translate to women".

## Why the three do not line up

I mark where each point comes from.

- **They measured different things (from the two trials' introductions).** Paoli's introduction says skinfold calipers depend on the operator, the site and skin thickness; Brobakken's introduction says calipers differ quite a bit from DEXA and MRI. With a different instrument, you may not be measuring the same layer of fat.
- **They compared different things (my summary).** The meta-analysis compares two sides of the same person's limbs; Paoli compares two training programs; Brobakken compares the trunk, with running only as the control. These are three different questions.
- **They are small (my summary; Paoli's authors also list it as a limitation).** The two trials together analyzed only 30 people.
- **The mechanism is unclear (from Brobakken's discussion).** The authors write that it is unclear whether the effect comes from metabolic or circulatory mechanisms. That is their speculation, not something the trial measured.

## How much do exercise and diet each contribute

I cannot answer this. In the range I searched, I did not find a recent, freely readable review that directly compares exercise only, diet only, and both together. Paoli's introduction says total body fat can be reduced through diet and/or exercise, but the reviews it cites are ones I could not read in full. Brobakken points out that energy balance affects whether excess energy is stored as fat or used as fuel, so not measuring diet is a gap in their design. Neither statement gives a ratio.

## Does this apply to climbing or daily activity

None of these studies involved climbers, so there is no data to apply. For evidence on daily activity, see [How Much to Move Each Week](/posts/learning/2026-10-25-activity-and-sedentary-en), which looks at health outcomes, not fat distribution.

## Two things to try tonight

1. **Count the participants in one trial.** Open [the abstract of Paoli 2021](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijerph18073845) and find "Fourteen volunteers". You should read that the abstract says 14 people were randomized into two groups; the methods section of the full text then shows that 18 enrolled and 4 were excluded. Ask how much confidence you would give a claim that rests on studies of that size.
2. **Read how a "no difference" is written.** Open [the Ramírez-Campillo 2022 journal page](https://hummov.awf.wroc.pl/A-proposed-model-to-test-the-hypothesis-of-exercise-induced-localized-fat-reduction,143162,0,2.html) and find "pooled ES". You should read an effect size of −0.03 and a confidence interval of −0.10 to 0.05, which crosses 0; then find "37 comparisons" and you should read that 17 favoured the trained side and 20 the untrained side.

Next time you see a claim that spot reduction "works" or "doesn't", ask three questions: how many studies were pooled, how many people were in each, and who was compared with whom. For this post the answers are: 13 studies in the meta-analysis, 14 and 16 people analyzed in the two trials, and the comparisons were two sides of a limb, two training programs, and running plus abdominal exercise against running only.

## What this post leaves out

How the body draws on fat during exercise and how muscle mass relates to resting metabolism are not covered, because I did not read sources reliable enough to write about them. Nor is compensation, meaning eating more or moving less after exercise. These studies also do not clearly say what applies to women, children, older adults, or people with chronic conditions. Whether to change your eating or activity depends on your own situation; please ask a doctor, physical therapist or dietitian. If your weight drops without a clear reason, see a doctor as well.
