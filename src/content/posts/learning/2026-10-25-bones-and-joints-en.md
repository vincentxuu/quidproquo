[How Muscles Contract](/posts/learning/2026-10-24-how-muscles-contract-en) and [How Tendons Respond to Load](/posts/learning/2026-10-24-tendons-and-load-en) covered how force from muscle passes through tendon and pulls on bone so the body moves. This post looks at the end that gets pulled: what do bones and joints look like, and when people say "load makes bone stronger", what has research actually measured?

The short version. On structure, the textbook is clear: bone is living tissue that is constantly broken down and rebuilt, and movable joints work through a capsule, cartilage, joint fluid, and ligaments. On load and bone, the textbook is more confident than the evidence I read. The two meta-analyses (studies that pool the numbers from many studies) I read show higher bone mass only for certain groups and conditions, and in the range I searched I found no freely readable recent review covering men or middle-aged and older adults in general.

By the end you should be able to name the parts of a bone, say what shape each of the six synovial joints has, and tell a textbook generalization from a measured result. This post gives no exercise or supplement advice; if you have a fracture, a diagnosis of bone loss, or joint pain, ask a doctor or physical therapist first.

## How a bone is built

Start with a long bone. The tubular middle part is the shaft (diaphysis); its wall is compact bone, and the hollow inside is the medullary cavity. The wider ends are the epiphyses, filled with spongy bone. In a growing bone, a layer of hyaline cartilage called the growth plate sits between shaft and end, and it is replaced by bone once growth stops.

The outside is wrapped in a membrane called the periosteum, and [tendons and ligaments attach at the periosteum](https://openstax.org/books/anatomy-and-physiology-2e/pages/6-3-bone-structure). Where two bones meet to form a joint, the periosteum does not cover the bone; articular cartilage does.

Bone has two kinds of material: mineral crystals make it hard and strong, and collagen fibers make it flexible so it is not brittle. Compact bone is dense and sits where support and protection are needed, such as the shaft. Spongy bone is a lattice of struts called trabeculae; each forms along lines of stress, and the gaps make the bone lighter.

## Bone is living tissue

Bone has few cells, but their roles are distinct. Osteoblasts make new bone matrix; an osteoblast trapped in the matrix becomes an osteocyte, which maintains the mineral content; osteoclasts break down old, injured, or unneeded bone.

In adults, bone is first resorbed and then rebuilt in the same place, which is [bone remodeling](https://openstax.org/books/anatomy-and-physiology-2e/pages/6-4-bone-formation-and-development). The textbook gives a rough figure: even without injury or exercise, about 5 to 10 percent of the skeleton is replaced each year. It adds that injury, exercise, and other activities also lead to remodeling. The two meta-analyses below test the exercise part of that.

## Joints: how bones connect

A joint is where two bones, or a bone and cartilage, meet. By structure, [the textbook](https://openstax.org/books/anatomy-and-physiology-2e/pages/9-1-classification-of-joints) sorts joints into fibrous (like the sutures of the skull), cartilaginous (like the discs between vertebrae), and synovial; by movement, into nearly immobile, slightly movable, and freely movable. [Synovial joints are the most common type in the body](https://openstax.org/books/anatomy-and-physiology-2e/pages/9-4-synovial-joints), and most limb movement comes from them.

Synovial joints share four structures:

- **Joint capsule**: encloses the joint and forms the joint cavity.
- **Synovial fluid**: a lubricating fluid made by the lining of the capsule. Articular cartilage has no blood vessels, and the fluid also feeds it.
- **Articular cartilage**: covers the contact surface of each bone and reduces friction.
- **Ligaments**: tie bone to bone, limit the range of motion, and prevent excessive or abnormal movement.

Synovial joints fall into six types by the shape of the bone surfaces:

| Type | What it can do | Textbook examples |
|---|---|---|
| Ball-and-socket | Moves in all directions and rotates | Shoulder, hip |
| Hinge | Bends and straightens only | Elbow, the finger and toe joints between phalanges |
| Condyloid | Bending and straightening, plus side to side | Knuckle (metacarpophalangeal) joints, wrist |
| Saddle | Movement in two directions | Base of the thumb |
| Plane | Small gliding movements | Between wrist bones, between foot bones |
| Pivot | Rotates around one axis | First and second neck vertebrae (turning the head), top of the radius in the forearm |

The textbook contrasts the shoulder and the hip: the shoulder socket is shallow and the range of motion is large; the hip socket is deep and backed by strong ligaments, so movement is more limited, in exchange for stability and weight-bearing.

## Load and bone: what the textbook says

[The textbook](https://openstax.org/books/anatomy-and-physiology-2e/pages/6-6-exercise-nutrition-hormones-and-bone-tissue) explains it through mechanical stress: without stress, bone loses mineral and collagen; with stress, it gains them. So people who exercise regularly have thicker bones and greater bone density, it says, and it cites "numerous, controlled studies". It also says any type of exercise stimulates more bone tissue, and that resistance training has a greater effect than cardiovascular activity.

The passage gives no source, and I could not find specific study citations on the page I read. "People who exercise have thicker bones", on its own, compares two groups of people and cannot rule out the reverse: people whose bones are already thicker may simply like to exercise more. So I treat the claim as something to test, and turn to two meta-analyses that list the studies they included.

## Premenopausal women aged 18 to 45: no overall difference, a signal in a subgroup

[This 2025 meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.3389/fphys.2025.1672997) included only randomized controlled trials (participants randomly assigned to an exercise or control group) of adults aged 18 to 45, with exercise lasting at least 8 weeks. The review has 22 studies; the pooled bone-density analyses used only the female studies, meaning premenopausal women, 7 to 12 studies depending on the bone site. The 6 studies that included men were used only for bone turnover markers, which this post does not cover.

First, what it compares: the authors used each group's average at the end of training and asked whether the exercise group was higher than the control group. It is not a gain measured from before training.

The overall result was no detectable difference. For lumbar spine density, pooling 12 studies gave an effect size of 0.15 with a confidence interval (the range the estimate reasonably allows) from −0.09 to 0.38, which crosses 0, so the two groups cannot be told apart statistically. The authors say as much in their discussion:

> “the overall results of this study did not show a significant improvement in BMD from exercise in premenopausal women”

The difference showed up in subgroups. The authors split studies at an average age of 30. In the group under 30, lumbar spine density was higher in the exercise group, with an effect size of 0.46 (confidence interval 0.16 to 0.76); in the group over 30, the interval crossed 0. The femoral neck showed the same pattern, and whole-body density was not significant in either group.

Read the subgroup result with care:

- **Few studies.** Each age group had at most 8 trials. The authors list among their limitations that some subgroups had few studies, so a real difference might go undetected, and that exercise intensity, frequency, and type varied a lot across studies.
- **Prone to bias.** In the risk-of-bias assessment of the 22 studies, only 2 were rated low risk.
- **The age reading is an inference.** The authors read the under-30 result as exercise during a young adult stage, when bone mass still has growth potential, possibly having a more positive effect. That is their inference, not something the study measured directly.

## Children and teenagers: a signal for jumping, none for resistance training

[Another 2025 meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.7717/peerj.19616) looked at children and teenagers, including 12 studies, with bone mineral content measured by DXA. Eight studies used jumping-type exercise (jumping, rope skipping, the Brazilian martial-art dance capoeira, and so on) and four used resistance training. This one compares the change from before to after training.

In the jumping studies, the gain in lumbar spine and femoral neck bone mineral content was larger in the exercise group than in the control group. Resistance training did not reach significance, and the authors' explanation is “possibly due to the limited number of studies and methodological limitations”.

This cannot be read as "jumping beats resistance training". Each type of exercise was compared only with its own control group, not head to head in the same children. The designs are also lopsided: 4 of the 12 studies did not randomize participants, only 1 of the 8 jumping studies but 3 of the 4 resistance studies.

## Putting the two together

In these two reviews, higher bone mass in the exercise group, or a larger gain, appeared in the premenopausal young-adult subgroup and with jumping-type exercise in children and teenagers. That points the same way as the textbook's "load stimulates bone".

Three things cannot be said:

- Premenopausal women overall showed no significant difference, so "exercise always makes bone stronger" is too strong for now.
- The textbook's "any exercise works, and resistance training is better" was not tested head to head by either analysis.
- Neither review rated its evidence with GRADE (the rating of certainty of evidence, see [Understanding the Human Body: Overview](/posts/learning/2026-10-24-human-body-overview-en)), and I have not rated them either.

The two also share largely the same authors and the same funding grant, so they are not independent confirmations of each other. The mechanism the authors describe in their background (cells in bone sense force and bone formation increases) comes from other literature they cite; it is not something these analyses measured.

## Does this apply to climbing?

Neither analysis studied climbers, and in the range I searched I found no study that followed climbers' bone mass, so I cannot say what climbing does to bone. The structure does apply: the finger joints between phalanges are hinge joints and the knuckle joints are condyloid joints. How finger tendons respond to load is currently a gap; see [How Tendons Respond to Load](/posts/learning/2026-10-24-tendons-and-load-en).

## Two things you can do tonight

1. **Tell three joint types apart with your own body.** Rest your hand on a table, gently bend and straighten your index finger, and feel the middle joint and the knuckle at the base with your other hand. Then swing an arm in a big circle at your side, and bend and straighten only your elbow. You should feel that the middle finger joint mostly only bends and straightens, the knuckle at the base can also spread side to side, the shoulder turns in every direction, and the elbow has just two, bending and straightening. These match the hinge, condyloid, and ball-and-socket rows in the table. Keep it gentle and stop if anything hurts.
2. **Read one limitation, then answer three questions.** Open the discussion of the [adult meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.3389/fphys.2025.1672997), find section 4.1, and search for “did not show a significant improvement”. Then try to answer: was there an overall difference? What about the under-30 subgroup? Is the "bone still has growth potential" explanation something the study measured or the authors' inference? You should get: no significant overall difference; the lumbar spine and femoral neck did show a difference under 30; and the explanation is the authors' inference.
