> 🌏 [中文版](/posts/learning/2026-10-25-claims-without-evidence)

Some claims sound reasonable and get repeated a lot, but when you try to find a study to check them against, you do not know which one to look for. This post picks two: zone 2 training, and the idea that hanging from a pull-up bar decompresses the spine.

The conclusion first, limited to what I searched. For zone 2, I found no study that directly compares it with other intensities to see whether it is best, and the closest studies define zone 2 in different ways. For hanging from a bar and the spine, I found no direct study, and the closest evidence tests something else. Not finding something only means that, with what I found, no conclusion can be drawn yet.

My searches covered English-language public biomedical databases and web pages. I did not search paid databases or Chinese-language literature, so any research in Chinese is something I did not see.

Ten thousand steps and spot reduction are covered elsewhere because evidence for them can be found, with limits: see [How Much to Move Each Week](/posts/learning/2026-10-25-activity-and-sedentary-en) and [Body Composition and Spot Reduction](/posts/learning/2026-10-25-body-composition-and-spot-reduction-en). This post holds the claims where direct evidence is missing. How certainty of evidence is graded is explained in the [Understanding the Human Body overview](/posts/learning/2026-10-24-human-body-overview-en). This post gives no training prescription; if you have a health condition, please ask a doctor or physical therapist first.

## Claim one: zone 2 is the best intensity

**How it is usually put.** This is my summary of the common version: exercise intensity is split into five zones, and the second is low intensity, below the lactate threshold (the intensity at which blood lactate starts to rise clearly). Spending a lot of time at this intensity does the most for the mitochondria in your cells and for fat-burning ability, and so is best for heart, lung and metabolic health.

**What I looked at.** I searched Europe PMC (a biomedical literature database) for "zone 2" with training or exercise. There were many results, and most had nothing to do with exercise training or mentioned zone 2 only in passing. I also followed the reference list of a recent meta-analysis. I read the five items below; two of them I read only as abstracts.

**The closest studies, and what each answers.**

1. **A meta-analysis of low-intensity endurance training (2026).** A meta-analysis pools the results of several studies. This one pooled 50 randomized controlled trials (trials that assign people to groups at random) in healthy adults aged 18 to 65, with training lasting at least 3 weeks. Its definition of low intensity allows several options, for example below the first lactate threshold, or a heart rate up to 75% of maximum, which is a wider range than zone 2. Compared with control groups, low-intensity training clearly raised maximal oxygen uptake (the most oxygen the body can use per minute), and the certainty of evidence for those outcomes was rated high (GRADE; see the overview). This meta-analysis did not compare low intensity with moderate or high intensity. In the full text I read, "zone 2" appears only in the introduction and the discussion, not in the abstract.
2. **An expert consensus on zone 2 (2025).** A commentary by 14 exercise scientists and coaches. The abstract starts with the current state: there is no clear consensus on how to define zone 2. After the panel's discussion, the consensus was that zone 2 is best placed just below the first lactate or ventilatory threshold (a similar turning point found from breathing data). The abstract also says the expected adaptations may not be unique to zone 2 and could be induced at slightly higher and lower intensities. This is expert opinion, not a test of outcomes. I read only the abstract.
3. **How much the ways of finding zone 2 differ (2025).** Fifty people who had cycled for more than three years did two lab tests. The coefficients of variation (the bigger the number, the more people differ) of the various markers ranged from 6% to 29%, largest when expressed as power and smallest when expressed as heart rate. The abstract describes the ventilatory threshold and the intensity of maximal fat burning as strongly aligned, but the tables in the full text show about 56% of maximal oxygen uptake for the latter and about 70% for the former, and the discussion calls them distinct intensity domains. A review pooling 42 studies (2026) likewise found that the intensity where fat burning peaks sits somewhat below the lactate or gas-exchange threshold on average, with the gap depending on participants and test method, and with group averages only, not individual information. Neither followed anyone through training, and neither compared whether zone 2 is better; the full text of the latter never uses the phrase "zone 2".
4. **A narrative review that questions the claim (2025).** A narrative review is one in which the authors choose and discuss the literature without a search method fixed in advance, so it more easily carries a stance. Its abstract says recommendations to treat zone 2 as the best intensity largely stem from observational data on elite endurance athletes. It concludes that current evidence does not support zone 2 as the optimal intensity for improving mitochondrial or fat-burning capacity, and argues for prioritizing higher intensities when training volume is lower. That is the review's position, not a plan for you, and I read only the abstract.

**Where the claim and the research part ways.** In three places. First, the term: boundaries for zone 2 differ between sources, and studies use thresholds, heart-rate percentages or maximal fat burning, which are not equivalent. Second, the people: studies of definitions and measurement mostly involve athletes or experienced cyclists, and the gap to ordinary adults has not been tested directly. Third, the comparison group: low intensity beating a control group does not show it beats moderate or high intensity.

The meta-analysis also ran a subgroup analysis: within the low-intensity range, higher intensity went with bigger gains in maximal oxygen uptake. Its discussion reads this as support for the idea that the upper end, where zone 2 sits, may work better. The result is an association, the subgroups are small, and the other health outcomes did not show the same pattern.

**Can we conclude anything now?** Healthy adults who do low-intensity endurance training improve their fitness compared with control groups. Whether zone 2 is the best intensity cannot be answered with the data at hand. The questioning review is a single review, so it is not the last word either.

## Claim two: hanging from a bar decompresses the spine

Safety first. If you have back pain, especially pain at night, leg weakness or numbness, bladder or bowel changes, pain after an injury, or fever, see a doctor promptly rather than trying hanging. If you have arm or shoulder discomfort, please ask a doctor or physical therapist first as well.

**How it is usually put.** You hang from a pull-up bar, your body weight pulls the spine apart, the discs get room and take up fluid, and tightness or pain in the back eases.

**What I looked at.** I searched Europe PMC for dead hang, passive hang and hanging from a bar combined with spine, lumbar, low back pain and disc terms, and looked at the first 25 titles. I saw no human study of hanging and back pain or discs. A search using hanging, suspension and traction with lumbar terms found none either. On the web I read two pages that discuss the claim (listed at the end of the references).

**The closest studies, and what each answers.**

1. **Hanging to take scoliosis X-rays (2023).** Thirty-three teenagers and young adults before surgery (mean age 16.9), in a retrospective study, meaning it looked back at X-rays and records already taken. They compared X-rays taken standing, bending sideways, under manual traction and hanging from a bar, to see how far the spinal curve could be pulled back. Across all structural curves combined, hanging pulled the curves further than manual traction and did not differ significantly from side-bending X-rays; by subgroup the results varied, for example side-bending gave better flexibility for smaller curves. The study measured curve flexibility and postoperative correction. It measured neither pain nor the discs.
2. **A Cochrane review of traction for low back pain (2013).** Cochrane is an organization that specializes in systematic reviews. This one included 32 randomized controlled trials with 2,762 people with non-specific low back pain (pain without a clear structural cause, with or without sciatica), searched up to August 2012, covering manual and machine traction. It concluded that traction has little or no impact on pain, function, overall improvement or return to work, and that the evidence is of limited quality; seven trials reported adverse effects, including increased pain. The force, duration and posture of traction differ from hanging, so this is only neighbouring evidence. Non-Cochrane reviews of traction have appeared since 2013, and I did not read them.

**Where the claim and the research part ways.** The claim bundles three things: whether the spine lengthens while you hang, whether the discs take up fluid because of it, and whether back pain eases. For the first, the scoliosis X-rays show that spinal curves are pulled out during a hang, but that was in scoliosis patients being imaged. For the second, I found no study. For the third, the closest traction review leans toward no clear effect, and traction is not hanging.

**Can we conclude anything now?** Whether hanging decompresses the spine or improves back pain cannot be answered with the data at hand. I found no study of its safety either; neighbouring traction trials did report increased pain, so I would not say it carries no risk.

## Re-reading both with the overview's four questions

The four questions in the [Understanding the Human Body overview](/posts/learning/2026-10-24-human-body-overview-en) apply to both claims:

| Question | Zone 2 | Hanging from a bar |
|---|---|---|
| 1. One study or many pooled? | A meta-analysis exists, but it asks about low intensity; the rest are an expert consensus and a narrative review | One 33-person retrospective study, plus a neighbouring traction review |
| 2. How certain is the evidence? | Low intensity improving fitness is rated high; for zone 2 being best there is no rating to look at | The neighbouring traction review calls its own evidence limited in quality |
| 3. Is advice separate from evidence? | The expert consensus is opinion, not an outcome study | The web pages carry individuals' views |
| 4. Do the people resemble you? | Athletes, experienced cyclists and ordinary adults mixed together | Teenagers and young adults before surgery, or people with low back pain |

When direct evidence is missing, these four questions show how far the closest research is from the claim you heard.

## Two things to try tonight

1. **Find one word.** Open the abstract of the 2026 low-intensity endurance training meta-analysis (see References) and look for the phrase "zone 2". You should not find it. To check the second half, open the full text, where it appears in the introduction and discussion. This helps you see that the study asks about low-intensity training, not about zone 2 itself.
2. **Split it into three columns.** Using the same abstract, write down on paper "who, what was done, compared with whom". You should end up with: healthy adults aged 18 to 65; at least 3 weeks of low-intensity endurance training (the abstract lists several ways of defining the intensity); control groups. Then ask yourself which group would have to be compared for the sentence "zone 2 is better than other intensities" to be tested. You should find that this study does not make that comparison.

## What this post leaves out

How to train in zone 2 and how to find your own threshold are not covered here. I did not read the reviews of polarized training (mostly low intensity with a little high intensity) in athletes. I did not read enough free full texts that compare moderate or high intensity with low intensity directly in ordinary adults, so I draw no conclusion there. The effect of hanging on the shoulders and grip is out of scope; climbers often hang from a wall or a hangboard, but that load mostly falls on the fingers and shoulders, see [Hangboard Training: When to Start](/posts/climbing/2026-10-18-hangboard-training-en), and research on the spine and low back is in [Spine, Low Back and Neck](/posts/climbing/2026-10-18-climbing-spine-back-en). I found no study specifically on climbers' hanging and the spine.
