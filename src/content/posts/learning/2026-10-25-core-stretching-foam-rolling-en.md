"Train your core and your back won't hurt." "Stretch before exercise so you don't get injured." "Roll tight muscles out with a foam roller." All three are common sayings, and the evidence behind them is very uneven in thickness.

The conclusion first: pooling many controlled trials, joint range of motion is larger after weeks of stretching, and foam rolling has less long-term evidence. Right after a session, both look about the same as other warm-ups such as walking or cycling. "Not better than other warm-ups" does not mean "useless", and it does not mean "harmful". In adults with eligible chronic low back pain, core training eased pain more than control groups did, with no significant difference among three approaches. Injury prevention is outside what these reviews analyzed.

This post splits the evidence into "acute" (right after a session) and "long-term" (after weeks of training), and asks what each result was compared with. It gives no training program. If back pain comes with numbness or weakness in the legs or feet, loss of bladder or bowel control or difficulty passing urine, a fever, or follows a fall or other injury, get medical care promptly. For other pain that persists or keeps getting worse, ask a doctor or physical therapist first; see [Does Poor Posture Cause Back Pain](/posts/learning/2026-10-25-posture-and-back-pain-en).

Most sources are systematic reviews with meta-analysis: they find studies by a fixed method and pool the results. Certainty of evidence is rated with GRADE, a system for grading how sure we can be; it is introduced in [Understanding the Human Body: Overview](/posts/learning/2026-10-24-human-body-overview-en).

## Range of motion: stretching and foam rolling

**Stretching, long-term.** A [meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jshs.2023.06.002) of 77 controlled trials (Konrad 2024) took only studies with at least 2 weeks of stretching and a non-stretching control group; that is an inclusion rule, not advice on how long to stretch. Range of motion became larger, and static stretching and PNF (contracting hard, then stretching) gained more than dynamic or ballistic stretching. The amount, intensity and frequency of stretching showed no clear influence in the cross-study average, but that cannot tell you what suits an individual.

Limits: participants were mostly recreational exercisers, results varied widely between studies, and there were signs of publication bias, meaning significant results are more likely to be published. Why does range of motion grow? The authors suggest that with smaller amounts of stretching, what changes is mostly tolerance of the stretch, not tissue structure. That is a suggestion from the literature, which this review did not measure.

**Stretching and foam rolling, acute.** [Warneke 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jshs.2024.01.006) pooled 38 studies, 34 of them in the main range-of-motion analysis, comparing stretching or rolling with other warm-up activities such as walking, cycling and strength training. There was no significant difference. The authors rated the certainty of evidence for range of motion as moderate, and study quality as only fair.

Note that this compares stretching or rolling with other warm-ups, and does not answer "one session of stretching versus doing nothing". The authors write “we are not suggesting exclusion of stretching or foam rolling from training routines”. They suggest that any activity raising muscle temperature may make range of motion temporarily larger, but studies linking temperature to range of motion are few, so that is a hypothesis and not a result. These papers all declare no conflict of interest, but the authors overlap, and I saw no independent replication; that is my judgment.

**Foam rolling, long-term.** [Konrad 2022](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-022-01699-8) pooled 11 studies and found larger range of motion after foam-rolling training. In subgroups, only studies longer than 4 weeks showed a significant change; that is a subgroup result, not advice. Rolling the calf did not increase ankle range of motion. The authors call the evidence "limited and inconsistent".

**Long-term stretching and strength.** [Warneke 2024](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40798-024-00706-8) (a different paper, same first author) pooled 42 studies with non-stretching controls: maximal strength rose slightly, with low certainty. The authors conclude that for gaining strength, resistance training is more efficient.

## Soreness and pain: foam rolling

[Wiewelhove 2019](https://doi.org/10.3389/fphys.2019.00376) pooled 21 studies, each compared with a no-rolling condition. Rolling before exercise improved flexibility slightly and left jumping and strength almost unchanged; sprinting improved a little, but the authors caution that few studies contributed and the result should be read carefully. Rolling after exercise reduced reported soreness (effect size 0.47, roughly half a standard deviation between groups). Their verdict is that effects are "rather minor and partly negligible", but may matter for flexibility and soreness, and that high-quality studies are limited, so conclusions should be treated cautiously.

Why might it work? One common explanation is that the roller breaks up trigger points, the stiff-feeling spots in muscle; the authors write “there is no concrete evidence proving that the release of trigger-points makes FR effective”, and suggest a warm-up or placebo effect could also explain it. They also cite literature that the pressure needed to deform firm fascia (the connective tissue around muscle) is greater than a roller usually applies. So "releasing the fascia" is for now only a hypothesis.

[Santos 2024](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12891-024-07276-6) found only 6 trials of foam rolling and pain, too few to pool. Two saw less pain when rolling was added; the only low back pain trial, of 42 older adults, found no significant difference. The authors conclude the results do not support clinical use of foam rolling for pain.

For recovering from soreness, see [Sleeping Less and Muscle Soreness](/posts/learning/2026-10-25-recovery-and-sleep-en).

## Core training and chronic low back pain

[Guo 2025](https://doi.org/10.3389/fphys.2025.1672010) pooled 57 randomized controlled trials (participants randomly assigned to do or not do the exercise), including adults aged 18 to 65 with low back pain for over 12 weeks and no clear cause. Whether you are in this group is for a doctor to decide. "Core training" covers Pilates, core stability and core resistance training; control groups were no exercise, standard care, or non-specific physical activity.

Pain improved more than in control groups with all three approaches, and function (on disability questionnaires) improved too. Two cautions. The pooled pain result became stable only after 10 widely differing studies were excluded. And the abstract says Pilates showed "superior effectiveness for pain relief", yet the differences among the three were not statistically significant (P = 0.24), so the ranking is a difference in numbers only.

Other limits: most studies had some concerns about bias and lasted 12 weeks or less, so long-term effects are unknown; and the control groups mixed three kinds of comparison, so the review cannot say whether core training beats ordinary exercise. Nothing in the main text analyzes adverse events, so the "safe" conclusion is unsupported there. Doses and mechanisms are the authors' inferences and are not repeated here.

## Summary

| Method | Right away (acute) | After weeks (long-term) |
|---|---|---|
| Stretching | About the same as other warm-ups (compared with each other, not with doing nothing) | Range of motion larger; maximal strength slightly higher (low certainty), both versus non-stretching controls |
| Foam rolling | About the same as other warm-ups; versus no rolling, flexibility slightly better before exercise and soreness lower after | Range of motion larger, but few studies and no gain at the calf |
| Core training | Not included | Adults with chronic low back pain: pain and function improved more than controls |

Injury prevention is outside what these reviews analyzed; Warneke 2024 says they did not analyze back pain or other benefits, such as fewer muscle and tendon injuries in changes of direction and explosive movements. For warm-ups and injury, see [Does Warming Up Prevent Injury?](/posts/learning/2026-10-25-warm-up-and-training-load-en). These reviews also did not measure fat; for "roll one spot and lose fat there", see [Does Training One Area Slim That Area](/posts/learning/2026-10-25-body-composition-and-spot-reduction-en).

## Does this apply to climbing?

Not directly. Participants were generally healthy people or ordinary adults with low back pain, not climbers, and there is no data on fingers or shoulders. For climbers, see [Climbing Warm-Up](/posts/climbing/2026-10-18-climbing-warm-up-en), [Flexibility and Mobility for Climbers](/posts/climbing/2026-10-18-climbing-flexibility-mobility-en), [Antagonist and Core Training for Climbers](/posts/climbing/2026-10-18-climbing-antagonist-core-en) and [Spine, Lower Back and Neck](/posts/climbing/2026-10-18-climbing-spine-back-en).

## Two things you can do tonight

1. **Classify the claim.** For each of these, decide acute or long-term, and compared with what: (a) "Right after rolling my thighs, I can reach the floor more easily." (b) "After eight weeks of daily stretching, my split angle got bigger." (c) "Before a run, a foam roller increases range of motion more than a jog warm-up does." You should come out with: (a) acute, only before-and-after on yourself with no comparison group; (b) long-term, versus a non-stretching control group; (c) acute, versus another warm-up, which is exactly what Warneke 2024 asked, and they saw no difference.
2. **Read one caveat from the authors.** Open [Warneke 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jshs.2024.01.006) and find “we are not suggesting exclusion of stretching or foam rolling from training routines” under 4.4 Practical applications. You should read that although the title says "not superior", the authors do not tell anyone to stop.

## What this post doesn't cover

How to do these safely, for how long, or on which body part is not covered. Acute low back pain, back pain with a specific diagnosis, older adults, and stretching for soreness are also outside these sources; please ask a doctor or physical therapist first in those cases.
