[Where Muscle Gets Its Energy](/posts/learning/2026-10-24-energy-systems-en) said that sustained activity mostly resupplies ATP through aerobic respiration, which needs oxygen. How does oxygen get from the air to the muscle? And what does "good cardiorespiratory fitness" mean, and how certain is the evidence?

The conclusion first: the path of oxygen is clear. The lungs exchange gases, the heart sends out the blood, and the blood carries the oxygen to muscle. As for "people with higher cardiorespiratory fitness live longer", a large 2024 summary says the association is strong; but that same summary's own GRADE assessment (a grading of how certain the evidence is, introduced in the [series overview](/posts/learning/2026-10-24-human-body-overview-en)) puts most outcomes at low to very low.

This post looks at the path of oxygen, then at the fitness evidence. After it you can say which stations oxygen passes through, and tell apart "the association is strong" from "the evidence is certain". It gives no exercise prescription; if something is wrong with your body, or you get chest discomfort, dizziness or fainting during exercise, see a doctor first.

## The path of oxygen: lungs, blood, heart, muscle

**First station, the lungs.** At the border between the [air sacs](https://openstax.org/books/anatomy-and-physiology-2e/pages/22-4-gas-exchange) and the blood vessels, oxygen enters the blood and carbon dioxide leaves it. A gas moves by itself from where it is more crowded to where it is less crowded, with no extra effort from the body; this is diffusion. "How crowded" is expressed as partial pressure, in mm Hg: oxygen is about 104 in the air sacs and about 40 in the blood that has just arrived at the lung's tiny vessels, a big gap, so oxygen enters the blood quickly. Once in the blood, most of the oxygen is carried by the hemoglobin in red blood cells, a protein that grabs oxygen.

**Second station, the heart.** The blood is [pumped by the heart](https://openstax.org/books/anatomy-and-physiology-2e/pages/19-4-cardiac-physiology). The amount of blood the heart sends out per minute is the cardiac output, equal to heart rate times stroke volume, the blood sent out per contraction. The textbook's example: at rest the stroke volume is about 70 milliliters and the heart rate about 75 beats a minute, which multiply to about 5 liters. During exercise both rise; the textbook's example is a healthy young person with a cardiac output of about 19.5 liters a minute, which it describes as about 4 to 5 times the resting level. These are the textbook's examples, not study results, and individuals vary a lot.

**Third station, muscle tissue.** At the tissue, the situation is the reverse of the air sacs: oxygen pressure in the tissue is low, so oxygen lets go of hemoglobin, diffuses out of the vessel and into the muscle, where aerobic respiration uses it. Carbon dioxide goes the other way, from the tissue back into the blood and on to the lungs.

So during exercise, what the "heart and lungs" do is this: the lungs keep oxygen entering the blood, the heart increases blood flow, and the blood carries the oxygen to muscle.

## Cardiorespiratory fitness: definition and units

[The 2024 review](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2023-107849) defines cardiorespiratory fitness as the integrated function of several bodily systems to deliver oxygen to muscle and use it. A common unit is maximal oxygen uptake, the most oxygen you can use per kilogram of body weight per minute; or it is given in METs, where 1 MET is about 3.5 milliliters of oxygen per kilogram per minute.

So cardiorespiratory fitness looks at the whole path, including how well muscle uses oxygen. Cardiac output is only one section of it.

## Fitness and health: one summary, two messages

The title of this review says cardiorespiratory fitness is a "strong and consistent predictor" of disease and death. It pooled 26 meta-analyses built on 199 cohort studies and more than 20 million person-observations. A cohort study measures fitness first and then follows people for years to see who has problems. The review is a "review of reviews", which the paper calls an overview of meta-analyses.

The result: in the meta-analyses of deaths in the general population, people with high fitness had an all-cause mortality hazard ratio (the risk as a multiple of the comparison group's) of 0.47, about half that of people with low fitness; for every 1 MET higher, all-cause mortality risk was about 11% to 17% lower, which is the result of two separate meta-analyses. The "high" and "low" here are each meta-analysis's own groupings, and I did not read a cut-off that would let you tell which group you belong to.

The authors conclude that high fitness is "strongly associated" with lower risk of death and chronic disease.

"Strong" describes the size of the association. A different question is how far we trust the estimate. The same study used GRADE to assess its own evidence, and the certainty ranged "from very low to moderate", with most outcomes at low to very low. The two statements do not conflict: an association can be large and consistent while the credibility of the evidence is still not high.

Reasons the authors list include:

- **The samples are mostly male.** Nearly all outcomes were marked down because the samples were largely male.
- **Measured once.** The evidence is limited to a single fitness measurement and later outcomes.
- **Studies differ a lot.** Inside many of the meta-analyses, the individual studies' results differ widely, and the authors often cannot explain why.
- **Confounding is not well controlled.** In studies of clinical populations, adjustment for factors such as age, sex and body mass was often lacking.
- **The included reviews are of low quality.** The authors rated the methodological quality of the 26 reviews with AMSTAR2: 11 were critically low and 4 low.

The authors also write that the result "may signal a causal relationship". Their reasoning is that the evidence pooled is from cohort studies, and this question cannot be tested in a randomized trial; you cannot randomly assign one group to a lifetime of good fitness and another to a lifetime of poor fitness. They also say repeated measurements of fitness would help to establish causality further.

Cohort studies like these share another worry: people who are already ill may have lower fitness to begin with, so "low fitness" may be a result of illness rather than a cause. All this post can say is that higher fitness and lower risk appear together, not that raising fitness will lower risk.

## Does this apply to climbing or daily activity

This evidence is about overall mortality and chronic disease risk, not climbing performance, so how climbers train their aerobic capacity is outside it. For daily activity, [How Much to Move Each Week](/posts/learning/2026-10-25-activity-and-sedentary-en) is about how much activity you do, while this post is about what your body can do; the two are related but not the same thing. For how aerobic and anaerobic supply divide the work, read [Where Muscle Gets Its Energy](/posts/learning/2026-10-24-energy-systems-en).

## Two things to try tonight

1. **Count a wrist pulse.** Sit and rest for about 5 minutes, lightly press two fingers on the inside of your wrist, and once you feel the pulse, count it for a full minute. The textbook says an adult's resting heart rate is usually 60 to 100 beats a minute. This measures only the heart-rate term in the cardiac output formula (heart rate times stroke volume), and cannot tell you how high your cardiorespiratory fitness is. Whatever the number, if you have dizziness, chest discomfort, palpitations or breathlessness, see a doctor.
2. **Tell the two "strongs" apart.** Open [that review](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2023-107849), read “strong and consistent predictor” in the title first, then find the word “certainty” in the abstract. You should read that the certainty ranges from very low to moderate. The title's strong describes the size of the association; certainty describes how far to trust the estimate.

## What this post leaves out

How to measure maximal oxygen uptake, how to train cardiorespiratory fitness, and how age and sex affect fitness are not covered, and I did not read sources reliable enough to write about them. How blood carries carbon dioxide and how vessels regulate blood flow are also outside this post.

## Textbook statements I did not use

The same chapter gives two other common numbers: keep your heart rate between 120 and 160 beats a minute during exercise, and estimate maximum heart rate as "220 minus age". The textbook itself calls the first "loosely defined" and presents the second only as an estimate. I found no source that tests either, so I do not present them as advice.
