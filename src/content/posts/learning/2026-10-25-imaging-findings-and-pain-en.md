You get an MRI or X-ray report that says "disc degeneration", "bone spur" or "calcification", and it is natural to think: so that is what is causing my pain. How reliable is that inference?

The conclusion first: the four studies I read fall into three groups, two on the spine, a Japanese community study, and a shoulder review. Degeneration-like spine findings are common in people without symptoms, and the shoulder review also found that shoulders with and without symptoms often look alike. So a report listing an abnormality cannot, on that fact alone, be called the cause of pain.

But that does not mean imaging is useless, or that you should skip a scan. In adults aged 50 or younger, some findings really are more common in people with low back pain. Whether to scan, and how to read the result, is for a clinician to decide from your symptoms and examination.

This post covers only the spine and shoulder; for the knee, hip and neck I did not read a sufficiently reliable source, so I did not write about them. After it you can tell prevalence from causation, and know what to ask a clinician when you get a report. For how the experience of pain itself works, see [What Is Pain](/posts/learning/2026-10-25-what-is-pain-en); for posture and back pain, see [Posture and Back Pain](/posts/learning/2026-10-25-posture-and-back-pain-en).

## When not to wait: see a doctor

If a report shows an abnormality, or pain persists or keeps getting worse, go back to your doctor. In the situations below, get care promptly, and go to the emergency department when it is severe:

- **Emergency or same-day care:** loss of bladder or bowel control or being unable to pass urine, numbness in the groin and inner thighs (the saddle area), or leg or foot weakness that is getting worse fast. Shoulder pain together with chest tightness, chest pain or shortness of breath also belongs here, because heart problems have to be ruled out first.
- **Get care promptly:** fever, unexplained weight loss, a history of cancer, pain that wakes you at night or is there even lying down, a shoulder you suddenly cannot lift after an injury, or leg numbness or weakness.

This list is general safety advice, not something from these four studies, and it is in the same spirit as the one in Posture and Back Pain.

## Two questions to keep apart

"Of people without symptoms, how many have an abnormality on imaging?" That is a prevalence question. "If you have this abnormality, is that why you hurt?" That is a causation question.

The first can be answered by a survey: scan a group of people without symptoms and count. The second is much harder, because imaging findings and pain can show up together simply because both increase with age. The evidence here is mostly of the first kind, and at most reaches association.

## The spine: in people without symptoms, more common with age

The [2015 systematic review by Brinjikji and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.3174/ajnr.a4173) collected 33 articles covering 3,110 people without symptoms who had had CT or MRI. The authors estimated prevalence by age group: disc degeneration rose from 37% at age 20 to 96% at age 80, and disc bulge from 30% to 84%.

Their conclusion says that many degenerative imaging features are "likely part of normal aging and unassociated with pain", and that findings must be interpreted in the context of the patient's clinical condition. The "likely" is the authors' inference; this design cannot prove it directly.

I did not get the full text of this paper this time and read only the abstract, so I do not know how it rates study quality or the certainty of the evidence.

## But some findings are more common in people with pain

The same group did [another meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.3174/ajnr.a4498) (pooling the results of several studies), looking only at adults aged 50 or younger: 14 studies covering 3,097 people, 1,193 of them without symptoms. The abstract reports that disc bulge, degeneration, extrusion and protrusion, among others, were more common in people with low back pain.

For example, the odds ratio for disc degeneration was 2.24 (95% confidence interval 1.21 to 4.15): the odds of a degeneration finding in people with low back pain were somewhat more than twice those of people without symptoms. Disc bulge had an odds ratio of 7.54 with an interval of 1.28 to 44.56, so the direction is "more common" but the size of the multiple is not reliable. Other findings, such as any Modic change (a signal change near the vertebral end plates) and spondylolisthesis, showed no difference; "no difference seen" is not "shown to be unrelated", since the samples may simply be too small.

Putting the two abstracts together: degeneration-type findings rise with age and are common without symptoms; in adults aged 50 or younger some findings are more common with pain and some show no difference. "More common" is an average over a group and cannot be applied to one person. Again I read only the abstract and did not get the full text.

## A community study from Japan: imaging findings, yet daily function intact

The [2026 study by Teraguchi and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.14245/ns.26520312.0156) comes from a community in Wakayama, Japan, and I read the full text. It is a cross-sectional study. The 866 participants averaged 66.4 years old and all had a spine MRI; 347 of them said they had low back pain on most days in the past month.

The study sorted these 347 people with the Oswestry Disability Index (ODI, a questionnaire on how much daily activity is affected), where 20% or below counts as minimal limitation. About 63% fell in that range: they had pain, but daily life was mostly normal.

Disc degeneration (counted if any lumbar disc reached grade 4 or 5 on the imaging scale) was very common in every group: 91.5% and 95.4% in the two groups with low back pain, and 88.6% and 87.4% in the two without.

Before adjustment, two differences are worth knowing: disc degeneration was more common in those with better function (95.4% versus 91.5%), while Schmorl's nodes (small pits where disc material presses into the neighboring vertebra) were less common in them (32.9% versus 45.7%). The authors then used a multivariable analysis (taking several factors into account at once; the text says age was adjusted, while the abstract also lists sex, obesity, pain intensity and physical performance, so the paper is not fully consistent). None of the three imaging findings was independently associated with functional status. For disc degeneration the odds ratio was 1.19 with an interval of 0.46 to 3.08. That interval is wide, so the more accurate statement is that this dataset found no independent association but cannot rule out a moderate one.

The authors think function measures such as walking speed and grip strength separate functional status better than imaging does. One caution: the groups were defined by the ODI, so the ODI separating function is true by definition, and the authors themselves treat grip and walking speed only as complementary descriptions. This is the authors' judgment, not independent validation.

Of the limitations the authors list, two matter most for interpretation: the cross-sectional design shows association only, and the participants were mostly older Japanese adults, so applying the results to younger people or other groups calls for care.

## The shoulder: numbers vary widely across studies, and shoulders with and without symptoms are often alike

The [2025 systematic review by Ibounig and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13018-024-05378-4) looks at the acromioclavicular joint (where the collarbone meets the top of the shoulder) and the subacromial space, and I read the full text. It includes 31 studies with usable data, using X-ray, ultrasound and MRI.

The review used an adapted GRADE approach (the grading of certainty of evidence introduced in the [series overview](/posts/learning/2026-10-24-human-body-overview-en)) and rates certainty as "very low", the lowest level, mainly because the studies were prone to bias, the estimates were imprecise, and the study populations did not match the question well. Because the studies differed too much, the authors did not pool the numbers.

The spread is large. Only one study used a general population: just 20 shoulders, participants averaging 65 years old. Among its shoulders without symptoms, acromioclavicular osteoarthritis was found in 85% on MRI and 95% on X-ray. In the other studies (volunteers, healthcare populations, athletes), averaging 41 years old, shoulders without symptoms showed it in 6% to 47% on X-ray and 0% to 82% on MRI. The age gap may be one reason. The authors say in the discussion that age had less impact than for the glenohumeral joint (the ball-and-socket joint of the shoulder), but the results section still shows a trend of rising prevalence with age.

The authors also made a descriptive comparison of shoulders with and without symptoms (no statistical test, and only some studies): abnormalities of the acromioclavicular joint and subacromial space in shoulders without symptoms were often about as common as in shoulders with symptoms. The one exception is subacromial calcification, which was more common with symptoms.

The authors' clinical advice is to be careful in linking these findings directly to symptoms, and to use imaging as a supplemental tool, mainly to confirm specific clinical suspicions or to exclude serious problems such as tumors or infections. That is the authors' recommendation, not a study result.

## Putting the four together

- Spine review 2015a: degeneration-type findings rise with age and are common without symptoms (abstract only, certainty unknown).
- Spine meta-analysis 2015b: in adults aged 50 or younger, some findings are more common with pain and some show no difference (abstract only).
- Japanese community study: among these older adults, imaging findings did not independently separate functional status (cross-sectional).
- Shoulder: shoulders with and without symptoms are often alike, with very low certainty.

All four address "how common" or "is there an association"; none followed patients to show that an abnormality causes pain, or does not. So an "abnormality" on a report should be read together with your symptoms and examination. The same finding can mean different things in different people; some may relate to symptoms and some are just the marks of age. Telling which is which takes a clinician comparing the report with your symptoms and examination.

## Does this apply to climbing or everyday activity?

None of the four studies is about climbers. The Ibounig review includes studies of athletes, but I did not read results split by sport, so I cannot say what holds for climbers. For the spine and back problems common in climbing, see [Spine, Low Back and Neck](/posts/climbing/2026-10-18-climbing-spine-back-en). The everyday use is simpler: when someone says "your back has degeneration", it may just be a finding many people have, not a sign that your back is broken.

## Two things you can do tonight

1. **Tell prevalence from causation.** Which of these four sentences is prevalence, which is causation, and which is association? (a) "Among people aged 80 without symptoms, 96% have disc degeneration." (b) "My disc degeneration is why my back hurts." (c) "In people aged 50 or younger with low back pain, the odds of disc degeneration are higher than in people without symptoms." (d) "Degeneration has made him hurt for five years."
   Answer: (a) is prevalence; (c) is association; (b) and (d) are causation, which these four studies cannot give.
2. **Prepare questions for your clinician (only if you have a report).** Copy each "finding" onto its own line and write after it: "Does this finding fit my symptoms?" Bring the list to your doctor or physical therapist; do not use it to draw conclusions yourself. If you have no report, skip this.
