"Running ruins your knees." "Don't squat too deep." "Stairs are bad for your joints." Claims like these are everywhere. This post reads several reviews of knee osteoarthritis to see what has been measured about exercise and arthritis risk. The studies I could read are almost all about the knee, so the scope stops there, and it mainly answers "will I get arthritis, will it hurt". On the structural question, "is cartilage actually being worn away", this post has only indirect evidence.

The conclusion first, with its limits in the same paragraph. In the reviews I read, walking and recreational running did not appear alongside more structural worsening of knee osteoarthritis. People with higher activity had higher risk than people with moderate activity, but no clear difference from people with low activity. Past injury and higher body weight also appeared alongside higher risk. Most of this is observational research, so it can only say things occur together, not that one causes the other. For people already diagnosed, randomised trials found that exercise therapy improved pain and function. Nothing I read directly answers the hip, squats or stairs.

After reading you should be able to separate two pairs of terms: risk factor versus diagnosis, and association versus cause. This post gives no exercise prescription. If your knee is painful, swollen or locking, or you have just been injured or already diagnosed, ask a doctor or physical therapist first, and do not use this post to decide for yourself.

## A few terms first

**Osteoarthritis** (arthritis below) is a long-term disease of the whole joint. The introduction of one systematic review says it involves cartilage, meniscus, ligaments and the surrounding muscles, not only thinner cartilage. For how joints are built, see [Bones and Joints](/posts/learning/2026-10-25-bones-and-joints-en).

**Diagnosis** is a judgement a doctor makes from symptoms and tests. The same introduction says about a third of people over 60 show knee arthritis on X-ray, and that symptoms and X-ray findings often do not line up; for imaging and pain see [Imaging Findings and Pain](/posts/learning/2026-10-25-imaging-findings-and-pain-en). That does not mean pain can be ignored: pain, swelling or locking are reasons to have a doctor assess you, whatever the image says.

A **risk factor** is a condition that occurs alongside a higher chance of getting a disease. It is not a diagnosis and does not mean you will get it. **Association** means two things occur together; **cause** means one produces the other. Observational research can support association at most. What systematic reviews, meta-analyses (pooling several studies' results) and "certainty of evidence" (how much to trust an estimate) mean is covered in [Understanding the Human Body: Overview](/posts/learning/2026-10-24-human-body-overview-en).

## Where the "wear and tear" idea comes from

[A review that summarises earlier reviews](https://doi.org/10.5152/eurjrheum.2022.21046) (published 2024, covering reviews from 2005 to 2020) says that for a long time a common belief was that exercise speeds up damage in the knee, with arthritis seen as gradual "wear and tear". It also says current guidelines list exercise as a first-line way to manage knee arthritis pain and function.

Keep two questions apart: "does exercise improve pain and function?" and "is exercise good or bad for the joint's structure?" This overview says there is far less research on the second. Tendon adaptation to load does have human studies (see [How Tendons Respond to Load](/posts/learning/2026-10-24-tendons-and-load-en)); I did not read comparable evidence for cartilage, so tendon results cannot be carried over.

## Walking and recreational running: not appearing together

The overview concludes that walking, running and some recreational sports are not related to structural worsening of knee arthritis. It pulled together 20 reviews plus 12 original studies the authors chose. It searched only PubMed and most of its reviews are narrative, so it works more like a curated guide than an exhaustive search.

Another [systematic review](https://doi.org/10.1177/23259671231152900) compared runners with non-runners: 17 studies, 7,194 runners and 6,947 non-runners, 5 of the studies without a control group.

- **Knee pain:** reported in only 5 studies. Pooled, runners had a lower share, 28.2% (384 of 1,360), against 41% (1,658 of 4,041) in non-runners. The gap comes mainly from one large study; another went the opposite way but was not significant.
- **X-ray and MRI:** several studies found no significant difference between the groups in arthritis rates or cartilage thickness. One study (of female former athletes) found more bone spurs and more narrowing of the space between kneecap and thigh bone in runners.
- **Joint replacement:** in one study, 2.6% of runners and 4.6% of non-runners; in another, no significant difference.

The authors' conclusion is limited to the "short term". Most studies were retrospective (looking back after the fact), which the authors say cannot track change before and after running. The groups also differed in average age (about 56 versus 62). The authors raise selection bias too: people who can keep running for years may have healthier joints to begin with, so a difference between groups is not necessarily caused by running. Runners also had a lower average body mass index (a height-and-weight measure).

So the result supports "runners were not seen to be worse off", not "running improves joints", and certainly not "knee pain can be ignored".

Three of this review's seven authors declare having received consulting or speaking fees from orthopaedic or therapy companies. The declaration does not say how those relationships relate to this study. I did not adjust my reading of the numbers because of it; I mention it so you know.

A [meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joca.2022.09.013) of which I read only the abstract found that right after a run, knee cartilage thickness and volume were about 3% to 5% lower, and a cartilage-composition measure in the joint between the thigh bone and shin bone returned to pre-run levels within 91 minutes. The authors rated the certainty of the evidence very low and inferred that a single run does not appear to harm knee cartilage; changes in the hip are unknown.

## Higher activity, competitive sport, injury, body weight

Activity volume gives a more complicated picture. A [2025 meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.7189/jogh.15.04173) pooled 14 observational studies, 507,696 people and 27,412 new cases of knee arthritis. These are relative risks, with confidence intervals in brackets:

- High activity versus low: 1.02 (0.84 to 1.23).
- Moderate versus low: 0.94 (0.84 to 1.05).
- High versus moderate: 1.26 (1.17 to 1.37).

The first two ranges include 1, so a difference cannot be ruled out as absent. The third does not: with moderate activity as the baseline, high activity came with higher risk. The three numbers come from different comparisons, so they cannot be converted into one another, and they cannot be matched to your own activity.

Four cautions. First, this is observational research, and the studies disagree widely: for high versus low, the inconsistency between studies is 98.9% (the measure is called I², and the closer to 100% the less the studies agree). Second, "high" and "low" are each study's own cut-offs; 8 studies counted more than 150 minutes a week as "high", so this "high" is not necessarily a very large amount. Third, the authors suggest in the discussion that there may be a threshold beyond which risk rises, but that is interpretation; these numbers cannot prove it. Fourth, the authors' abstract and conclusion use phrases like "dose-dependent" and "significantly increases the risk", which is stronger than this kind of data supports and does not fit their own non-monotonic results (0.94, 1.02, 1.26); they use "association" only in the implications paragraph.

So this section may look like it clashes with the closing point that current evidence only shows meeting activity guidelines does not make knees worse. The reason is that the studies group activity differently, and this post cannot reconcile them.

The overview also cites other work: people with a past knee injury had about 4.2 times the odds of knee arthritis, and people with obesity about 4.55 times the risk (odds and risk are calculated differently; take both simply as "clearly higher"). Competitive sports such as soccer, long-distance running, weightlifting and wrestling also occurred alongside higher arthritis odds, but these sports cause many injuries, so the result may be injury-driven, and the reviews do not agree with each other. All of this is second-hand and I did not go back to the originals. The overview's authors think injury and obesity, rather than exercise itself, are the main drivers; that is interpretation, not something measured directly. Injury is a risk factor, which is not the same as exercise leading to injury; whether to address body weight is not discussed here, so talk to a doctor.

## People who are already diagnosed

If you have been diagnosed, talk to a doctor or physical therapist before you move. What follows is only what the research says, not advice.

The above is about whether someone gets arthritis; this asks whether exercise helps symptoms, which randomised trials can answer. A [2025 BMJ systematic review and network meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj-2025-085242) (a meta-analysis that compares several interventions at once) included 217 trials and 15,684 people with knee arthritis. Compared with controls, aerobic exercise probably reduced pain a lot in the short term (about 4 weeks) and mid-term (about 12 weeks), with moderate certainty; other types also helped somewhat, with certainty moderate or low.

The limits matter too:

- **Quality:** 76% of comparisons had "some concerns", 21% were at high risk of bias, and only 2% at low risk.
- **Small-study effects:** the authors' test showed possible asymmetry for pain at 4 and 12 weeks, which suggests small studies may make the effect look larger, and those are exactly the two results cited above.
- **Long term:** at 24 weeks, no type of exercise passed the authors' conservative threshold for a clinically meaningful difference.
- **Scope:** the trials were all in people with knee arthritis, and some trials excluded people with a history of knee surgery or with knee and hip arthritis at once.

On safety, look at the data first: only 40 studies (18%) reported adverse events, and the ranges were wide and crossed 1 for every type; for most types the point estimate was even above 1, but the ranges are too wide to conclude anything. The authors conclude exercise therapy is safe, but that is stronger than the data support. The more accurate reading is that no clear difference was seen and the sample cannot confirm safety, which is not the same as having shown it is safe.

## Does this apply to the hip, squats or stairs?

Mostly not directly.

- **Hip:** the abstract above says hip cartilage changes are unknown, and within what I searched I found no recent hip arthritis review with readable full text.
- **Squats, stairs:** nothing I read grouped people by these movements. The systematic review's introduction lists repeated kneeling or squatting at work as a risk factor, but that is second-hand and gives no setting. So it cannot be read as saying gym squats are harmful.
- **Finger joints and climbing:** these studies did not measure fingers, and I read no knee data on climbers, so the results cannot be carried over. For climbing injuries see the [climbing injury overview](/posts/climbing/2026-10-18-climbing-injury-overview-en).

## Two things to do tonight

1. **Read one table.** Open [the runners versus non-runners systematic review](https://doi.org/10.1177/23259671231152900) and find the Total row at the bottom of Table 3 (Knee Pain). You should read: runners 384/1,360 (28.2%), non-runners 1,658/4,041 (41%). This is the share in one review's runners and non-runners, not evidence that running protects the knee.
2. **Write one limitation for that result.** First write "In these studies, runners had a lower share with knee pain." Then go to the Limitations section of the same paper, find the term "selection bias", copy the example the authors give, and turn it into "So we can't say running reduces knee pain, because...". You should copy out something like: runners may have had healthier joints to begin with. Doing this practises "association is not cause".

## What this post doesn't cover

The hip and finger joints, the separate risks of squats and stairs, teenagers and long-term competitive athletes, how people with different body weights or injury histories should move, and the right amount for any one person are all outside this post. There is also almost no tissue-level evidence on how cartilage itself responds to load: of the two most relevant reviews on running and cartilage, I read only the abstract of one. Whether exercise can prevent arthritis is unanswered too: the overview's authors say current evidence only supports that meeting activity guidelines does not make knees worse, not that exercise protects joints.
