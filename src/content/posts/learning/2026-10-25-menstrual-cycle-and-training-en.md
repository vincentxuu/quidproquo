Does the menstrual cycle affect training? Some people notice certain days feel harder. Others have heard "lift heavy in the first half, go easy in the second." Such claims are often stated with great confidence.

The conclusion first: most of the sources I read say that differences between cycle phases, if present, are small. They also repeat that research quality in this area is low and that studies define "which phase" in inconsistent ways. The research is mostly on women with regular cycles. It does not cover irregular cycles, polycystic ovary syndrome or endometriosis, and the sources I read did not cover transgender or non-binary people either.

This post looks at three things in turn: how phases are defined, the evidence on performance, and the evidence on muscle gains. After it you can check a "cycle and training" claim: how was the phase determined, who were the participants, and what was measured. It does not tell you how to train in any phase. If your periods that used to come have stopped for several months, cramps are severe enough to disrupt daily life, the amount or length of bleeding worries you, you feel constantly overtired, or you are worried about your eating or weight, ask a doctor first rather than explaining it by the cycle.

## How to read the three sources

- **McNulty et al. 2020**: a systematic meta-analysis comparing exercise performance of women with regular cycles across phases. A meta-analysis pools data from several studies; for how confidence in evidence is graded, see the [Understanding the Human Body overview](/posts/learning/2026-10-24-human-body-overview).
- **D'Souza et al. 2023**: a physiology review. Only one topic, fuel use of fat and carbohydrate, was meta-analyzed; the performance and muscle sections are the authors' narrative summaries, and they say plainly that these reflect "our view".
- **D'Souza et al. 2026**: a randomized controlled trial, a single study of 24 people.

The last two come from the same lab, with the same first and corresponding authors, so the three sources are not fully independent. The 2026 paper also discloses funding and interests. The corresponding author has received research funding from Nestlé Health Sciences, FrieslandCampina, the US National Dairy Council, Dairy Farmers of Canada and Cargill, among others; has received travel expenses and speaking honoraria from some companies; and holds patents licensed to Exerkine Inc., reporting no financial gains from them. FrieslandCampina supplied the pea protein the participants took after training, and the study itself was supported by a Canadian NSERC grant. These funders relate to protein supplements, not to how to schedule training around the cycle. I state the facts and draw no inference about motives.

## "Which phase" is hard to define

The cycle divides at ovulation: the first half is the follicular phase and the second half is the luteal phase. Each half can be split further into early and late parts, about six sub-phases in all.

Textbooks often draw a 28-day curve with ovulation on day 14. D'Souza 2023 points out that even among regular cycles, length can differ by up to 14 days. It differs more between people and, less often, within the same person. Estrogen and progesterone levels also vary widely from person to person.

The 2026 trial gives a concrete example. Its 24 participants had passed strict screening: cycles of 21 to 35 days and a detected sign of ovulation. Yet once tracked, cycle length ranged from 21 to 39 days, and the follicular phase from 10 to 27 days.

This leads to the quality problem. D'Souza 2023 says many studies recruit "naturally menstruating" women and verify the cycle at only one phase, so the timing of the other phases is just an estimate. The authors think this has lowered the trustworthiness of the evidence and may have produced unreliable conclusions. The 2026 paper adds that earlier studies favoring the follicular phase estimated phases from calendar counting or basal body temperature, while it used ovulation test strips and blood tests to confirm them.

## Performance: tiny differences, low-quality evidence

McNulty 2020 included 78 studies comparing performance of women with regular cycles across phases. Of these, 51 could be used for the first analysis and 73 for the second.

- Comparing the early follicular phase, just after a period starts, with all other phases combined, performance may be slightly lower, by a small amount. The effect size is about -0.06; an effect size puts the gap on a common scale, where 0 means no difference and the farther from 0, the bigger the gap.
- The plausible range for that estimate is -0.16 to 0.04. It crosses zero, so it cannot rule out no difference at all.
- A finer pairwise comparison found the largest gap between the early and late follicular phase, an effect size of about -0.14.

The authors rated the overall evidence "low". Their rating method was adapted from GRADE and is not a formal GRADE level. Of the 78 studies, 42% were rated low quality, 26% very low, 24% moderate and 8% high.

The authors' conclusion is cautious: because the effect is trivial, the variation between studies is large and many included studies are poor, general guidelines cannot be formed, and they recommend a personalized approach based on each person's response.

D'Souza 2023 adds that these studies are far from real competition: of the 78 studies, only 3 enrolled athletes. Of 51 strength outcomes, only 1 closely reflected real-world sport performance. It also cites a Danish survey in which 57% of elite female athletes used hormonal contraception, so results for naturally cycling women may not apply to them.

## Feelings deserve attention, and the cause is unclear

D'Souza 2023 also summarizes athletes' own perceptions: in one survey it cites, two-thirds of Australian Olympic and Paralympic athletes believed their cycle affected performance. It also describes a more carefully controlled study of women with regular cycles, in which changes in performance were related to psychological state, such as motivation, mood and pain, but not to changes in hormone levels. That is one study; neither its sample nor its conclusion should be stretched.

The authors' inference is that performance changes may come from cycle-related symptoms rather than hormone levels themselves. They also admit there is no reliable way to assess menstrual symptoms in athletes, and that it is hard to tell whether symptoms come from the cycle or from sleep, illness, injury or nutrition.

## Muscle gains: the 2026 randomized trial

"Train more in the first half, less in the second, and you gain more" comes from a few earlier studies. The 2026 randomized controlled trial was designed to test it. In such a trial participants are assigned to conditions at random, so cause and effect is easier to judge than in observational studies.

The main design points:

- 24 young, healthy women with regular cycles, no regular lower-body weight training in the past six months and no hormonal contraception, trained for three complete cycles, about 12 weeks.
- Each person's two legs were randomly assigned to one of four conditions: no training, steady training volume, more training in the follicular phase, or more training in the luteal phase. Each person received two of the four, one per leg.
- Total training volume was matched by design, and the volume actually completed did not differ significantly among the three training conditions.

The result: in thigh lean tissue mass (weight excluding fat), muscle cross-sectional area and 1RM strength (the heaviest weight you can lift once), all three training conditions improved more than the untrained leg, and the three training conditions did not differ from one another. The authors conclude that scheduling volume by cycle phase gave no advantage over steady volume in these participants. They also say that adjusting by phase can follow personal preference but is not necessary; that is the authors' position, not something the experiment measured directly.

The authors also ran "equivalence tests", which check whether the gap between groups is small enough to count as the same. For thigh lean tissue mass, all comparisons passed; for strength, results were mostly inconclusive, so it cannot be said that strength definitely does not differ.

The limits the authors list include: three cycles may miss subtle or cumulative effects; whether people with hormonal disturbances, more severe menstrual symptoms or more training experience would respond differently is unclear; and the findings may not carry over to hormonal contraceptive users. Beyond that, the study measured only the thigh, split phases into just two, and enrolled young women who were untrained or recreationally active. Those points are this post's observations, not the authors' list.

The study also collected subjective measures, in additional analyses run after the fact, whose results are only leads. A composite score of self-rated fatigue, sleep and soreness did not differ clearly between phases.

## Recovery and injury: my sources have no answer

Readers often ask about these two, but what I read gives no direct answer.

On recovery, D'Souza 2023 discusses mostly mechanisms, such as how estrogen affects muscle repair, but the review it cites says the evidence is limited and inconclusive. For the evidence on sleep and soreness, see [Sleeping Less and Muscle Soreness](/posts/learning/2026-10-25-recovery-and-sleep-en). On injury, none of the three sources I read analyzed injury risk by phase; two studies used a recent injury only as an exclusion criterion. So if someone says one phase makes injury more likely, within what I searched I found no source supporting that.

## Hormonal contraception

D'Souza 2023 relays another meta-analysis (Elliott-Sale and colleagues): women using oral contraceptives may, on average, perform slightly worse in a single bout of exercise. This is secondhand; I did not read the original. Please do not stop or change a pill because of this sentence; whether to use one, and which, is a question for a doctor.

## Does this apply to climbing?

There is no climbing-specific data. The sources I read measure general exercise performance and thigh muscle, not fingers, shoulders or climbing grades, and I did not find a systematic review of climbers and the menstrual cycle. For how to think about climbing training, see [Plateaued in Climbing, Should You Start Training?](/posts/climbing/2026-10-18-climbing-training-diagnosis-en); for the broader strength evidence, see [How Heavy, How Many Sets, To Failure or Not](/posts/learning/2026-10-25-strength-and-hypertrophy-en).

## Two things to try tonight

1. **Find how the review declines to give general guidance.** Open the Key Points of [McNulty et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-020-01319-3). You should read this sentence: "Practically, the current evidence does not warrant general guidance on modulating exercise across the menstrual cycle." The first item in the same Key Points says the possible drop in performance is “trivial” in size.
2. **Look at one concrete range of cycle lengths, then check a claim.** Open [D'Souza et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0000000000004031) and find “cycle length spanning 21–39 d” under Menstrual Cycle Tracking in the Results. You should read that participants all entered with cycles of 21 to 35 days, yet cycles of up to 39 days appeared during follow-up. Then take one "cycle and training" claim you have seen and ask three questions: was the phase confirmed by blood or urine tests, or counted from a calendar? Who were the participants? What outcome was compared? You will probably find the first answer is missing. There is no need to name the post; just discount it in your head.

## What this post leaves out

How to plan a program around the cycle is not covered. Menopause and after, adolescents, pregnancy and the postpartum period are also outside these sources; for evidence on muscle and bone with age, see [How Muscle Strength Changes With Age and Whether Exercise Helps](/posts/learning/2026-10-25-aging-muscle-and-bone-en). For unusually heavy bleeding, severe cramps, overtiredness, or concerns about eating and weight, ask a doctor; I did not read a source reliable enough to discuss standards or numbers for those situations.
