You practice the same movement for a week and it gets smoother. Does that mean you have learned it, or only that your hands feel good right now? [How Much of a Beginner's Early Strength Is Nerves and How Much Is Muscle](/posts/learning/2026-10-25-neural-adaptation-en) covers early strength gains; [Does Warming Up Prevent Injury?](/posts/learning/2026-10-25-warm-up-and-training-load-en) covers preparation before practice. This post turns to skill and asks one narrow question: does the order of practice change how much is learned?

The scope first: this post does not explain how a skill forms inside the body, and it does not tell you how to order your practice. It collects the evidence on practice order in the free full texts I could read. Classic studies reported a pattern: mixing variations in random order looks worse during practice but better when tested some time later. Two 2024 meta-analyses (which combine the numbers from many studies) test the second half. Pooled, random order comes out ahead, but the difference sits mainly in lab studies; in real sport settings no firm difference shows up, and age is tangled with setting so the two cannot be separated. Study quality is mostly low and none involves climbing. If something is wrong with your body or you are rehabilitating, ask a doctor or physical therapist first; this is not a practice prescription.

## Three kinds of results

A 2021 review by Tassignon and colleagues cites a textbook definition: motor learning is “a set of processes associated with practice or experience leading to relatively permanent gains in the capability for skilled performance”. In plain words: a lasting improvement in ability that comes from practice. The key word is "lasting", so studies do not look only at practice itself.

- **Performance during practice**: how well you do while practicing.
- **Retention**: testing some time after practice and seeing how much is left. The first analysis below counts only tests given at least 24 hours after practice ended.
- **Transfer**: using a practiced skill in a situation you did not practice, for example a free-throw distance you never practiced.

Smoothness during practice alone cannot tell you whether something was really learned; you have to test again after a gap. That is why the studies below all wait a day or more.

## Two orders: blocked or random

Suppose you practice basketball shots from near, middle and far. **Blocked** means finishing one distance before the next: near near near, middle middle middle, far far far. **Random** means the order keeps changing: near far middle near middle far. The latter is called high contextual interference: you must switch every time, practice is messier in the moment, hence "interference".

The pattern was first reported in 1966 in verbal learning, and from 1979 people tested it with motor tasks. The retention review's wording is “random practice condition hinders performance during acquisition, although it facilitates retention and transfer”. The "worse during practice, better afterward" idea comes from these classic studies; the two meta-analyses did not pool results from the practice phase and only test the later tests.

## What the two meta-analyses found

The 2024 analysis by Czyż and colleagues in *Scientific Reports* included 54 studies and 2,068 people and counted only retention tests given at least 24 hours after practice. The pooled effect was medium, favoring random order. An effect size puts different tests on the same scale to show how far apart two groups are; by convention 0.2 is small, 0.5 medium and 0.8 large. Here it was 0.63, with a 95% confidence interval of 0.33 to 0.93, the range the estimate probably falls in. After removing 10 results that differed greatly, it shrank to 0.43, which the authors call small.

The differences appear in two places.

- **Setting.** Lab studies gave a large effect (0.92). Real sport settings gave 0.23, with a range from −0.16 to 0.62 that includes 0, so no firm difference can be claimed.
- **Age.** Children and adolescents showed almost no difference, adults a medium one, and people over 60 a large one, but the test for age differences was itself not significant. The discussion section is also inconsistent about adults: it says the difference was not significant in the three-level model, while the results section gives 0.63 for adults, with a range of 0.30 to 0.96.

Every experiment with people over 60 was done in a lab, while the experiments with children and adolescents were almost all in real settings (only one was in a lab), so age and setting are tangled together and cannot be separated.

The same group's transfer analysis included 34 studies and found a medium pooled effect (0.55) in the same direction, again significant in the lab and not significant in real settings.

## Where these analyses need a discount

- **Low study quality.** In the first, only 3 studies reached moderate quality or above, and the text reads “Unfortunately, studies on the CI effect are of poor quality.” In the second: “None of the included studies was assessed as strong.” They used a quality checklist, not the GRADE rating introduced in [Understanding the Human Body: A Map of How It Works, and Four Questions for Judging Fitness Claims](/posts/learning/2026-10-24-human-body-overview-en); in the parts I read I saw no GRADE rating, and I do not add one.
- **Results vary a lot.** The first analysis's overall inconsistency measure, I², was 90%; the higher the number, the less the studies agree.
- **One group.** The first paper explains that one review was split into two papers, one on retention and one on transfer. They cannot be counted as two teams reaching the same conclusion.
- **Another analysis had a weaker overall result.** A 2023 meta-analysis covering only sport settings found a small, non-significant pooled effect, also favoring random order, and its title calls the benefit a "myth". The Czyż group criticizes it for searching only a publisher database, not searching EBSCO, and not being preregistered (publishing the study plan before the study starts). That is the view of the side it challenges; I did not read that paper and cannot say who is right.

The Czyż group's own conclusion is that the effect rests mainly on lab studies of adults and older adults, and is weaker in real settings.

## Is more variation always better: differential learning

One school pushes variation to the extreme: change the method every time, never repeat the same movement, and give no feedback that points out errors. It is called differential learning. Tassignon and colleagues' 2021 review covered 27 studies. Only 12 experiments had a retention phase, and compared with other methods the effect there was medium (0.61). The comparison groups were mostly traditional practice, meaning repetition or a fixed series of exercises, often with corrective feedback.

The review's conclusion is cautious: the studies differ a lot, samples are small and risk of bias is high, so “inferences about the effectiveness of DL would be premature”. What follows is my own reading, not the authors' conclusion: in a number of experiments in Table 1, the differential-learning group got no feedback while the comparison group did, so the gap between groups may be mixed up with a difference in feedback.

## What this post cannot answer

- **Climbing.** The tasks listed in the transfer review include golf, volleyball, hockey, basketball, tennis, rifle shooting, laparoscopic surgical skills and handwriting, but not climbing; of the retention paper's task table I read only the first part. [Plateaued in Climbing, Should You Start Training?](/posts/climbing/2026-10-18-climbing-training-diagnosis) is about telling whether you are stuck on technique, mindset or fitness; this post does not answer how technique practice should be ordered.
- **"10,000 hours."** The number was popularized by Gladwell's 2008 book, which cited the 1993 study by Ericsson and colleagues. Ericsson and Harwell's 2019 review says “there was no evidence for a magical number” and that winning an international piano competition would take around 25,000 hours. Ericsson wrote the original study, so he is not a neutral party. Macnamara and Maitra redid the violin study in 2019, double-blind and preregistered, and did not reproduce the “complete correspondence” between practice hours and skill level; practice amount explained 26% of the variation in performance, against 48% in the original. Macnamara is a critic of the theory. Both papers concern expert performance in music, not the mechanisms of motor learning, and neither involves climbing.
- **"Muscle memory."** In the titles I saw, the term is also used in research at the level of muscle cells. I did not read those, so this post does not use it to explain skill.
- **Does practicing while fatigued make learning worse?** A 2026 systematic review addresses it, but I could read only the abstract. The abstract says fatigue does not uniformly impair learning: local muscle fatigue is most likely to disrupt the practice phase and sometimes retention, and retention results varied with the type of fatigue and the task. I did not read the full text and do not cite details.
- **Everything else.** How to give feedback, where to direct attention, the role of sleep in learning, and which brain regions change: I read no source on these and make no judgment. I searched only the titles of open-access papers in Europe PMC, not other databases.

## Two things you can do tonight

1. **Classify your most recent practice.** Think of a skill you practiced lately (basketball shots, an instrument, one move on a climb). Write the order of practice as a string of letters, such as AAABBBCCC or ACBABC. If you finished one variation before the next, it is blocked; if you kept switching, it is random. You should end up with a string of letters and one of the two names beside it. This is only classification; you do not need to change how you practice.
2. **Sort three statements.** Imagine these: ① "This week I practiced free throws, and my makes went up every day." ② "After a week off, I shot again and made about as many as last week." ③ "From a distance I never practiced, I still make them." Sort each as performance during practice, retention or transfer. The answer: ① performance during practice, ② retention, ③ transfer.
