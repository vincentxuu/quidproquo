> 🌏 [中文版](/posts/learning/2026-10-25-neural-adaptation)

People who start lifting weights often feel stronger within a few weeks, even though their muscles do not look bigger. A common claim is that what improves at this stage is mainly the nervous system, not the muscle.

The conclusion first: the claim is reasonable and I found some indirect support, but the direct evidence is thin. Within the range I searched (title-keyword searches of open-access systematic reviews in Europe PMC), none is dedicated to how much nerves and muscle each contribute in a beginner's first weeks. The people studied and the training durations in the three sources I read are not "a beginner's first weeks", and I will keep pointing that out.

A systematic review searches for, screens and summarizes existing studies by rules set in advance; for how to read evidence, see [Understanding the Human Body: Overview](/posts/learning/2026-10-24-human-body-overview-en). After reading, you should be able to say, for "beginners get stronger through the nerves", which evidence is direct, which is indirect, and which is only the authors' inference.

It gives you no program. If something is wrong with your body, or you are unsure whether you can do resistance training, ask a doctor or physical therapist first.

## First, what might the nerves change

A motor unit is one motor neuron plus the muscle fibers it supplies; when more force is needed, more and larger motor units are switched on, which is called recruitment. For details see [How Muscles Contract](/posts/learning/2026-10-24-how-muscles-contract-en); the concept is also covered in OpenStax's textbook, [section 10.4](https://openstax.org/books/anatomy-and-physiology-2e/pages/10-4-nervous-system-control-of-muscle-tension).

So "neural adaptation" roughly means that the muscle does not grow, yet the nerves drive it more effectively. The possible steps include recruiting more motor units and firing faster (the motor unit's neuron sending more signals per second). These are conjectured steps; the sections below look at whether experiments support them.

## Indirect evidence: train one side, the other gets stronger

Cross-education means training one limb and seeing the untrained limb on the other side improve too. [Altheyab and colleagues (2026)](https://doi.org/10.1113/EP091881) is a systematic review with meta-analysis (pooling the numbers from several studies) of randomized controlled trials, comparing "train one lower limb" with a control group and measuring the side that was not trained. The inclusion criterion was healthy adults, not specifically beginners, and I did not tabulate the training duration of each study. Twenty studies went into the pooled calculation.

The untrained side scored higher than the control group on 1RM (the heaviest weight you can lift once), maximal voluntary contraction and several kinds of peak torque. The effect sizes (the standardized size of the gap between two groups) were roughly 0.4 to 0.6, medium by common convention, though the authors did not grade them that way. Certainty varied a lot between outcomes; for 1RM it was only the lowest level.

What about muscle structure? Only 2 studies measured pennation angle (how steeply muscle fibers are angled) and only 3 measured muscle thickness; neither showed a detectable difference from controls. But with so few studies and wide confidence intervals, that means no change was detected, not that the muscle certainly did not change.

The direction fits the neural story: the untrained side showed no detectable muscle growth, yet its strength rose. In their introduction, citing earlier studies, the authors say this effect is rarely accompanied by gains in muscle mass or structure and that neural adaptation is thought to underlie it, though the exact mechanism remains unidentified.

This review did not measure nerves directly, and it measured the untrained side. Carrying it over to the trained side's first weeks is my inference.

## Direct evidence: measuring motor units

[Elgueta-Cancino and colleagues (2022)](https://doi.org/10.3389/fphys.2022.817631) asked whether resistance training changes motor unit firing. They found only 7 studies with 167 participants, including non-randomized ones, and "All studies investigated men only."

Four of those studies (82 people) could be pooled; motor unit discharge rate did not change significantly, but the studies differed a great deal, and certainty was the lowest level. Other measures showed some changes, not always in the same direction, with certainty from the lowest level to moderate. The abstract concludes that high-quality evidence is lacking, and none of the 7 studies was rated low risk of bias (study design unlikely to produce distortion).

This does not mean there is no neural adaptation; it looks more like the studies measuring it directly are few and small, so the picture is unclear. It is also not clear which neural factors matter most.

## The muscle side

In their discussion, Elgueta-Cancino and colleagues write that neural changes are most notable with short-term resistance training, while long-term strength gains come mainly from changes in muscle form. That sentence has no citation attached; it is the authors' view.

[Aslam and colleagues (2025)](https://doi.org/10.3389/fphys.2025.1598149) write that neural adaptations are a defining feature of early strength gains, especially in recreational trainees. This is a narrative review: the authors describe their approach as narrative, and although they list search and inclusion criteria, in the parts I read I saw no study-by-study risk-of-bias assessment, and its topic is elite versus recreational trainees. By study type it is a weaker kind of evidence, so I use it only for background. The same paper also cautions that changes seen on electromyography (a recording of muscle electrical activity) may actually be caused by muscle growth, so they cannot all be credited to the nerves.

So my reading is that both are probably moving, and the sources I read cannot give a split.

## Does this apply to climbing?

These sources contain no climbing data and did not study fingers. How much of a new climber's fast progress comes from the nervous system, I have no evidence to say. For a climbing take on training, the site has [Should You Start Training When Climbing Stalls](/posts/climbing/2026-10-18-climbing-training-diagnosis-en). Evidence on how heavy and how many sets is in [Strength and Hypertrophy](/posts/learning/2026-10-25-strength-and-hypertrophy-en); this post only asks where the progress comes from.

## Two things you can do tonight

1. **Read one review's abstract.** Open [Elgueta-Cancino 2022](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/35295567) and look for "Seven trials including 167 participants". You should read 7 trials and 167 people, with a conclusion that high-quality evidence is lacking; then look in the main text for "All studies investigated men only." and you will see that every participant was male.
2. **Take apart a new claim.** Use this one: "Only the right hand was trained, the left hand got stronger too, so it must be the nerves." Write three lines: direct evidence, indirect evidence, authors' inference. You should end up with: the indirect evidence is the cross-education meta-analysis; the direct evidence is none, because that review did not measure nerves; the authors' inference is "must be the nerves", where the sources say only that it is thought to be so and the mechanism is not yet clear.

## What this post does not cover

- **The first-weeks time window.** None of the three sources tells me the split between nerves and muscle in the "first weeks".
- **Neural detail.** I did not read about changes at the cortex or spinal cord. One transcranial-magnetic-stimulation meta-analysis ([Li and Hu, 2026](https://doi.org/10.3389/fphys.2026.1852484)) I saw only as an abstract and did not include. How a skill is learned is a separate question; see [Motor Learning](/posts/learning/2026-10-25-motor-learning-en).
- **Women and older adults.** Elgueta-Cancino includes only men; the Altheyab authors say an age-based subgroup analysis was not done because of insufficient data.
- **How to train.** Altheyab mentions that cross-education may be relevant to limb injury and stroke rehabilitation; that is the authors' outlook, and this post gives no advice. If you have such a condition, ask a doctor or physical therapist.
