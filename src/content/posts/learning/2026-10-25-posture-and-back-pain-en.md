"Poor posture causes back pain" sounds reasonable and is often treated as fact. This post asks what the research actually saw.

The conclusion first: the evidence I read supports an association between posture and back or neck pain, not a cause. And "posture" means different things, measured in different ways, in different studies. As for "fix the posture and the pain goes down", within what I searched I found no readable full-text systematic review that tests it directly. (For what a systematic review is and how to read evidence, see [Understanding the Human Body: Overview](/posts/learning/2026-10-24-human-body-overview-en).)

If back pain comes with leg numbness or weakness, bladder or bowel problems, fever, or an injury, get medical care first; details are in "When back pain should go to a doctor first" below. This post does not diagnose or prescribe.

## First question: what is "posture" measuring

A 2025 scoping review (a review that maps the studies and tools on a topic, without necessarily pooling results) catalogued the posture measures used in spinal pain research. It found 28 tools, plus measures the individual studies made up. Most were computer-based devices or time-consuming questionnaires. The authors concluded that these tools have limited clinical usefulness for back and neck pain.

Sadler 2017 shows the same problem: for "lumbar curve while standing", three studies used three instruments, and the reference points for measuring the angle also differed.

So when you see "this posture is linked to pain", the first question is how they measured posture. Standing still, or while working seated?

## The low back: the review that followed people for 12 months

Sadler and colleagues' 2017 systematic review included only prospective cohort studies: measure some body characteristics first, then follow people over time to see who later develops low back pain. It covers 12 papers and 5,459 people, mostly followed for 12 months.

The most posture-related measure is lumbar lordosis, the inward curve of the low back. Pooling three studies gave an odds ratio of 0.73. An odds ratio compares the "odds" of an outcome between two groups; 1 means no difference. Because 0.73 is below 1, the authors inverted it to 1.37: the group with the smaller curve had odds of later low back pain about 1.37 times those of the other group, or about 37% higher. These are odds, not probabilities; it does not mean 37% of people will get pain.

The result is shaky. The 95% confidence interval (the range the true difference could plausibly fall in) is 0.55 to 0.98, and its upper end almost touches 1, which is "no difference". Of the three studies, only Adams's own result was clear; the other two had intervals crossing 1, so the pooled result was driven mainly by Adams. The authors write:

> some caution is advised when interpreting these significant meta-analyses

The average ages in these three studies ranged from 12.8 to 27 years, so the result mainly comes from children, teenagers and young adults. Some of the included studies had people who had had back pain before (among the three lordosis studies, only Adams), which the authors say makes cause and effect harder to establish.

In the same review, restricted side-bending and restricted hamstring flexibility were also associated with later low back pain, but the hamstring result was dominated by one study. Back muscle strength, endurance and abdominal strength showed no statistically significant result when pooled, but few studies were included, so they cannot be ruled out.

As for a larger curve, the review did not analyze it. The same goes for "anterior pelvic tilt": pelvic parameters such as sacral slope and pelvic incidence were not measured in these follow-up studies, so the follow-up studies have no direct data. A 2026 review on handling work did include one cross-sectional field study (nurses, 13 with pain and 13 without) in which people with back pain showed more pelvic tilt while moving a load. That is a dynamic task, not standing posture, and it cannot show which came first.

## Other sources (abstracts only)

I read only the abstracts of these, so I report only what the abstracts state.

- **Chun 2017, lumbar curve.** The abstract describes observational studies comparing about 800 people with low back pain and about 900 comparison people without it. Overall, those with pain tended to have a smaller curve, but results varied widely between studies. This kind of comparison cannot show which came first.
- **Swain 2020, a review of reviews.** It gathered 41 systematic reviews: reviews that pooled data more often supported an association between exposures such as posture and low back pain, while reviews limited to follow-up studies found it harder to reach consistent conclusions. The paper's title says there is no consensus on causality.
- **The neck.** Mahmoud 2019 pooled 15 cross-sectional studies (comparing people with and without pain at one point in time) and found that adults with neck pain had more forward head posture than adults without; teenagers showed no such association, and age was a confounder (age affects both posture and neck pain). Rani 2023 pooled 26 studies and found one measure, the craniovertebral angle, differed between people with and without pain, while two other head-neck measures did not. Rani's abstract does not state the study design, but it also compares people with and without pain, so it likewise cannot show order.

## Is there evidence for "correcting posture", and why not simply say "causes"

Within what I searched, I found no readable full-text systematic review that directly tests whether correcting posture reduces pain. I found only these two:

- A 2020 systematic review of "postural education" in elementary school children included 7 randomized trials (children assigned by chance) with 2,568 children. It measured knowledge and posture habits, not pain. The trials had design gaps that make results possibly unreliable (high risk of bias), so the review's authors concluded they cannot reliably support postural education.
- The authors of the scoping review above write that the evidence on interventions with posture adjustment is conflicting; they suggest many studies lacked a reliable posture measure, so benefits could not be attributed to posture change. That is their inference; the review itself did not test it.

Not finding evidence is not proof that it does not work.

As for "causes", all the sources here are observational, so cause stays uncertain, for at least three reasons.

1. **Order.** The Sadler authors raise one possibility: once pain starts, people may change posture to ease discomfort, so a posture difference may be a result.
2. **Earlier pain.** People who have had back pain are more likely to have it again.
3. **Back pain has many contributors.** Sadler mentions psychological and environmental factors; the 2026 review mentions psychological, socioeconomic and lifestyle factors.

The scoping review's authors also argue that no universal ideal posture has been found, and that the idea that a given posture is always bad may make people fearful of moving. That is their position and reasoning.

This does not mean posture is unimportant or can be ignored. It only means these sources cannot tell you which posture should be corrected.

## When back pain should go to a doctor first

This is a general safety reminder, not content from these studies. If back pain comes with numbness or weakness in the legs or feet, loss of bladder or bowel control or difficulty passing urine, a fever, or follows a fall or other injury, get medical care promptly. This is not a complete list; for other pain that persists or keeps getting worse, ask a doctor or physical therapist first.

## Does this apply to climbing

These sources have no data on climbers. For what research supports about climbers' low back pain, "climbing hunchback" and "belayer's neck", see [Spine, Low Back and Neck](/posts/climbing/2026-10-18-climbing-spine-back-en).

What is the upper end of the pooled confidence interval, and what does it mean? 2. **Classify two claims.** Ask three things: how was it measured, was it a follow-up or a one-time comparison, and could pain have come first? "Forward head posture is linked to neck pain": against Mahmoud 2019, you should classify it as a cross-sectional finding that shows only that two things occur together. "Anterior pelvic tilt causes low back pain": you should classify it as: the follow-up studies read here have no direct data.
