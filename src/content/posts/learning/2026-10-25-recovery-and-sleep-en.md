How much does sleeping less cost you in exercise performance? And after a hard session, do massage or cold baths help with soreness?

The conclusion first. On sleep, I read two meta-analyses (pooled summaries of many studies; the [series overview](/posts/learning/2026-10-24-human-body-overview-en) explains how evidence is graded; one of them, Kong 2025, has an author employed by a sleep-related company, discussed below). Both found that, under experimental conditions, losing a night of sleep or having it shortened lowers performance, and the overall direction agrees. But these are mostly short-term, mostly male, laboratory studies. On recovery methods, none of the reviews I read gives a solid "it works", and none proves "it doesn't": the studies are small, the protocols differ, and soreness is a rating people give themselves, which is easily shaped by expectation.

After this post you should be able to separate three things: short-term lab results are not the results of a long-term habit; "no evidence that it works" is not "proof that it doesn't"; and what to check when an author has a financial interest. This post gives no sleep hours and no recovery routine.

If you have long-term trouble sleeping, are very sleepy in the daytime, or have been told you snore and stop breathing in your sleep, see a doctor rather than relying on an article about exercise recovery.

## Sleeping less: what two meta-analyses found

[Kong and colleagues 2025](https://doi.org/10.3389/fphys.2025.1544286) pooled 45 studies (670 people, as the authors count them); most of the studies (28) used athletes and the rest used healthy non-athletes. These were randomised experiments: the same people (or groups) slept normally on one occasion and had their sleep removed or cut on another, with the order or the groups assigned by chance, and performance was then compared.

In athletes, aerobic endurance, explosive power, maximal force, speed and skill control were worse, and rating of perceived exertion (RPE, the effort score people give themselves during exercise) was higher. In healthy non-athletes, aerobic endurance was worse. Anaerobic endurance showed no statistically significant difference.

The other review, [Craven and colleagues 2022](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-022-01706-y), pooled 69 publications, with mostly male participants (89%). "Sleep loss" there is defined as 6 hours or less in 24 hours; that is a grouping rule for the analysis, not a recommended amount of sleep. Performance fell by 7.56% on average; but the studies disagreed enormously (heterogeneity I² was 98.1%; the higher the number, the less the studies agree), so that average should not be read as "you will lose 7.56%".

On which kind of sleep loss matters more, the two reviews agree on only one point: waking earlier than usual showed the clearest effects in both. For a night with no sleep at all, Craven saw a consistent drop while Kong found a significant effect only for aerobic endurance. For going to bed later, Craven saw no consistent effect, while Kong found effects on a few outcomes (skill control, perceived effort), so the two do not fully agree there. The two papers use opposite labels (Kong's PSDE means waking earlier), so I describe them in plain words. On timing, Craven found a significant drop in morning tests too, only smaller than in the afternoon; Kong also found larger effects for afternoon tests. Because both reviews cover the same field, their included studies may overlap; I did not check, so I do not treat them as independent confirmation of each other.

The authors list limits themselves:

- Kong says most studies looked at acute (short-term) effects, and the review "lacks insights into long-term effects".
- Too few women took part: Kong could not analyse sex differences, and in Craven women were about 11% of participants, also too few.
- Craven says about 98% of outcomes came from studies that measured only how long people slept, not how well.
- Kong's aerobic endurance result showed signs of publication bias (a tendency to publish only studies with results); the authors say the result stayed stable after a correction method.
- The sleep was set up in a laboratory, which differs from real competition or daily life.

## How the conflict of interest is handled

Kong 2025's conflict-of-interest statement reads: "Author GG was employed by DeRucci Healthy Sleep Co., Ltd." The other authors declare no commercial or financial relationships. The paper also says the work received no funding. I did not check what the company sells, so I do not assess the effect of this relationship; I only put it here so readers know.

This post does not rest on that paper alone: the Craven meta-analysis points the same way overall (performance falls after sleeping less), and its authors declare no conflicts of interest directly relevant to the article. The details do not fully agree, as shown above. Kong's closing recommendation, that adequate sleep is important for performance, is the authors' position, and this post does not treat it as a study result.

## Soreness and recovery: what the reviews say

Delayed onset muscle soreness usually increases in the 24 to 72 hours after exercise and subsides within 5 to 7 days (as described in the massage review). This post is about ordinary soreness after exercise. If the pain lasts longer than you expected, one side is clearly swollen, the pain is severe, a joint will not move, or anything else worries you, see a doctor rather than pushing through with recovery methods. For how the body senses pain, see [Does Pain Mean Injury?](/posts/learning/2026-10-25-what-is-pain-en).

Here are three groups of evidence.

**Massage.** [Davis and colleagues 2020](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjsem-2019-000614) pooled 29 randomised studies and looked at seven outcomes separately. They found no evidence that massage improves strength, jumping, sprinting, endurance or fatigue; only flexibility and delayed onset muscle soreness improved slightly (the authors call it small, in percentage terms: about 7% and 13%). Heterogeneity was high for both, each mainly driven by one outlier study, so the true size is still uncertain. Soreness is a subjective rating, open to the placebo effect (feeling better because you believe in the method). The authors also list their own limits: small studies, varied massage protocols and low statistical power. Their conclusion is that there is no evidence to justify massage in order to directly improve strength, sprinting or endurance.

**Cold-water immersion.** [Berjisian and colleagues 2026](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40798-026-01116-8) is an overview of reviews (a re-summary of several meta-analyses) that pooled 15 meta-analyses covering 161 studies. The authors rate the methodological quality of those reviews as low to critically low, and the certainty of evidence as low to very low (the grading is explained in the overview). Within that limit, the summary reports that cold-water immersion lowered soreness ratings and creatine kinase (a blood marker of muscle damage), and that some power and jump measures improved at 24 to 48 hours; but strength performance did not improve. Repeated use after strength training may blunt gains in strength and muscle size, and the authors point out that the clear reduction appeared when only the exercised limbs were immersed, while whole-body immersion showed no clear effect. This claim rests on a heavily overlapping group of studies; the authors call the effect on muscle size small, and the strength review had very low statistical power. The authors conclude that it should be used according to context rather than routinely after every session. For background on strength training, see [How Heavy, How Many Sets, To Failure or Not](/posts/learning/2026-10-25-strength-and-hypertrophy-en).

**Other physical recovery methods.** [Jin and colleagues 2026](https://doi.org/10.3389/fspor.2026.1876924) studied endurance athletes: 16 randomised trials, using a network meta-analysis (one that compares several methods at once) across compression garments, active recovery, massage, cold-water immersion and others. The authors conclude that no method has been shown to beat doing nothing special (passive recovery). One trial in adolescent swimmers included a sham, thermoneutral immersion group, which ranked at or above most active methods; but it is a single trial, no sham comparison reached significance, and the authors themselves say no inference about the sham immersion should be drawn. They read the pattern as a hint that expectation may not be separable from physiological effect.

Jin draws no conclusion on soreness: its soreness result depends entirely on two trials whose values were estimated rather than extracted, which the authors have not yet checked, so they say nothing in either direction. Its submitted version was found during peer review to contain coding errors, and the authors re-ran the whole analysis; they say the analysis code and arm-level data are available. This is a new paper, so I treat it as one reference, not a final word.

So Berjisian and Jin are not opposing statements about soreness: the first summarises meta-analyses that mostly saw lower soreness ratings, and the second draws no soreness conclusion; their populations and levels of evidence also differ.

## Does this apply to climbing or daily activity?

These studies mostly involve endurance athletes, team-sport athletes, or healthy adults in a laboratory, and within what I searched I found no climbing-specific sleep or recovery evidence. The sleep results are mainly short-term experiments and do not tell you what happens if you sleep a little less over a long period. If you want to think about how much training is too much, see [Does Warming Up Prevent Injury?](/posts/learning/2026-10-25-warm-up-and-training-load-en); climbers' fatigue and injury risk is something this post cannot help with.

## Two things you can do tonight

1. **Read the disclosure.** Open Kong 2025 and go to the Conflict of interest paragraph at the end. You should read that one author was employed by DeRucci Healthy Sleep Co., Ltd., and that the other authors declare no conflicts. Copy that sentence, then write down one more thing you would want to check. A reference answer: whether an independent study points the same way, or where the funding came from (Kong says it received none).
2. **Tell short term from long term.** Open the title and abstract of Craven 2022. You should read "Acute" in the title, "n = 959 [89%] male" in the abstract, and the definition of sleep loss as "≤ 6 h sleep in any 24 h period". Then ask: does this result speak to losing one night of sleep, or to habitually sleeping less over a long time? And how much do its participants resemble you?

## What this post does not cover

How long to sleep, whether naps help, and how sleep loss affects muscle building or injury risk are not answered by the sources I read, so I do not write about them. Supplements and recovery products are out of scope. For an assessment of a sleep problem itself, see a doctor.
