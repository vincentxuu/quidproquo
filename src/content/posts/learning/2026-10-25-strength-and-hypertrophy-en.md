How heavy should you lift? How many sets of each exercise? Should every set go to failure, meaning until you cannot do the next rep? The fitness world has confident answers to all three, and they conflict with one another.

The conclusion first: a recent summary gives fairly specific answers to these questions, and finds that many variables matter less than you might think. But the answers come from studies of healthy adults, not from your own program, and they contain no data on fingers or climbing.

This post looks at what evidence there is for each of the three questions, then at which parts are study results and which are only the authors' recommendations. After it you can say whether the basis of a program claim is a "systematic-review conclusion" or an "authors' recommendation". It gives you no program; if something is wrong with your body or you are unsure whether you can do resistance training, ask a doctor or physical therapist first.

## What this evidence is

The main source is the 2026 American College of Sports Medicine (ACSM) [resistance-training position stand](https://doi.org/10.1249/mss.0000000000003897). It is an "overview of reviews": the authors pooled 137 systematic reviews into one summary, looking only at healthy adults, and it updates the 2009 version. The authors call it the most comprehensive summary to date. One author formerly served on the scientific advisory board of a fitness equipment company; the other authors declare no relevant conflict.

It attaches a quality percentage to each conclusion, for example about 79% for "load on strength". That score is adapted from the idea of GRADE, the grading method introduced in the [series overview](/posts/learning/2026-10-24-human-body-overview-en). But the authors say that pooling the GRADE ratings of the individual reviews was not possible, so the percentage should not be read as GRADE's "high, moderate, low".

The other source is a [2021 analysis by Lopez and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0000000000002585), a network meta-analysis (a meta-analysis that compares three or more groups at once) comparing low, moderate and high loads, pooling 28 studies with 747 healthy adults. It included only groups that trained to failure, the studies lasted 6 to 12 weeks, and the participants averaged about 23 years old.

## How heavy

First separate what you want: strength, or bigger muscle. The answers differ.

**Strength.** The higher the load, the more strength improves. ACSM writes that load has repeatedly been found to affect strength more than it affects the other adaptations. Lopez agrees: high and moderate loads both improved strength more than low load; between high and moderate, the statistics could not tell a difference.

**Bigger muscle.** Load makes no clear difference. ACSM concludes that loads from 30% to 100% of 1RM made no difference to hypertrophy, and Lopez concludes that hypertrophy appears independent of load, but that is when every set goes to failure.

## How many sets

Two sets beat one, and one beats none, but nobody knows the best number. For hypertrophy, people doing 10 or more sets per muscle group per week improved more. ACSM also cites another analysis that points to diminishing returns beyond about 18 to 20 sets a week.

A set is one continuous round, for example 10 reps in a row.

Frequency depends on total amount: training one day a week versus more than five days a week made no difference to hypertrophy when total sets were the same. For strength, frequency does matter, but the difference shrinks when total volume is equal.

## Whether to go to failure

ACSM concludes that going to failure does not improve gains in strength and hypertrophy, so it is not necessary. But the authors also admit that there is not enough evidence to set an exact target such as "how many reps to leave in each set".

One caution: "hypertrophy is independent of load" and "failure is not necessary" each have evidence, but whether the combination of very light weights and not getting close to failure has been tested, I did not see in these two sources.

## Which are results, which are the authors' recommendations

ACSM's "primary recommendation" is that healthy adults do resistance training with high effort at least twice a week, covering the major muscle groups. This is a position the authors drew from the results, not a number obtained directly from a single experiment.

The difference between study results and authors' recommendations is clear in this statement:

- **Conclusions of the reviews:** load having no clear effect on hypertrophy and failure not being necessary come from pooling 137 reviews.
- **The authors' recommendations:** "at least twice a week" is their primary recommendation; planning by four or six body regions is, in the authors' own words, "in the view of the authors".

## Recommendations also change

ACSM points out that the 2009 recommendation that beginners train 2 to 3 times a week for hypertrophy was rated the highest quality of evidence at the time. Looking back, it rested on just three studies with 59 people in total, and two of those did not study frequency at all.

So a recommendation being rated high does not mean the evidence behind it is solid; it depends on what it stands on.

## Limits of these conclusions

Limits the authors list:

- **It cannot compare which program is better.** An overview of reviews can only see whether one variable has an effect, not compare combinations.
- **Evidence quality varies.** Randomized trials in this field are often small and of poor quality.
- **Double-counting risk.** Different reviews may include the same studies, and the overlap analysis was done only for strength.

Limits Lopez lists: the included studies were mostly of low quality, and the conclusions cannot be extrapolated to highly trained people.

Questions this evidence does not answer:

- **Specific body parts.** ACSM did not compare different body regions or muscle groups, so this evidence cannot answer for fingers or shoulders.
- **Age and sex.** Lopez's participants averaged about 23 years old, and neither source gives conclusions split by age or sex.
- **How it relates to the WHO benchmark.** [How Much to Move Each Week](/posts/learning/2026-10-25-activity-and-sedentary-en) cites WHO as finding no evidence that higher amounts of muscle-strengthening bring more health benefit. The ACSM statement here looks at muscle function and hypertrophy; the two ask about different outcomes.
- **Detail on closeness to failure.** I did not read the 2023 meta-analysis devoted to this question, so the failure section rests on ACSM alone.

## Does this apply to climbing

Not directly. The results are general measures such as strength and hypertrophy, with no data on finger strength or climbing performance. For how climbers train finger strength, the site has [Hangboard Training](/posts/climbing/2026-10-18-hangboard-training-en) and [Plateaued in Climbing, Should You Start Training?](/posts/climbing/2026-10-18-climbing-training-diagnosis-en); those draw on sports-training sources, whose evidence is no stronger than here. For how tendons respond to load, read [How Tendons Respond to Load](/posts/learning/2026-10-24-tendons-and-load-en).

## Two things to try tonight

1. **Classify a claim.** Pick a program claim, such as "every set must go to failure to work", and decide which kind it is: a conclusion from pooled reviews, an authors' recommendation, or a single case or experience. Compare: in this post, "failure is not necessary" is a conclusion of the reviews, and "at least twice a week" is an authors' recommendation. A claim with no source goes under "not found"; do not take it at face value.
2. **Read why one recommendation was changed.** Open [the ACSM 2026 position stand](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/41843416) and find “this recommendation was informed by only three studies” in the discussion. You should read that the older recommendation, rated the highest quality at the time, rested on three studies and 59 people.

## What this post leaves out

How to build a program, how to choose exercises, and how to train with an injury or a chronic condition are not covered. Why muscle gets bigger and how much of strength gain comes from the nervous system are also outside these two sources, and later posts will decide whether to write them depending on the evidence.
