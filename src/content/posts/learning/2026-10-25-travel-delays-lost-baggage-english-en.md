Your flight should have left, but the time on the departure board keeps moving. You understand delayed, yet at the counter you cannot find a way to ask when the plane is now expected to leave. Another stressful moment comes after landing: everyone is heading for the exit when you realise your cabin bag may still be on the plane.

Practise these situations separately. When a flight changes, ask what happens next. When something is missing, describe the item and where you last saw it. For check-in, hotels and ordering food, use the [earlier travel lesson](/posts/learning/2026-10-08-travel-airport-hotel-restaurant-english-en/).

## Flight delays: describe the situation, then ask for an estimate

| What you want to do | Reference expression | Source and use |
|---|---|---|
| Tell a travelling companion how long the delay is | My flight is delayed by two hours. | Adapted from a British Council announcement by changing This flight to My flight |
| Ask airline staff for the expected departure | When do you expect the plane to leave? | The question from an Oxford Online English delay dialogue, without its opening So |
| Ask about the next available flight after a cancellation | When’s the next available flight? | A sentence from the Oxford Online English cancellation dialogue |

The [British Council airport announcements lesson](https://learnenglishteens.britishcouncil.org/skills/listening/a2-listening/travelling-abroad) uses **is delayed by + a length of time**. **By two hours** tells you how much later the flight is, not what time it leaves. To ask for the current departure estimate, follow up with When do you expect the plane to leave? rather than simply replacing two hours with a clock time.

[Oxford Online English’s airport lesson](https://www.oxfordonlineenglish.com/airport-english) separates delays and cancellations into different dialogues. A **delayed** flight is later than scheduled; a **cancelled** flight is not operating as originally planned. Practise the question about the next flight in the cancellation situation. **Available** refers to a flight that can be arranged; asking the question does not itself reserve a seat.

Ask one complete question to obtain the information, then respond to the answer. You do not need to begin with a long complaint.

## A missing bag: explain the problem, then its last location

| What you want to do | Reference expression | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Tell a member of staff that your bag is missing | I’ve lost my bag. | Introduce the problem, then add the item and location |
| Explain where you think you last left it | I think I left it on the plane. | I think leaves room for uncertainty |
| Ask whether you may be able to recover it | Will I be able to get it back? | Get it back means recover the original item |

All three appear in [Oxford Online English’s Baggage Reclaim dialogue](https://www.oxfordonlineenglish.com/baggage-reclaim-listening-lesson-a1-a2). The speaker has left cabin luggage on the plane, rather than waiting at a carousel for a checked suitcase. The bag practice here follows that same situation.

**I’ve lost my bag** does not explain how it went missing. Adding **I think I left it on the plane** gives the listener a direction to investigate. If checked baggage has not arrived, explain that situation instead of memorising a statement that you left it onboard.

## Do these expressions mean the same thing?

| Expression | What it communicates | A useful next step |
|---|---|---|
| My flight is delayed by two hours. | The length of the delay | Ask about the expected departure |
| When do you expect the plane to leave? | A request for the current estimate | Listen for the time in the reply |
| I’ve lost my bag. | An item is missing; the cause is not stated | Add where you last saw it |
| I think I left it on the plane. | You remember possibly leaving it onboard | Supply flight and item details |

You can connect these sentences, but they answer different questions. First decide which piece of information you need to communicate.

## A short dialogue

This is an authored practice example. The staff member’s replies are there to help you practise responding.

> **You:** Excuse me. I’ve lost my bag.<br />
> **Staff:** Where did you last see it?<br />
> **You:** I think I left it on the plane.<br />
> **Staff:** Can you tell me your flight number?<br />
> **You:** QP123. Will I be able to get it back?<br />
> **Staff:** I’ll check with the team.

You introduce the problem; the staff member asks about the location. You give the location; they ask for the flight. Practise supplying information in stages so the conversation can continue.

Cover the right column, speak, and then compare. Exact wording is not the goal: communicate the problem and the information you need, then practise a follow-up question. You can also try different prompts in the [travel practice area (Chinese interface)](/english/#home/travel).
