"Warming up prevents injury" and "train too much and you'll get hurt" are both things you hear all the time. This post splits them into three questions: can a warm-up improve this session's performance, can it keep you injury-free over time, and how do you know you trained too much?

The short version. Performance: I read one very mixed review that points to a possible short-term benefit. Injury: a review of climbers found no association between warming up and overuse injury, while trials in young players link a structured warm-up programme to fewer injuries. Training too much: one index is often cited, but it has been heavily criticised, and I read only the abstracts of the critiques.

This post gives no warm-up routine. If you are injured, in pain, or unsure whether you can train, ask a doctor or physical therapist first. Terms such as systematic review, meta-analysis and GRADE are explained in plain language in [Understanding the Human Body: Overview](/posts/learning/2026-10-24-human-body-overview-en).

## Two Benefits of Warming Up, Two Separate Bodies of Evidence

A warm-up could make today's performance better, or it could keep you injury-free over the long run. Different experiments are needed to test each, and the evidence sits at different levels, so each has to be read on its own.

## Warm-Ups and Performance Today

[Kłobuchowski and colleagues (2026)](https://doi.org/10.3390/sports14050187) reviewed 40 studies, mostly in youth football, and did not pool them because they differed too much. I read the methods, the start of the results and the conclusions. Its abstract says dynamic warm-ups may acutely improve sprint and jump performance, and it admits that differences in design, outcome measures and populations limit comparison.

So all I can say is that some evidence points to a warm-up helping the performance of the moment, but this review is very mixed, and within the range I searched I found no sturdier source that is free to read in full. The review's title also mentions injury prevention; I did not use its injury results because the studies it gathers differ so much in design.

The site's [Climbing Warm-Up](/posts/climbing/2026-10-18-climbing-warm-up-en) post cites performance studies in climbers; I did not read those originals. It offers a warm-up routine based on advice from physical therapists and coaches, not one a trial tested directly; this post does not repeat it.

## Warm-Ups and Injury (1): Observations in Climbers

[Quarmby and colleagues (2023)](https://doi.org/10.3389/fspor.2023.1269870) reviewed overuse-injury studies in adult climbers, 34 in all. Their conclusion:

> BMI/body weight, warm up/cool downs, stretching, taping and hydration were not associated with risk of overuse injury

The key phrase is "not associated": no link was seen. In the results, the review reports three studies, two of them prospective (following a group forward in time and seeing who gets hurt), in which warming up had no impact on how common injuries were. The discussion says four studies, again two prospective. The counts differ between the two places; I follow the results section, and the site's climbing warm-up post uses four. One prospective study found higher odds of injury among climbers doing a finger-specific warm-up; the review attributes this to other factors the study did not measure. The review concludes that traditional warm-ups or cool-downs cannot be recommended with the explicit goal of reducing overuse injury risk.

Three cautions. These are observational studies: nobody was assigned to warm up; researchers just looked at who warmed up and who got hurt. Quality is modest: scores ran from 8 to 20 out of 32, and 64.7% of the studies were cross-sectional, meaning a survey at one point in time. And "no association seen" is not "warming up does nothing"; it means these data give no support.

## Warm-Ups and Injury (2): Structured Programmes That Replace the Usual Warm-Up

[Herman and colleagues (2012)](https://doi.org/10.1186/1741-7015-10-75) looked at something else: neuromuscular warm-ups needing no extra equipment, meaning strength, balance, jump-landing and agility work built into the warm-up. They included 9 studies, 6 of them randomised controlled trials.

One programme is the 11+ (later called FIFA 11+). In [Soligard and colleagues' 2008 trial](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.a2469), 125 football clubs were assigned to groups as units, and 1,892 girls aged 13 to 17 did the programme for one season (eight months). I read only that trial's abstract. Its primary outcome was lower-limb injury, with a risk ratio of 0.71, but the confidence interval, 0.49 to 1.03, crosses 1, so it was not statistically significant. The significant results were secondary: overall injuries 0.68, overuse injuries 0.47, severe injuries 0.55. A risk ratio of 0.68 means the programme group's risk was about two thirds of the comparison group's. Herman's review recalculated slightly different numbers (overall injuries 0.67, overuse injuries 0.45).

The review has limits:

- Participants were mostly young women aged 13 to 26, in football, basketball, volleyball and military training.
- The studies differed too much to add their results together; injuries were reported by coaches, the authors, players themselves or athletic trainers, and some trials were not blinded or not randomised.
- Two programmes showed no reduction in overall injuries; the review suggests low participation, a sudden increase in training intensity, or content that was too simple.

One easily missed point: in 8 of the 9 studies the comparison group did its usual warm-up, the 11+ trial included; in one the comparison group did an upper-body programme. So these trials mostly compare a structured programme with an ordinary warm-up, and do not tell you how much better warming up is than not warming up. The Herman authors also recommend doing such a programme for more than three consecutive months, at every session. That is their recommendation, not a result any single trial measured directly.

## How Do You Know You Trained Too Much? One Index, Proposed and Criticised

The best-known answer is the "acute:chronic workload ratio" (ACWR). [Gabbett (2016)](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2015-095788) proposed dividing the most recent week of training by the average of the previous weeks, to see how much more this week is than your body is used to. He defines chronic load as the rolling average of the most recent 3 to 6 weeks, and cites a study of cricket fast bowlers in which a ratio of 1.5 or more went with 2 to 4 times the injury risk in the next week. The author himself recommends the index; his competing-interests statement says none.

I read only the abstracts of the critiques, so this is what the abstracts say. Impellizzeri and colleagues (2020) write that no study has properly estimated causal effects, so manipulating ACWR to change injury rates is conjecture, and that the metric is ambiguous and prone to statistical artefacts. In 2021 they suggest dismissing ACWR as a framework and model, partly because substituting randomly generated chronic loads gave similar results. Wang and colleagues (2020) discuss limits of the ratio itself and of how it has been analysed.

More direct than any critique is a randomised trial. [Dalen-Lorentsen and colleagues (2021)](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2020-103003) randomised 34 elite youth football teams; one group planned a whole season of training around ACWR principles. The proportion with health problems did not differ between groups (relative risk 1.01). The authors write that this specific load-management approach was not successful in preventing health problems.

The most recent [meta-analysis by Ding and colleagues (2026)](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpubh.2026.1896651) pooled observational studies in team sports: an elevated ACWR went with higher injury risk, a small-to-moderate association, with very large differences between studies. The authors conclude that current evidence does not support using it as a stand-alone causal or predictive model.

To sum up: the index is in use and there are associational data, but whether it can actually manage training is heavily criticised, and one randomised trial saw no benefit. Gabbett's core idea is that trouble often comes from sudden jumps rather than from training itself; whether that holds, these studies do not answer. If pain persists during training or gets worse week by week, do not rely on any ratio; ask a doctor or physical therapist.

## Does This Apply to Climbing?

Partly. Quarmby's review found that higher climbing intensity (grade) went with higher injury risk, with 19 of 21 studies showing a positive association. The link between climbing volume and injury was unclear, with conflicting studies, and previous injury strongly predicted future injury. These are only associations; they cannot show cause. The ACWR studies are in team sports and have no climbing data. For more on climbing injuries, see [Climbing Injury Overview](/posts/climbing/2026-10-18-climbing-injury-overview-en).

## Two Things You Can Do Tonight

1. **Sort three sentences.** "Sprints are faster after a warm-up", "warming up prevents injury", "don't increase this week's load too much over last week's". Which is performance, injury or load, and what design would it take to support each? You should arrive at: the first is performance, and needs an experiment comparing warm-up with no warm-up, timing sprints; the second is injury, and needs a long follow-up of many people, ideally randomised, comparing who gets hurt; the third is load, and needs a randomised trial comparing rule-based scheduling with training as usual. Then compare with this post: for the first I read one very mixed review; for the second, climbers showed no association while programmes in young players went with fewer injuries; for the third, one trial saw no benefit from that specific approach.
2. **Compute one ratio twice from the same data.** Suppose someone trains 120 minutes a week for four weeks, then 140 minutes in week five. Method one: divide 140 by the average of the previous four weeks, 120. You should get about 1.17. Method two: use the average of the most recent four weeks including week five, 120, 120, 120 and 140, which averages 125, and divide 140 by 125. You should get 1.12. The same data gives different numbers depending on the method; Ding and colleagues also note that studies differ in how they calculate the ratio and in the time windows. These numbers are hypothetical, neither means safe or dangerous, and this post gives no cut-offs.

## What This Post Doesn't Cover

I give no warm-up content or timing, and no advice on arranging your own training volume. For the three ACWR critiques and the randomised trial I read only the abstracts, and likewise for Soligard's trial. Within the range I searched, I found no randomised trial of warm-ups in climbers that I could read in full for free, and none on a prevention programme for the fingers. Warm-ups for older adults, children or people with chronic illness are outside these studies.
