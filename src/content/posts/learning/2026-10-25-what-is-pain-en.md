After a fall or a sprain it hurts, and it is natural to think: it hurts, so something is broken; it does not hurt, so it is fine. Are pain and injury the same thing? This post first looks at how the International Association for the Study of Pain (IASP) defines pain, then at a method called pain neuroscience education, and how certain the evidence is in research on chronic low back pain.

The conclusion first. IASP's definition describes pain as an experience, and its notes say pain and "nociception" (the nervous system detecting harmful stimuli and passing the signal along) are different phenomena. So by definition pain and injury are not the same thing, and how much it hurts cannot be read directly as how injured you are. I did not find a source that tells you how to judge from the level of pain whether something is serious; that is for a doctor to assess.

Pain not being a precise reading does not mean it can be ignored. The same notes say pain usually serves an "adaptive role"; my plain-words version is that it usually does the body some good, for example by warning you off using that spot. Pain that is severe, sudden, or after an injury, or that will not go away, should go to a doctor first, who can refer you to a physiotherapist if needed. For warning signs, see the "Warning signs for back pain" section of [Spine, Lower Back and Neck](/posts/climbing/2026-10-18-climbing-spine-back-en). That post is about the back and neck, and it uses the UK NHS's grading rather than a list I made up.

As for pain education, I read a 2025 meta-analysis (which pools the results of several trials) covering only adults with chronic low back pain. Compared with physiotherapy alone, groups that added pain education scored lower for pain, disability, fear of movement and catastrophizing (thinking of pain as worse and more frightening than it is). But the trials were few, bias was a risk, and I could not find GRADE (the certainty-of-evidence grading introduced in the [series overview](/posts/learning/2026-10-24-human-body-overview-en)) in the full text. The safer wording is: there are signs of a benefit, but not enough to say education cures pain.

## The Definition: An Experience, Not a Reading from the Wound

IASP revised its definition of pain in 2020. Its website currently reads:

> “An unpleasant sensory and emotional experience associated with, or resembling that associated with, actual or potential tissue damage.”

In plain words: an unpleasant sensory and emotional experience that is linked to actual or potential tissue damage, or that feels like what that damage would feel like.

After the definition, the website lists six notes. Four matter most for "does pain mean injury?":

- Note 1: pain is always a personal experience, influenced to varying degrees by biological, psychological and social factors.
- Note 2: pain and nociception are different phenomena (“Pain and nociception are different phenomena.”), and pain cannot be inferred solely from activity in sensory neurons.
- Note 4: a person's report of an experience as pain should be respected.
- Note 5: pain usually serves an adaptive role, but may have adverse effects on function and on psychological and social well-being.

The website defines nociception as “The neural process of encoding noxious stimuli.” and adds: “Pain sensation is not necessarily implied.” The nerves responding does not always mean a feeling of pain.

A commentary on children's pain (Stevens 2021) reads the new word “resembling” this way: in conditions such as neuropathic pain and nociplastic pain (pain from altered pain processing, with no clear tissue damage or nerve disease found), tissue damage may not be detectable clinically even though there is pain. That is the commentary author's reading, not a research result.

So pain is often linked to tissue damage, but neither the definition nor the notes equate the two. Finding no clear damage does not mean nothing is wrong; under note 4 that pain should still be respected, and whether to see a doctor is for a doctor to assess.

The definition is also challenged. A 2026 argument-style review (Cohen and colleagues) says it contains internal contradictions and proposes tying it to nociception, but it has no new data and its authors say the proposal may be controversial. The paper has a published correction that I did not read, and I did not read any IASP reply. So IASP's definition is the current official version, and some in the field still question it.

## Pain Neuroscience Education: A Meta-Analysis in Chronic Low Back Pain

Pain neuroscience education (PNE) is an educational method within the biopsychosocial model (which looks at body, mind and social factors together). It uses metaphors and analogies to help patients understand the mechanisms behind pain. What follows covers only adults with chronic low back pain.

This meta-analysis (Medina-Viedma and colleagues, 2025) included 15 randomized controlled trials with 810 adults who had chronic low back pain. In its introduction, "chronic" means symptoms for more than 12 weeks; each trial's duration is in its Table 2.

What was compared? The experimental groups mostly got PNE plus exercise or other physiotherapy; most comparison groups got the same physiotherapy, but in some trials the comparison group got a different kind of patient education. Two trials used PNE on its own. So it mainly answers: does adding PNE on top of physiotherapy make a difference?

Results are given as standardized mean differences. A negative value means the PNE group scored lower than the comparison group, and the more negative, the bigger the gap; −1 is roughly one standard deviation, not one point. For pain intensity the value was −0.65 right after the intervention (95% confidence interval −1.1 to −0.2, so the plausible range is wide) and about −1 at one and three months. Disability and fear of movement pointed the same way at all three time points; catastrophizing had data only right after and at one month, also pointing the same way. The review did not convert the scores into "how many points less pain", and I will not do that for it.

## How Certain Is This Evidence?

The review's conclusion calls PNE an effective therapeutic strategy (“PNE is an effective therapeutic strategy”). I use more cautious wording because of these three points.

- **Few trials, few people.** Some analyses had only three or five trials.
- **No blinding, and self-reported outcomes.** Therapists and participants knew whether education was given (one 2004 trial was the exception for participants); the full text says “A risk of performance bias was present in all RCTs due to the impossibility of blinding participants”. Pain, disability and fear of movement were all filled in on questionnaires, and such outcomes may be more open to expectation effects (that is my inference, not the authors' conclusion).
- **No long-term data.** There was no follow-up at 6 or 12 months.

The authors also list selection and detection bias and possible publication bias, and say in the conclusion that the results should be read with caution because the PNE and control interventions varied a lot across trials (“these findings should be interpreted with caution due to the variability observed in the PNE and control interventions across the included studies”).

On quality assessment, the review used only the PEDro scale to rate how well each trial was done, with a mean of 6.8, which the authors call good; that rates single trials and does not mean the body of evidence is trustworthy. It also did not measure anxiety; for the psychological side it measured only fear of movement and catastrophizing.

## Does This Apply to Climbing or Daily Activity?

The evidence covers only adults with chronic low back pain: no fresh injuries, and no climbers. For the severe pain after a sudden "pop" in a finger while climbing, see [Finger Pulley Injury](/posts/climbing/2026-10-18-finger-pulley-injury-en) and the [Climbing Injury Overview](/posts/climbing/2026-10-18-climbing-injury-overview-en), and get assessed by a doctor first. For daily activity, the takeaway is that how much it hurts cannot precisely reflect the damage, so do not judge only by the size of the pain; if pain gets in the way of sleep or daily life, take a note of where it hurts and which movements set it off, and show it to a doctor.

## Two Things You Can Do Tonight

1. **Read IASP's definition.** Open the [terminology page](https://www.iasp-pain.org/resources/terminology/), find the PAIN entry and click Expand beside it. You should read six notes, and note 2 starts with “Pain and nociception are different phenomena.” Then expand NOCICEPTION; you should read “Pain sensation is not necessarily implied.”
2. **Read how bias is described.** Open the [meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.3390/medsci13040290) and find section 3.3. You should read that performance bias was present in all the trials because therapists and participants could not be blinded (one 2004 trial was the exception for participants), and that the mean PEDro score was 6.8. Then try to write one sentence: why does participants knowing whether they were educated make self-reported pain scores harder to interpret? (My reference answer: their expectations may affect how they fill them in.)

Both actions are reading only and cannot replace an assessment. If you are in pain right now, or the pain has changed, see a doctor first.
