Near the end of one interview, Bernstein said: "Mackay wrote… a very similar book 180 years ago, and that didn't seem to help very much." He then predicted more financial manias and more religious manias in the coming decades and centuries: "I just don't see any way out of it."

A man who spent five hundred pages updating Mackay concludes that the update probably won't help either. This post asks what exactly he updated, and why he remains a pessimist.

## What the book is

*The Delusions of Crowds: Why People Go Mad in Groups*, Atlantic Monthly Press, February 2021. Bernstein trained as a neurologist before turning to investing and financial history. The title is an open homage to Mackay, and the content follows Mackay's two tracks: financial bubbles (South Sea, Mississippi, the 1840s British railway mania, the 1920s, the 1990s internet bubble) and religious end-times movements (Anabaptists, millenarianism, through to ISIS).

There is a Traditional Chinese edition from 樂金文化 (*群眾的幻覺*, translated by Liang Dongping, October 2021, 544 pages) and a Simplified Chinese edition from CITIC Press (*群体的疯狂*, translated by Wang Xinghua, November 2022).

**The source level needs saying up front.** The book has no open full text. This post reads two first-hand accounts by the author, the [New Bazaar podcast transcript (September 2021)](https://eig.org/newbazaar/bad-bubble-behavior) and his [own five-point summary for the Next Big Idea Club](https://nextbigideaclub.com/magazine/delusions-of-crowds-why-people-go-mad-in-groups-bookbite/26891/), plus two reviews that quote the book at length. Every "Bernstein says" below is at the level of the author describing his book, not chapter-by-chapter argument.

## How he updates Mackay

### The thesis in one sentence

The line from the book quoted at the start of the interview: "Manifestly, man is the ape that imitates, tells stories, seeks status, morally condemns others and yearns for the good old days, all of which guarantee a human future studded with religious and financial mass manias."

Why finance and religion in particular? His answer ranks stories by pull: "The most compelling story that was ever devised, of course, is the story of the end of the world, and a close second is the story that tells you that you're gonna become fabulously rich without any effort at all." Add status: knowing the world will burn while you alone are saved is the ultimate status good.

### Four social behaviors: the usable part

This is what readers can take away, and the summary and the interview agree on it. When a financial mania is under way, you see:

1. **The object of speculation becomes "a topic of everyday conversation."**
2. **"Otherwise sensible professionals quit reliable, good-paying jobs to speculate."**
3. **"Skepticism is often met with anger"**: not rebuttal but ridicule. He voiced doubts during the 1990s internet bubble, was called an idiot, and had his family background mocked.
4. **Outlandish forecasts appear**: not "another 10–30%" but double, triple, add a zero.

All four are **social behaviors**, not price indicators. Mackay's template is a moral story told afterward; Bernstein's checklist is a set of symptoms you can observe while it happens. That is the biggest difference between the two books.

He also stresses that bubbles are rare: before the crypto mania, the only true stock mania he had seen first-hand was the late-1990s one, and "you have to go back to the '20s to see a real stock mania." That is a built-in brake: not every rally is a bubble, and the four items are meant to be read together.

### Why people fall for it again: evolution and narrative

His underlying explanation is evolutionary mismatch: "We are the prisoners of our evolutionary history, doomed to operate in the Space Age with Stone Age minds." Imitation had adaptive value for hunter-gatherers (you cannot build a kayak or a blowgun without watching someone do it), so "Mother Nature rewarded credulity," and that credulity becomes a liability in the modern world.

Narrative is the channel that bypasses reason. His practical version is blunt: "If you want to analyze a subject, stick to the numbers and facts… But if you want to convince others of something, forget the facts and data, and tell them the catchiest story you can."

### When prophecy fails: the Festinger mechanism

On the religious track he uses the mechanism from Festinger's *When Prophecy Fails*: when a prophecy is disconfirmed, believers do not leave; they double down and start proselytizing. His contemporary example is QAnon, a disconfirmable hypothesis. After it was disconfirmed at noon on 20 January 2021, most people left and a minority doubled down: "those are the people who are really dangerous."

### Bubbles are not all bad

This is the most counterintuitive part: "In a way, bubble investors can be thought of as capitalism's unwitting philanthropists." His example is Global Crossing, once worth $40 billion, whose shareholders ended up with about a penny on the dollar when Asian investors bought it, but which left behind a global undersea fiber network. The 1840s British railway bubble and Insull's 1920s utility empire have the same shape: investors lose, infrastructure stays.

The LARB review relays another detail from the book. Mackay himself lived through the 1840s railway mania; as editor of the *Glasgow Argus* he covered railway schemes in a promotional register, and "almost completely missed the massive one he lived through." The historian of manias did not recognize the one in front of him.

### Amnesia: the pessimistic core

"The most powerful force in the universe is amnesia." He calls the line often credited to Einstein, that compound interest is the most powerful force, apocryphal, and offers his own version instead. Generational amnesia makes each generation pay the tuition again; of the 2008–09 wound, he said "I don't see much evidence that the people have retained that memory very well."

That is where the pessimism in the opening comes from: if knowing history doesn't stop it, what is writing history for? His only hope in the interview is that human neural wiring itself evolves away from these responses, "maybe brain implants, who knows."

### Platforms: quantitative, not qualitative, with one exception

On social media and trading apps his judgment has two levels, and the distinction matters for the fifth layer of this series:

- **Manias themselves**: "Quantitatively yes, but not qualitatively." From print to the internet, transmission keeps accelerating, but the South Sea bubble also rose and fell over months, and the internet bubble was slower; mania timelines look about the same as three hundred years ago.
- **The epistemic crisis**: this one he considers qualitatively new, "an algorithmically-driven epistemic crisis" in which algorithms and social media companies spread fake news until people no longer know what to believe.

His analysis of Robinhood sits here too: free shares are the casino's free chicken dinner, the most-popular list is social proof, the celebration animation is reinforcement, and the design language of "time on device" comes straight from casino machines (he cites anthropologist Natasha Dow Schüll's research).

## What later research and reviews corrected

**LARB (Zoellner, December 2021) names the book's central irony**: an author who keeps warning that people prefer stories to data rests his most important argument on narrative. That argument is that investors' dreams of riches and end-times believers' prophecies come from the same psychological place, and the review says "One wishes for a stronger bridge between the eschatological and the financial arguments." It also notes that the religion chapters pick history's worst cases in a manner resembling the New Atheists' fallacy of composition: round up history's extremists and elect them representatives of the whole.

**Quillette (Trutor, February 2021) is sharper**: grouping pro-Israel dispensationalist evangelicals with ISIS and David Koresh's Branch Davidians on one end-times continuum "casually dissolves useful distinctions between a broad range of theological viewpoints," which Trutor calls "demi-Vulcan cant." Quillette leans culturally conservative, so its pushback on criticism of religion is not purely a scholarly judgment; but the point that the religion chapters merge distinct traditions also appears in the LARB review, which is positive overall (see above), so it is not one outlet's view.

**The brain model he borrows has been abandoned by neuroscience.** LARB quotes the book: fast emotional responses sit in "our deeply placed and evolutionarily ancient limbic system, our so-called 'reptilian brain,'" while slow reasoning "arises from the evolutionarily newer cortex that overlies the limbic system." That is MacLean's triune brain model. [Cesario, Johnson and Eisthen (2020)](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/0963721420917687), in *Current Directions in Psychological Science*, say plainly that the idea of newer structures layered over older ones "has long been discredited among neurobiologists": all vertebrates have structures homologous to the cortex, and evolution transforms existing parts rather than adding floors. That does not make the fast-intuition / slow-reasoning distinction wrong (that is the subject of the order 6 Kahneman post), but the anatomical costume Bernstein gives it is out of date. His evolutionary foundation needs more caution than his behavioral checklist.

**Tracing one number**: in the interview he says false news is 70 percent more likely to be retweeted, with no source. The study that matches is [Vosoughi, Roy and Aral's 2018 *Science* paper](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.aap9559): about 126,000 news stories on Twitter from 2006 to 2017, classified by six fact-checking organizations; falsehood spread farther, faster, deeper and more broadly in every category. [MIT's press release](https://news.mit.edu/2018/study-twitter-false-news-travels-faster-true-stories-0308) states that "false news stories are 70 percent more likely to be retweeted than true stories are." The same paper has an often-omitted finding: bots accelerated true and false news at the same rate, so the difference comes from people. The conditions travel with the number: one platform, before 2017, news that had already been fact-checked.

## Where the book sits in the explanation chain

Mackay supplied the narrative template of "crowd madness"; Bernstein rewrote it as a checklist of behaviors observable while a mania runs, and added a question Mackay never asked: **why knowing the script doesn't help**. His answers, evolution and amnesia, both sit at the individual level. That leaves a gap: what actually happens to people once they are in a group? The next post, on Le Bon, covers the original text on that question, and the one most thoroughly dismantled since.

## One test to take away

**Read the four together; the third is the most telling**: everyday conversation, quitting jobs to speculate, skepticism met with anger, outlandish forecasts. The first two show up in any hot market. The third, where a reasonable doubt draws ridicule instead of argument, signals that a belief has become an identity.

**Something you can do tonight**: open the investing forum or group chat you read most, find a comment that is bearish on the hot asset, and count how many replies address its argument and how many mock the person who wrote it. The ratio is a reading of behavior number three.

## The Taiwan question

What does this mechanism look like in Taiwan in 2026? The four-behavior checklist needs no translation: investing forums, LINE groups and finance YouTube are all places where you can watch "everyday conversation" and "skepticism met with anger" happen directly. Bernstein's caution points the other way too: bubbles are rare, and the checklist is for detection, not for calling every hot market a bubble.

## Limits

- The full text was not read. Common claims that Bernstein says religious mania declines with secularization, a "four P" framework and Minsky's four conditions do not appear in the two author sources read here, so they are not included.
- On the review side, the NYRB (Chancellor, August 2021) and *The Times* (Aaronovitch, May 2021) are paywalled; only summaries were visible, and neither is quoted.
- His comments on January 6 and the evangelical right are explicit political positions; this post covers only the methodological problem in the religion chapters and does not take them further.
