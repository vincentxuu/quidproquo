One passage in Le Bon's book says that the impulsiveness, irritability, incapacity to reason and absence of judgment found in crowds are "almost always observed in beings belonging to inferior forms of evolution—in women, savages, and children." After quoting it, the social psychologist Stephen Reicher commented: "Psychology has often been accused of sexism, of ageism and of racism. It takes a truly great psychologist to achieve all three in a single sentence."

The same Reicher also calls it probably the most influential psychology book ever written. Both statements are true, and this post reads them together.

## What the book is

Gustave Le Bon's *Psychologie des foules* appeared in France in 1895; the next year T. Fisher Unwin in London published the English translation, *The Crowd: A Study of the Popular Mind*, which is still almost the only translation circulating in English ([Project Gutenberg #445](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/445)). It has three books: the mind of crowds, the opinions and beliefs of crowds, and a classification of crowds (criminal crowds, juries, electoral crowds, parliaments). There is no data, no footnotes and no method; it is pure argument.

The Chinese translations are messier than the content. In Traditional Chinese there are at least three editions: 臉譜 (translated by Zhou Ting), 華志 (translated by Wu Songlin, 212 pages, noticeably shorter than the full English text; whether it is abridged was not checked for this post) and 天蠍座 (translated by Gu Ding, 2023). The Simplified Chinese market has at least thirty editions. A [long passage-by-passage comparison of five Simplified translations](https://writingisleading.com/2021/11/27/乌合之众的译本比较/) shows that the earliest, Feng Keli's translation (claimed to have sold a million copies), was made from the English and renders "successive revolutions" as "successful revolutions," and that the best-seller-list leader has the most errors. Its author recommends the two translations made directly from the French, by Chen Jian (Yilin) and Lu Quanzhi (Shanghai Translation).

## How the original reads

### Three mechanisms (Book I, Chapter 1)

Le Bon names three "causes" that give people in crowds traits they do not show alone:

1. **Numbers bring invincibility, anonymity brings irresponsibility**: the individual "acquires, solely from numerical considerations, a sentiment of invincible power," and because a crowd is anonymous and therefore irresponsible, "the sentiment of responsibility which always controls individuals disappears entirely."
2. **Contagion**: "In a crowd every sentiment and act is contagious, and contagious to such a degree that an individual readily sacrifices his personal interest to the collective interest."
3. **Suggestibility**, which he calls "by far the most important," with contagion merely its effect. His analogy is hypnosis: someone immersed for a while in a crowd in action enters a state that "much resembles the state of fascination in which the hypnotised individual finds himself in the hands of the hypnotiser"; "The conscious personality has entirely vanished; will and discernment are lost."

The chapter concludes that by joining an organised crowd "a man descends several rungs in the ladder of civilisation," and that what crowds accumulate is "stupidity and not mother-wit."

### Three means and prestige (Book II)

How do beliefs spread in crowds? "The opinions and beliefs of crowds are specially propagated by contagion, but never by reasoning." The leader's means are "three in number and clearly defined—affirmation, repetition, and contagion." Affirmation works when "kept free of all reasoning and all proof"; used long enough, the three give an idea the mysterious force he calls prestige.

His advice in the chapter on electoral crowds is frank: to deal with a rival candidate, use affirmation, repetition and contagion to establish that he "is an arrant scoundrel, and that it is a matter of common knowledge that he has been guilty of several crimes." It reads like a smear manual, which is part of why politicians later read the book.

### His own qualifications (often dropped)

- **A methodological limit**: he says he is discussing only crowds in their fully organised phase, "we shall see what crowds may become, but not what they invariably are."
- **A normal-people model**: his example is the National Convention of the French Revolution, whose most savage members included men who would ordinarily have been "peaceable notaries or virtuous magistrates," and who returned to their normal character once the storm passed.
- **On that analogy**, he wrote: "I merely indicate this analogy in passing; its demonstration is outside the scope of this work." In other words, the sentence that later critics target most was offered without any evidence by its own author.

The common paraphrase that "Le Bon says crowds are necessarily irrational" attacks a generalized reading, not what he wrote. Another frequent conflation: "deindividuation = anonymity" is a later development by Festinger, Zimbardo and others; Le Bon's own wording is "the conscious personality vanishes" plus the hypnosis analogy.

## What later research corrected

### Thonhauser 2022: a three-level audit

Philosopher Gerhard Thonhauser's [open-access 2022 paper in *Continental Philosophy Review*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8919694/) is a recent systematic audit. He goes back to the French and German originals and works on three levels:

**Level one, the concept itself**: "the concept of the 'crowd,' as Le Bon defined it, does not correspond to an actual phenomenon." The choice of word is already a verdict: calling a gathering a *foule* presupposes that it is ignorant and potentially out of control, and the same word is not used for a theater audience or a group of law enforcement officials.

**Level two, the disease model of emotional contagion**: Le Bon's "contagion" misconceives how people emotionally affect one another. Contemporary emotion research offers two counterpoints: seeing someone angry tends to trigger fear and submission, not the same anger; and emotional mimicry depends on a prior implicit interpretation rather than direct copying of movements. His recommendation is to stop saying "contagion" and use less charged terms such as transfer, convergence or entrainment.

**Level three, ideological origins**: "Le Bon was an outspoken elitist and racist." *The Crowd* is the third book of a trilogy whose first two volumes were meant to provide "scientific" proof of racial hierarchy and elitism, aimed at three enemies: other "races," the lower classes and socialism. The decisive evidence is the first sentence. The French original opens with "Notre précédent ouvrage a été consacré à décrire l'âme des races" (our previous work was devoted to describing the soul of races), and **the English translation omits the entire sentence**, starting with the innocuous second one. For over a century English readers have read a crowd psychology with its racial frame removed. The original conclusion is equally blunt: "Civilisations as yet have only been created and directed by a small intellectual aristocracy, never by crowds. Crowds are only powerful for destruction."

Thonhauser adds a warning that reaches beyond Le Bon. In 1916 the German philosopher Scheler described the enthusiasm for the First World War as a shared feeling; in the 1923 second edition he recast it as "contagion." Thonhauser's reading: beliefs you reject get filed under contagion. **Describing opponents as infected is itself a mechanism of devaluation.** The same warning returns in the order 8 post on Hofstadter's paranoid style.

### Reicher: people don't lose their selves, they switch selves

The social identity school is the most direct replacement for Le Bon. In a [2011 paper in *Contemporary Social Science*](http://www.bbcprisonstudy.org/includes/site/files/files/CSS%20Crowds.pdf), Reicher uses a commuter example to set out the key distinction:

- **A physical crowd**: hundreds of people squeezed into one train carriage, avoiding eye contact, uncomfortable at a stranger's touch: many people, each psychologically separate.
- **A psychological crowd**: the train breaks down, they become "aggrieved commuters," start talking, and sometimes share sandwiches.

His argument is that members of psychological crowds "do not act irrationally. Rather they act meaningfully in terms of shared social identities." In one line: "Contra Le Bon, people do not lose identity and lose control in the crowd. Rather, they shift identity and shift the basis of control."

The evidence comes from history rather than the lab. E. P. Thompson's study of several hundred eighteenth-century English food riots found that they usually did not happen at the worst of the dearth but when stocks were rising again and grain was about to be moved out of the locality; crowds seized the grain, sold it at a popular price, and often handed the money and even the sacks back to the merchants. That is not atavistic violence; it is the enforcement of a "moral economy."

Reicher also points to a blind spot in lab work: many experiments simulated "anonymity in the crowd" by putting hoods on participants or keeping them in the dark, but a real crowd looks like a sea of faces only from the outside; from the inside, you are usually there with people you know. In an [interview](https://www.socialsciencespace.com/2016/02/stephen-reicher-on-crowd-psychology/) he puts the origin of crowd psychology bluntly: it arose in the unstable French Third Republic "as a defense of the status quo," a way of saying these people are mad and what they are doing is illegitimate.

### The field now: what language crowd research uses in 2026

[Drury's 2026 review in the *Annual Review of Psychology*](https://www.ovid.com/journals/anrp/fulltext/10.1146/annurev-psych-012125-121447~the-psychology-of-crowd-behavior) summarizes twenty years of research in the social identity approach: crowd density behavior, heightened emotion in crowds, mass-gathering health, behavior in emergencies, and how these models have shaped emergency response and public order policing.

Research on crowd disasters no longer uses Le Bon's vocabulary either. [Liang and colleagues (2024, *PLOS ONE*)](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0306764) reconstruct the 2022 Itaewon crush in Seoul: mobile device data show population far above normal in the hours before; a hydrodynamic simulation gives an average density during the crush of 7.57 people per square meter (maximum 9.95) and average crowd pressure peaking at 1,063 N/m (maximum 1,961 N/m). Their three causes are too many people, a bidirectional collision of flows and escalating panic; the remedy is flow separation and management. "Panic" here is a consequence of physical crowding, not a Le Bon-style regression of the collective mind.

### What the audit did not clear away

An audit like this is easy to read as "the classic was all wrong," but several things survive:

- **The phenomenon is real**: emotions do spread through crowds, and crowds do things their members would not do alone. Thonhauser rejects the disease model, not the fact that people affect one another emotionally.
- **Leaders matter**: the social identity school also recognizes informal leaders, with a different mechanism: a leader has to translate "who we are" into "what we should do," which Reicher calls being an "entrepreneur of identity," not a hypnotist.
- **The influence itself is historical fact**: Reicher says Le Bon was cited by Mussolini and Goebbels and that *Mein Kampf* has been called "a poor person's *The Crowd*." That is his statement in an interview; this post did not read a dedicated reception history, so it stands as a field expert's account. Freud's 1921 *Group Psychology and the Analysis of the Ego* devotes its second chapter to Le Bon.

## Where the book sits in the explanation chain

Mackay and Bernstein answer "what happened." Once Goldgar recasts tulipmania as "not a financial crisis but a social and cultural one," the question becomes: what goes on inside the group? Le Bon was the first to answer systematically, and his three mechanisms remain the default vocabulary when people talk about "crowd madness." Most of his answer has been overturned; his question remains. The next post, on Hoffer, takes another angle: not what happens inside crowds, but what kind of person becomes a true believer.

## One test to take away

When someone describes a group with "brainwashed," "mass hysteria" or "viral contagion," ask two questions: **Is this a physical crowd or a psychological one? Did the writer ask the people involved what they thought they were doing?** A description that never asked is usually Le Bon's language, not observation.

**Something you can do tonight**: open [Gutenberg #445](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/445/pg445-images.html), search for "women, savages," and read the next sentence. Le Bon says the analogy is offered only "in passing" and he will not demonstrate it. The book's most criticized sentence is, in the original, an aside without evidence.

## The Taiwan question

What does this mechanism look like in Taiwan in 2026? In Chinese, 烏合之眾 is already an insult, used far more often than the book is read. The next time a news story or post describes a protest, a crowd of netizens or a buying frenzy as "collective loss of control," read it with the two questions above, plus one from Thonhauser: would the writer use the same word for a group they agree with?

Two cautions on translations. First, Chinese editions made from the English also lose the French original's racial first sentence; only translations from the French include it. Second, the translation comparison happens to sample Le Bon's key qualification, and one translation skews "what they invariably are" (what crowds always are): the book's most important caveat can drift in translation too. For the three Traditional Chinese editions, no comparison of source text or quality was found for this post.

## Limits

- Of Le Bon's text, this post read the Introduction, all of Book I Chapter 1, the opening of Chapter 2, and the Book II passages on leaders and prestige; Book III, the classification, was not read.
- McPhail's 1991 *The Myth of the Madding Crowd* is the book-length precursor critique; this post refers to its position only through Thonhauser's and Reicher's citations, without reading the book.
- Only the abstract of Drury 2026 was read; the body is paywalled.
- The Mussolini and Goebbels line rests only on Reicher's account.
