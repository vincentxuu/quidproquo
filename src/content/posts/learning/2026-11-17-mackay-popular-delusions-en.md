A bulb of the tulip Semper Augustus, weighing 200 perits, was "thought to be very cheap at 5500 florins." In the same chapter, a sailor mistook a bulb worth three thousand florins for an onion and ate it with his red herring. Those two images are the founding material of the entire "crowd madness" genre: after Charles Mackay wrote them in 1841, tulipmania became the synonym for financial irrationality, and *The Economist* updated his book title directly in its 1987 crash editorial.

The question this post asks: where did those numbers and anecdotes actually come from?

## What the book is

*Memoirs of Extraordinary Popular Delusions and the Madness of Crowds* appeared in 1841 in three volumes; Volume I (National Delusions) holds Mississippi, South Sea and Tulipomania, with volumes II–III covering the Crusades, witch mania, alchemy, fortune telling and magnetisers. Mackay was a journalist, not a historian: the chapter has no footnotes. A Traditional Chinese translation exists from 大牌 (Li Youning, 2015 first edition / 2017 second / 2024-11 classic edition, claimed complete), plus many simplified editions.

## How the original reads: the template in four moves

Reading the [full text at Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/636), the method is fixed:

1. **Price numbers**: Admiral Liefken at 4400 florins, the viceroy at 3000, Semper Augustus at 5500; in 1635 "many persons were known to invest a fortune of 100,000 florins in the purchase of forty roots."
2. **All-classes frenzy**: "Nobles, citizens, farmers, mechanics, seamen, footmen, maidservants, even chimney-sweeps and old clotheswomen, dabbled in tulips."
3. **Institutions fail**: the courts refuse enforcement — "debts contracted in gambling were no debts in law" — and the government's arbitration plan comes to nothing.
4. **Moral ending**: Cowley and Burns bookend the chapter; the kicker is that if chickweed and groundsel ever came into fashion, the rich would pay just as foolishly. Madness is humanity's permanent stupidity.

Only two sources are ever named: Beckmann's *A History of Inventions* and Munting's thousand-page folio. Everything else arrives as "It is related that…".

## What later research corrected

**Garber 1989 (JPE) reconstructed the chain.** Mackay's account "plagiarized most of his description from Beckmann with a little literary embellishment"; Beckmann relied on Munting and on the 1637 anonymous dialogue *Samen-spraeck tusschen Waermondt ende Gaergoedt* — a pamphlet written as moral attack on speculation. The footnotes contain the kill shot: "Mackay must have recorded Beckmann's reference to Munting without examining the Munting text." Mackay never read Munting; the pamphlet's price list entered history as fact.

Garber's specific charges against the narrative: Mackay "provided neither the sources of these bulb prices nor the dates on which they were observed"; he cited prices from sales 60, 130 and 200 years after the collapse as evidence of its magnitude; and he offered no evidence of the general economic context.

**But Garber does not argue the mania was rational.** High but rapidly depreciating prices for rare bulbs are normal in the flower-bulb industry; "Most of the 'tulipmania' was not obvious madness." Only the last month, when common bulb prices ran and crashed, "remains as a potential bubble" — and he stresses observational equivalence: the data cannot distinguish "the market was mad" from "the researcher's model is misspecified," so taking a side requires strong priors.

**Goldgar 2007 redid the history from archives.** Her chain adds one more ring (the chronicler van Aitzema, 1669, himself built on the pamphlets), concluding we are left with "a picture of tulipmania based almost solely on propaganda, cited as if it were fact." Her positive claim is not that nothing happened: "Not everyone was involved in the trade, and those who were were connected to each other in specific ways," and "It might not have been a financial crisis, but it was a social and cultural one."

**2017 and 2024 economic history adds two more mechanisms**: McClure & Thomas (Financial History Review) explain the price shape with sequestered-capital timing; Doten-Snitker et al. (Theory and Society 2024) replace "crowd madness" with ideational diffusion for the witch hunts. The frame itself being swapped by different schools is exactly what layer L1 is for.

## Where the book sits in the chain

Mackay opens layer L1 (historical cases), but his real contribution is not fact, it is **template**: price numbers + all-classes frenzy + institutional failure + moral ending. Bernstein 2021 takes the book as its direct inspiration ("I had read the script"), and 180 years later the same template still runs — the observation that knowing the script does not stop you is the engine of this whole series.

## One criterion to keep

For any "historical madness" story, ask three things: **do the prices carry dates and sources? Is the source propaganda or archive? Is there economic data for the crash?** Mackay's tulip chapter fails all three. Stories that fail them are fine as templates, not as evidence.

## The Taiwan hook

The book sits in two edition systems here: 大牌's Traditional Chinese (Li Youning, 2024-11 classic edition) and two dozen-plus simplified editions; [the Douban works page](https://book.douban.com/works/1150290) lists 29. Translation quality was not checked book by book; quote the Gutenberg English when it matters. Next time a "modern tulipmania" headline appears (crypto and AI-concept stocks both qualify), run the three questions before treating it as history.

## Limits

- Goldgar's archival numbers from later chapters (no bankruptcies, longest buyer chain of five, only 37 people paying over 300 guilders) are from the book's body, which this post did not read; the publisher's introduction excerpt was the source, so the numbers are marked 🟡 unverified and are not cited above.
- The 1841 three-volume and 1852 two-volume illustrated editions differ; Garber's "seven pages" remark does not state which edition he means.
- The witch-mania chapter is one line here and is left to the Hofstadter post in layer L4.
