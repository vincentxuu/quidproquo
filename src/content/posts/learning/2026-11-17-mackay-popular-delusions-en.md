Whenever a market soars and crashes, someone brings up tulipmania: seventeenth-century Dutchmen ruined themselves over a single bulb, even chimney sweeps piled in, and then overnight it all collapsed and took the national economy with it. After the global stock crash of October 1987, *The Economist* wrote that the event had supplied a new chapter for Charles Mackay's 1841 book.

This post reads that book, *Extraordinary Popular Delusions and the Madness of Crowds*, which is cited in almost every discussion of why crowds lose their reason. You will come away with three things: what Mackay actually argues, whether his most famous evidence survives scrutiny, and a set of tools for explaining a "mania" that the rest of this series will keep using.

## What Mackay set out to prove

Mackay was a Scottish journalist, not a historian. The first edition of 1841 ran to three volumes, and the opening of the first volume states the book's thesis:

> Men, it has been well said, think in herds; it will be seen that they go mad in herds, while they only recover their senses slowly, and one by one.

His evidence is a long list of historical cases: the Crusades, witch hunts, alchemy, fashions for poisoning, duelling, fortune-telling, and three money manias: the Mississippi scheme in France, the South Sea Bubble in England, and, earlier, the Dutch tulips. In his telling they are all the same thing: an idea sweeps through a whole society like a contagion, top to bottom, and people come to their senses only afterward, one at a time.

This post reads only the tulip chapter, for two reasons. Every later article asking "is this the new tulipmania?" tells the story the way that chapter does. And the chapter can be checked against two solid pieces of archival research, which lets us test Mackay's thesis directly: did the Dutch really go mad in a herd?

## What actually happened in the winter of 1636–37

This section rests mainly on two studies: economist Peter Garber's 1989 paper in the *Journal of Political Economy*, which reconstructs the bulb prices, and historian Anne Goldgar's 2007 book *Tulipmania*, which went back to the contemporary archives. All money here is in Dutch guilders; Mackay calls the same coin the florin.

**The commodity.** Tulips reached western Europe from the Ottoman Empire, took root at the Leiden botanical garden in the 1590s, and quickly became collectors' items among wealthy Dutch merchants. The most prized were "broken" tulips, with flame-like stripes on the petals. Nobody could predict when a bulb would break; much later the cause turned out to be a virus, which also made the bulbs weaker and slower to multiply. So rare varieties were scarce and hard to reproduce. Semper Augustus and Viceroy, which come up below, are names of such varieties.

**People traded contracts.** Bulbs are lifted in June and must be replanted by September, so between September and the following June any purchase can only be a contract for delivery next summer. By the summer of 1636, trading had grown enough that buyers and sellers began meeting in taverns in Haarlem, Amsterdam, and other towns, in groups called "colleges" with fixed bidding rules; on each deal the buyer paid the seller a small "wine money" fee.

The key point is that the buyer usually had no cash and the seller usually had no bulb, and neither expected delivery. They were betting on the price: at settlement, if the market price was above the contract price, the seller paid the buyer the difference; if below, the buyer paid the seller. A modern futures exchange takes a deposit up front (margin) and settles gains and losses daily, so a trader whose deposit runs out is closed out before the losses grow. The tavern trade of 1636 had neither safeguard, so one person could sign contracts far beyond what they owned.

**How prices moved.** Rare bulbs had been expensive since 1634, and Garber does not count that as madness: a new variety starts at a very high price and depreciates fast as it multiplies, a pattern visible in the bulb markets of the eighteenth and twentieth centuries too. What does not fit is the common bulbs. They entered tavern trading only in November 1636, rose up to twentyfold in January 1637, and peaked around February 5; then buyers stopped honoring contracts and trading froze. The records do not say why the crash came in early February.

**The cleanup.**

| Date | What happened |
|---|---|
| 24 February 1637 | Florists' delegates meeting in Amsterdam propose that contracts made on or before 30 November 1636 be honored, and that later buyers may withdraw by paying 10% of the contract price; the authorities do not adopt it |
| 27 April 1637 | The States of Holland (the largest of the Republic's seven provinces) put all contracts on hold. Growers may sell the bulbs lifted in June at market prices, with the difference to be settled however the authorities later decide |
| May 1638 | Haarlem's city council lets buyers cancel by paying 3.5% of the contract price; according to the Dutch economic historian Posthumus, many cities followed |

**How bad was it?** In the end the courts did not enforce a single tulip contract. Most contracts were bets on price differences, with no bulbs delivered and no money paid, so little wealth actually changed hands. Goldgar, after going through the archives, says she "couldn't find anybody that went bankrupt," and that far fewer people took part than the legend claims, concentrated in a few circles who knew one another. Garber notes that economic histories of the period barely mention tulips; they describe the Netherlands of those years as a run of commercial and financial triumphs.

## Where Mackay went wrong

Mackay's tulip chapter ([full text on Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/636/636-h/636-h.htm)) has four fixed elements, and later bubble stories mostly follow them in the same order:

1. **Fabulous prices**: a Semper Augustus "thought to be very cheap at 5500 florins"; a single Viceroy traded for wheat, rye, four fat oxen, eight fat swine, twelve fat sheep, wine, beer, butter, a thousand pounds of cheese, a bed, a suit of clothes, and a silver drinking cup.
2. **A whole nation gone mad**: "Nobles, citizens, farmers, mechanics, seamen, footmen, maidservants, even chimney-sweeps and old clotheswomen, dabbled in tulips." People sold houses and land cheap to buy bulbs.
3. **Institutions that cannot help**: the courts refused to hear the cases because "debts contracted in gambling were no debts in law."
4. **A moral ending**: substantial merchants reduced almost to beggary, noble houses ruined, and Dutch commerce suffering a shock it took many years to recover from.

Plus two anecdotes: a sailor who ate a bulb worth three thousand florins, taking it for an onion to go with his herring, and spent months in prison; and an English traveler who peeled and halved a bulb out of curiosity and was hauled before the magistrate.

Against the previous section:

- **The dates are wrong.** Mackay puts the national frenzy in 1634–35. In the records, the explosive rise in common bulbs lasted only through January 1637.
- **The scale is inflated.** The archives show no nationwide participation, no wave of bankruptcies, no economic collapse.
- **Even the meeting record is reversed.** Mackay says the deputies declared contracts made before November 1636 "null and void"; the proposal Garber cites says they were to be **honored**.
- **The crash is measured with the wrong era's prices.** Mackay's prices carry no dates or sources, and he uses bulb prices from 60 to 200 years after the collapse to show how far they fell. But new varieties get cheap once they spread: a 1739 Haarlem catalog that Garber found lists Semper Augustus at 0.1 guilders.
- **The anecdotes are credited to the wrong source.** Mackay cites Blainville's travels for both; Garber read Blainville, and it is the diary of a 1705 tour, 70 years after the episode, with one sentence about tulips.

Where did these errors come from? Garber and Goldgar each traced Mackay's sources backward and found the same chain:

1. **1637**: Adriaen Roman's dialogue pamphlet *Samen-spraeck tusschen Waermondt ende Gaergoedt* (a dialogue between "True-mouth" and "Greedy-goods"), published after the crash to attack speculation on moral grounds. The price lists and the Viceroy barter list trace back here.
2. **1669**: the chronicle of Lieuwe van Aitzema, itself based on pamphlets of this kind.
3. **1670s**: Abraham Munting's botanical folio, all of whose price data can be found in the dialogues. Mackay says Munting "wrote a folio volume of one thousand pages upon the tulipomania"; Garber checked, found only six pages on the episode, and concluded in a footnote that Mackay "must have recorded Beckmann's reference to Munting without examining the Munting text."
4. **Late eighteenth century**: the German scholar Johann Beckmann's *History of Inventions*, which cites the dialogues and Munting and at least names its sources.
5. **1841**: Mackay, who "plagiarized most of his description from Beckmann with a little literary embellishment" (Garber).

Goldgar's summary: "a picture of tulipmania based almost solely on propaganda, cited as if it were fact."

## So did anyone go mad?

"Nothing happened" is wrong too. Common bulbs did rise twentyfold in January 1637 and then crash. The question is how to explain it, and there are at least three accounts, pointing to very different conclusions:

**1. Individual irrationality: everyone went mad.** This is Mackay's account: greed spread like a contagion and everyone lost their judgment. It is the easiest to tell and the most vivid, but also the hardest to prove. Garber is blunt: price data alone cannot tell "the market went mad" apart from "the theory the researcher uses to judge a sensible price is wrong."

**2. Rules that let rational people take wild risks: the market design was broken.** Garber describes that month as a collection of people without net worth making ever-larger bets with each other, with some knowledge that the state would not enforce the contracts. With no margin, no daily settlement, and little chance of having to pay if you lost, signing an outrageous contract was not necessarily madness; it might be a calculation that nothing would come of it. This account moves the focus from people to rules: what needs guarding against is not human nature but a system that lets losers walk away.

**3. Moral panic: a society's anxiety about the new.** Goldgar's answer: the economic effects were small, but it was a social and cultural crisis. Commerce ran on trust; a crowd of people who had promised face to face to buy now said "I don't want it and I won't pay," and the courts would not step in. New money, new men, exotic luxuries, and broken promises: that is what unsettled the Dutch. Notably, people at the time already described it as a disease. On 5 February 1637, the Haarlem cleric Jodocus Cats wrote in a letter that "another sickness has arisen… the sickness of the blommisten or floristen"; Goldgar reads it as, for Cats, "a sickness in the head." Describing a group's behavior as contagion was not Mackay's invention; it was already there in 1637.

The three accounts are not mutually exclusive, but the order matters: ask first whether the rules and the social context explain it, and reach for "everyone went mad" last, because "everyone went mad" is the account that costs the least effort and needs the least evidence.

## The real herd was the next two centuries of readers

Back to Mackay's thesis: men think in herds and go mad in herds. The tulip chapter does not show that the Dutch of 1637 went mad in a herd, but it proves the thesis somewhere else: in how the story itself spread.

A moralizing pamphlet was copied by a chronicler, a botanist, a German scholar, and a British journalist, and in the twentieth century it entered investment books and the news. Garber notes that Burton Malkiel's *A Random Walk Down Wall Street* has a whole chapter titled "The Madness of Crowds" that cites Mackay extensively; American newspapers reached for tulips when gold soared in 1979 and after the 1987 crash; the dot-com bubble and bitcoin drew the same comparison. For two centuries almost no one went back to Munting, read Blainville, or checked the dates on the prices. Each person believed it because the people before them believed it.

The story lasted for three reasons:

- **It was a sermon, not a report.** Dutch Calvinist moralists of the time feared that new wealth would corrupt society, and the fable that greed gets punished has lasted to this day.
- **It is vivid, memorable, and fits anything.** Fabulous prices, a nation gone mad, courts that will not help, a moral at the end: none of it requires economic data.
- **It has no sources, so it cannot be checked.** Readers can only believe it or not, and everyone believed it. What institutions a society relies on to check what is true is the question taken up later in the series by [Rauch's The Constitution of Knowledge](/posts/learning/2026-11-18-rauch-constitution-of-knowledge-en).

So this post's answer to "why do crowds lose their reason?" is this: the thing to watch for is not necessarily a crowd going mad in a market, but a vivid story that flatters our moral intuitions being accepted by the crowd without anyone checking. Mackay meant to write about the first; his own book became an example of the second.

## What to take away

**A set of explanatory tools.** The next time you hear that something was "crowd madness," ask in order:

1. **Did the rules let people avoid the consequences?** (market design)
2. **What social anxiety did it touch?** (moral panic)
3. **Once those are accounted for, do you still need "everyone went mad"?** (individual irrationality)

**Three source questions.** For any story of "historical madness" or headline calling something "the new tulipmania": do the prices have dates and sources? Is the source a contemporary record, or propaganda and retelling? Is there economic data on the crash, such as bankruptcies, prices, or employment? Mackay's tulip chapter fails all three.

**Something to do tonight**: open [the tulip chapter on Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/636/636-h/636-h.htm) and use Ctrl-F to find "It is related" and "Blainville". Count how many prices and anecdotes have no source.

**Next.** Does knowing the story was made up keep you out of the next bubble? The financial theorist William Bernstein read Mackay in the early 1990s, watched the dot-com bubble arrive, and said "I had read the script and I knew how the movie ended," yet he also concedes that 180 years of Mackay in print have not stopped people repeating the same mistakes: see [Bernstein's The Delusions of Crowds](/posts/learning/2026-11-17-bernstein-delusions-of-crowds-en). For how the image of a crowd "catching" an idea like a disease became a theory of crowd psychology, see [Le Bon's The Crowd](/posts/learning/2026-11-17-lebon-the-crowd-en).

## If you want to read the book

- The English original is free on [Project Gutenberg #636](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/636); the tulip chapter is in volume one. Read it as a primary source on how nineteenth-century Britons saw speculation and crowds, not as a historical record. The book rarely gives sources, so check any number or anecdote before you cite it.
- For a ten-minute summary of Goldgar's findings, read *Smithsonian*'s "[There Never Was a Real Tulip Fever](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/history/there-never-was-real-tulip-fever-180964915/)." Garber's paper is openly available for anyone who wants the price data (see references).
