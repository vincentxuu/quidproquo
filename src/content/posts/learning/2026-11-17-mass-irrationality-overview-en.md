In 2021 the financial theorist William Bernstein published *The Delusions of Crowds*. In an interview he said that when he read Charles Mackay's 1841 *Extraordinary Popular Delusions and the Madness of Crowds* in the early 1990s, it seemed to have nothing to do with the fairly well-behaved capital markets of the time; within a few years the dot-com bubble arrived: "I had read the script and I knew how the movie ended." At the end of the same interview he added that Mackay had written a very similar book 180 years earlier and it did not seem to help much, and that more financial and religious manias are coming: "I just don't see any way out of it."

That is the question this series asks: **why do crowds lose their judgment together, and why does knowing the history not stop the next time?** The series reads eleven books, one per post; each post first lays out what the book claims, then compares it with what later research revised. The books are arranged along five questions, moving inward and then back out: first what happened in history, then what people become in a crowd and how the individual mind works, then culture and institutions, and finally today's information environment. Each section answers the question the previous one leaves open.

This overview walks through those five questions with real cases from finance, crowd disasters, health, politics, and the internet, showing how the classics answered and how later research rewrote the answer. By the end you will have a checklist: the next time you hear "those people have gone mad," four questions to ask before you reach for "mad."

## 1. What actually happened in history?

Books: Mackay, *Extraordinary Popular Delusions and the Madness of Crowds* (1841); Bernstein, *The Delusions of Crowds* (2021)

Mackay's thesis: "Men… think in herds; it will be seen that they go mad in herds, while they only recover their senses slowly, and one by one." His evidence is a long list of historical cases: the Crusades, witch hunts, alchemy, and three money manias: the Mississippi scheme in France, the South Sea Bubble in England, and the Dutch tulips of 1637. Bernstein brought the subject up to date 180 years later with the dot-com bubble and religious end-times movements, and set out four behavioral signs of a mania: everyone talks about it, people quit their jobs to speculate, skeptics get shouted down, and predictions grow ever more extreme.

Later research asked a more basic question first: did these things actually happen the way the stories say? Most tulip trading consisted of contracts signed in taverns for delivery next summer, bets on the price difference; after the crash the courts enforced no contracts, so little money actually changed hands, and the archives show no wave of bankruptcies. Rare varieties had always been expensive; what soared and crashed were the ordinarily cheap common varieties, and only in January 1637. The familiar version of a nation gone mad and an economy ruined comes from two centuries of copying a moralizing pamphlet. What scholars, journalists, and investment books did believe as a herd for two centuries, without anyone checking, was the story itself.

**The question this leaves**: if many "manias" were exaggerated, what actually happens to people in a crowd?

## 2. What do people become in a crowd?

Books: Le Bon, *The Crowd* (1895); Hoffer, *The True Believer* (1951); Canetti, *Crowds and Power* (1960)

The classic answers all describe the crowd as an illness. Le Bon says people in a crowd become anonymous and unaccountable, emotion spreads like an infection, and a leader's words are taken in like hypnotic suggestion. Hoffer says mass movements attract people who want to escape themselves; the frustrated, the new poor, and the bored are the likeliest true believers. Canetti says people normally fear being touched by strangers, and only in a dense crowd does that fear flip into release.

Later research revises this in two directions. One is "losing the self": the social identity school argues that in a crowd people act on a shared "we" and its norms, not that their reason disappears; and the sense of release in a packed crowd that Canetti describes turns out to depend on a shared identity, not on numbers alone. The other is "who joins": social movement research finds that most people are brought in by acquaintances already inside, not isolated misfits who seek movements out.

Two recent cases make the contrast concrete:

- **The 2022 Halloween crowd crush in Itaewon, Seoul.** Researchers reconstructed the scene with mobile phone location data and a fluid-dynamics model: hours before the disaster, the number of people in the area was already far above normal, two streams of people collided head-on, and the average density reached 7.57 people per square meter. They concluded that the main causes were the sheer number of people and the head-on collision of two flows, with panic rising as the crush built. Disasters like this are now treated as problems of crowd flow and management, not of a crowd suddenly going mad.
- **Tourette-like symptoms spreading on social media, Germany, 2021.** Doctors in Hannover reported a group of teenagers with Tourette-like movements and vocalizations that were not actually Tourette syndrome; the common source was a hugely popular YouTube creator, the symptoms spread through social media, and similar cases appeared in the UK, the US, and elsewhere. Behavior really can be "contagious," but it travels along lines of identification with someone young people follow, not through an arbitrary crowd of strangers.

**The question this leaves**: even if crowds matter less than claimed, does the individual mind have weak spots that make it easy to lead astray?

## 3. Where is the individual mind easy to lead astray?

Book: Kahneman, *Thinking, Fast and Slow* (2011)

Kahneman divides thinking into fast intuition and slow reasoning and catalogs a long list of biases: anchoring (the first number you see pulls later judgments toward it), loss aversion (a loss hurts more than an equal gain pleases), and "priming," the idea that words you glimpse without noticing change your behavior. If these hold, group mistakes can be explained at the level of the individual.

Later research says the biases have to be checked one at a time. Anchoring and loss aversion hold up when experiments are rerun; social priming (reading words about old age makes you walk more slowly, for example) and ego depletion (using willpower leaves you with less self-control afterward) mostly failed to reproduce when many labs redid the experiments. Kahneman himself admitted in 2017 that he had placed "too much faith in underpowered studies."

The biases that do hold up can add up to a whole market. Using every trade on the Taiwan Stock Exchange from 1995 to 1999 (more than a billion trades), researchers found that Taiwanese investors were about twice as likely to sell a stock at a gain as one at a loss, a widespread reluctance to take losses; individual investors as a group earned 3.8 percentage points a year less because of their trading, a total loss equal to 2.2% of Taiwan's GDP. This is not a quirk of a few small investors but a collective tendency of a market (details in [the Kahneman post](/posts/learning/2026-11-18-kahneman-thinking-fast-slow-en)).

**The question this leaves**: everyone has biases, so why do some societies, at some times, go wrong more easily?

## 4. How do culture and institutions amplify it, or contain it?

Books: Hofstadter, *Anti-Intellectualism in American Life* (1963); Hofstadter, "The Paranoid Style in American Politics" (1964)

Hofstadter traces American anti-intellectualism through religion, politics, business, and education, and his most counterintuitive argument is that intellectuals are resented not because they are useless but because they are so badly needed. The more a society depends on experts, the more power experts hold over other people's lives, and so intellect is resented as a form of power and privilege. His essay on the paranoid style describes a conspiratorial way of talking about politics: not a lack of evidence but a pile of it, used to protect a belief rather than to correct it.

Later research revises him in two places. Historians criticize him for pathologizing populist movements and political opponents. Empirical work finds that conspiracy belief has not been rising: Uscinski, Enders, and colleagues re-asked 37 conspiracy questions first asked between 1966 and 2020 and found only 6 rose significantly while 15 fell; the link between conspiracy thinking and left or right varies by country and period, and the more stable clue is being shut out of power. Much of what gets called anti-intellectualism is a reasoned distrust of particular experts.

Taiwan shows how trust splits along identity. In Academia Sinica's "Portrait of America" survey, the share of Taiwanese agreeing that "the US is a country that keeps its word" fell from about 45% in 2021 to 33.4% in 2025; about six in ten Democratic Progressive Party supporters agree, fewer than one in ten Kuomintang supporters. Same country, same facts, and the trust gap follows political camp (details in [the paranoid style post](/posts/learning/2026-11-18-hofstadter-paranoid-style-en)).

Anti-intellectualism is not only American. Inspired by Hofstadter, the historian Yü Ying-shih wrote "Anti-Intellectualism and the Chinese Political Tradition" in 1975, opening with the claim that "a layer of anti-intellectual atmosphere has always pervaded China's political tradition," but arguing that it had least to do with Confucianism and came from ruling power and political thought; the same word has different sources in different societies.

**The question this leaves**: in the age of the internet and platforms, what is different about these mechanisms?

## 5. What has today's information environment changed?

Books: McIntyre, *Post-Truth* (2018); Rauch, *The Constitution of Knowledge* (2021); Nichols, *The Death of Expertise* (2017). They are ordered by question rather than publication date: where post-truth came from, how a society tells true from false, and why people stopped listening to experts.

McIntyre says post-truth is not denying facts but making them serve a position, and its playbook was written by the tobacco industry, whose internal memo said "Doubt is our product." Rauch says a society decides what is true through institutions such as journals, newsrooms, courts, and statistical agencies, plus two rules: no one has the final say, and no one counts by personal authority. Nichols says what died is not expertise itself but the consensus that expert opinion should change our minds.

Later research and events make this section concrete:

- **Correction works, if it comes fast enough.** The widely cited "backfire effect," in which corrections make people believe a falsehood more, has barely replicated. A 2025 PNAS study found that once a Community Note is attached to a post on X, reposts fall by 46.1%; the researchers also note that notes usually arrive too late, and would be far more effective if attached sooner.
- **The institutions of fact-checking are changing.** In January 2025 Meta announced it would end third-party fact-checking in the US and switch to X-style community notes. This is exactly the kind of institutional shift Rauch worries about: the work of judging true and false moves from professional checkers to crowds of users, and its effect depends on whether notes arrive in time.
- **Whom people trust is changing.** According to the Reuters Institute's 2025 survey, overall trust in news in Taiwan is 30%, down 3 points from the year before; meanwhile 46% use YouTube for news and 42% use LINE. People have not stopped taking in news; their attention has moved away from newsrooms.
- **How much harm it does is still disputed.** In 2024 *Nature* published two opposing pieces on the same day, one arguing that most people see little misinformation and exposure is concentrated in a small minority, the other that this underestimates the threat.

**How this ties the sections together**: today's problem is not just that there is more misinformation, but that the institutions and trust a society uses to tell true from false are being reshuffled, and trust in turn splits along identity, which leads back to sections 2 and 4.

## Putting the five together: the series' answer

Taken together, the classic answer is that people in a crowd catch something like a disease; later research keeps rewriting it as: **when a group seems to lose its reason, it is usually not that everyone has gone mad, but that people are making choices that make sense to them within particular identities, relationships, rules, and information environments.** "They've gone mad" is the explanation that costs the least effort and needs the least evidence, so it should come last.

That also answers Bernstein's question. His answer is amnesia: every generation has to live through it again. Reading the series suggests another answer: the script we read is itself wrong. If you think bubbles are caused by other people going mad, you will look only for other people's madness, not at which rules let someone avoid paying, which "we" you belong to, or which sources you trust.

So the next time you hear "those people have gone mad," ask in order:

1. **Did it actually happen?** Scale, dates, sources, economic data.
2. **Did the rules let people avoid the consequences?** Contracts without margin, platforms nobody checks, lies nobody is held to account for.
3. **What identity were they acting under, and who brought them in?** Not "lost themselves" but "switched to which self."
4. **Whom did they trust or distrust, and for what reasons?**

If, after those four, you still need "everyone went mad," that is the part left for individual irrationality, and even then, check whether the bias in question has replicated.

## Three ways to read

- **In order**: follow the five sections above; each answers the question the previous one leaves.
- **Financial bubbles only**: Mackay → Bernstein → Kahneman.
- **Anti-intellectualism, conspiracy thinking, and misinformation only**: both Hofstadter posts → McIntyre → Rauch → Nichols.

Every post stands on its own; you do not need to read the previous one first.

## What this reading list leaves out

- **It leans on English-language classics.** Only two of the eleven books were not written in English (Le Bon in French, Canetti in German); authors and cases are mostly European and American, and six of the books date from 1964 or earlier. This overview adds Itaewon, Yü Ying-shih, and Taiwan, but the individual posts stay close to their books. Modern medical cases of mass psychogenic illness are concentrated in South Asia, Southeast Asia, and Africa, and the series does not cover them.
- **Chinese translations vary widely.** Some books have many translations of uneven quality; some are out of print or untranslated. Each post notes the translation situation and links to the original where that is the better choice.
