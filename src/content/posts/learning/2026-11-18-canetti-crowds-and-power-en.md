On 15 July 1927, workers in Vienna took to the streets over a verdict: the men who had shot workers in Burgenland had been acquitted. The crowd had no leader. It marched to the Palace of Justice and set it on fire. The police opened fire, and more than eighty people died. Elias Canetti, a twenty-one-year-old chemistry student at the University of Vienna, was in the crowd. More than fifty years later he wrote in his autobiography: "I became a part of the crowd, I fully dissolved in it, I did not feel the slightest resistance to what the crowd was doing."

He started thinking about the subject in the 1920s, worked on it on and off for thirty-five years, and published *Masse und Macht* (*Crowds and Power* in English) in 1960. Two years later, in a radio conversation, Adorno told him to his face that what struck him most about the book was "something of a scandal": the subjectivity of its approach. In 1981 Canetti won the Nobel Prize in Literature "for writings marked by a broad outlook, a wealth of ideas and artistic power".

A philosopher called its method a scandal; a literature prize praised its artistic power. This post reads the book's two core parts: how crowds form and what their attributes are, and the chapter "The Command," which argues that every order leaves a sting in the person who carries it out; then it looks at which parts later research has tested and which remain images. You will come away with a way to tell an image from a claim, and with Canetti's answer to why people seek out crowds, an answer very different from the other classics.

## The book and its author

*Masse und Macht* was published in Hamburg by Claassen in 1960; Carol Stewart's English translation came out from Gollancz in 1962. The book has eleven parts and an epilogue, about 470 pages of text in English. The first four parts deal with the crowd, the pack (a small group gathered for a purpose such as hunting or war), the pack and religion, and the crowd in history; the remaining seven deal with aspects of power: seizing and incorporation, the survivor, the elements of power, the command, transformation, aspects of power, and rulers and paranoiacs (ending with the memoirs in which the German judge Daniel Paul Schreber recorded his own psychotic illness). The material comes almost entirely from anthropology, myth, the history of religion and history. There are no statistics or surveys.

Canetti was a Sephardic Jew born in Bulgaria who wrote in German, left Vienna in 1938 and settled in Britain. He considered this book his life's work.

Two things to know before reading. First, the bibliography of two hundred-odd entries does not include Le Bon (the French writer whose 1895 *The Crowd* described crowds as if hypnotized), and Freud appears only in a note on the Schreber case, where Canetti explains that he "made no use of his study". He knew the tradition: in the 1962 conversation with Adorno he criticized Freud for explaining the crowd according to Le Bon's description, called the concept of identification "too imprecise", and said "I try to do without identification." He chose to write outside that tradition. Second, the book is hard to get in Chinese. The only traditional-character edition is the 1982 Boxue (博學) translation by Huang Han-ching and Chen Wei-ping (638 pages), no longer listed by new-book retailers. What Taiwanese bookstores sell now are simplified-character editions: Central Compilation & Translation Press, 2003 (trans. Feng Wenguang, Liu Min and Zhang Yi), and Shanghai Joint Publishing, 2020 (trans. Feng Wenguang, 720 pages).

## What Canetti says

Quotations below are from Stewart's translation (page numbers from the Continuum/FSG edition). This post covers where crowds start and what they are, command and sting, how the two correspond, and the most-cited passage from the history chapters.

### The starting point: the fear of being touched, reversed

The book's first sentence: "There is nothing that man fears more than the touch of the unknown." Houses, locks, the distance we keep from others on the street, the tension while waiting for an apology after being bumped: all of these come from that fear.

Then the book's first reversal: "It is only in a crowd that man can become free of this fear of being touched." It has to be a dense crowd, body pressed against body, so dense that "he no longer notices who it is that presses against him". The tighter the press, the safer people feel.

This is Canetti's biggest difference from Le Bon. Le Bon's crowd is where the individual is degraded; Canetti's crowd is a place people seek out, because it gives them a relief they cannot get anywhere else.

### Open and closed crowds

A spontaneous crowd starts with five, ten or twelve people, and suddenly "everywhere is black with people". It wants one thing: to grow. "The open crowd exists so long as it grows; it disintegrates as soon as it stops growing."

The closed crowd is the opposite: it accepts a boundary and gives up growth in exchange for permanence. It has fixed entrances, a space and a building; "it sets its hope on repetition". The church is the classic case. The building is still there after the people leave, and even empty it reminds them of the last time it was full.

### The discharge: what brings a crowd into being

The most important event in a crowd is "the discharge". "Before this the crowd does not actually exist; it is the discharge which creates it." Ordinarily people carry the distances that rank, status and property impose, standing apart like windmills on an enormous plain; in the moment of discharge, all those distances fall away and everyone feels equal.

He immediately adds: "It is based on an illusion." After the event, people go back to their own houses, their own beds, their own names. So the crowd fears disintegration, and can prolong the discharge only by taking in new people.

### Four attributes

Before classifying crowds, he lists four attributes:

1. **The crowd always wants to grow.** It has no natural boundaries; artificial ones will be broken through sooner or later.
2. **Within the crowd there is equality.** "one might even define a crowd as a state of absolute equality".
3. **The crowd loves density.** It can never feel too dense. He adds: "One day it may be possible to determine this density more accurately and even to measure it."
4. **The crowd needs a direction.** "A crowd exists so long as it has an unattained goal."

There is no leader on this list. Le Bon's crowd needs a leader to supply suggestion; Canetti's crowd needs only a direction, any direction, because what it fears is falling apart.

### Command and sting

This is the most-cited idea in the book and the one that travels best on its own. It is in the chapter "The Command" (pp. 303–333 in the English edition).

He starts with animals: "Commands are older than speech." A dog cannot understand words but understands commands. The original command is the lion's roar, which makes its prey flee. "The oldest command—and it is far older than man—is a death sentence". Human commands stopped taking lives long ago, but "Beneath all commands glints the harshness of the death sentence."

Then the core sentence: "Every command consists of momentum and sting." The momentum makes you act; the sting stays behind in you. When a command works as expected, the sting is invisible, showing itself at most as a faint reluctance before you obey. But it sinks deep and does not change: "In the whole psychological structure of man there is nothing less subject to change."

How does the sting come out? Only one way: wait for a situation that exactly matches the original one, with the roles reversed, and pass the same command on to someone else unchanged. "What spurs men on to achievement is the deep urge to be rid of the commands once laid on them." Children carry the heaviest load of commands: "No child, not even the most ordinary, forgets or forgives a single one of the commands inflicted on it." When they become parents, they hand the identical commands to the next generation.

In this framework freedom gets a strange definition. The free man is not the one who rids himself of commands after receiving them, "but the man who knows how to evade them in the first place".

### The parallel between crowd and power

This is where command and crowd connect. A command given to a single person leaves a sting; the same command given to many works differently. In a crowd, a command spreads sideways, each person passing it at once to those nearby, and "Since the command is immediately diffused, no sting is formed." "A command to a crowd leaves no sting behind."

The one exception is the army. Every command in an army comes from above, complete and isolated, "Thus an army should never be a crowd."

This is the "and" in the title. Power works through isolated commands, building up stings in each person; the crowd is one of the few places where many stings can be discharged at once. Canetti calls this the reversal crowd: a large number of people unite to turn on a group seen as the source of all their commands, "for the joint liberation of a large number of people from the stings of command they cannot hope to get rid of alone". To soldiers, any officer can stand for those who gave the orders; to workers, any employer. A revolutionary crowd forcing its way into the palace wipes out, in one stroke, the distance it was ordered to keep for years.

### Commands and irresponsibility

The last section of "The Command" is about people who did terrible things under orders. Afterward they say "I never did that", and they are not necessarily lying. Canetti's explanation: the sting stays in them but remains foreign, "It lives within its host as an alien, not subject to his authority, and thus does not cause him any feeling of guilt." He blames the sting rather than himself; he sees himself as its victim, "and thus has no feeling left for the real victim".

His conclusion is heavy: the command in its finished modern form is "the most dangerous single element in the social life of mankind". "The stings that man suffers must become burrs which can be removed with a touch." This chapter was published in 1960, a year before the Eichmann trial and the start of Milgram's obedience experiments (both 1961).

### The survivor and the epilogue

The other main thread is the survivor. "The moment of survival is the moment of power." The satisfaction of standing while others lie dead is power's most primitive nourishment. The epilogue ties the two threads together: the ruler is the survivor in extreme form, and the most unquestioned and therefore most dangerous thing he does is give commands; in the age of the bomb, the survivor "is mankind's worst evil, its curse and perhaps its doom". The book's final sentence: "If we would master power we must face command openly and boldly, and search for means to deprive it of its sting."

### The history chapters: inflation and Hitler

Two sections of "The Crowd in History" analyze Hitler directly. The first cites Hitler's own account in *Mein Kampf*: when war broke out in August 1914, he fell to his knees and thanked heaven. Canetti calls it "the one moment at which he himself honestly became part of a crowd", and says his whole later career was devoted to re-creating that moment from outside.

The second, "Inflation and the Crowd", is the passage most often held up against history. Inflation devalues each person along with the currency: "Together people are worth as little as each is worth alone." People who have been devalued do not forget, and "The natural tendency afterwards is to find something which is worth even less than oneself". His conclusion: "The object Hitler found for this process during the German inflation was the Jews." The Nazi treatment of the Jews "repeated the process of inflation with great precision".

The section includes a passage on Jews and money that describes how they "seemed" to Germans at that moment, but it is written in the stereotypes of the time, and the prose does not separate impression from fact. Canetti was himself Jewish; readers should still know how the passage is written.

### He barely qualifies anything

Some authors remind readers that their arguments are only theories; Canetti has no sentence like that. He often writes as "we", his sentences are mostly assertions, and his categories are presented as universal. The few places that count as qualifications: he says density may one day be measured; in the 1962 conversation, when Adorno pressed him on whether images mattered more to him than real crowds, he answered that real crowds are "incomparably greater" in significance and that the dictatorships he had lived through "are made up entirely of crowds"; and he said a second volume would follow with more on transformation. Readers have to separate observation, analogy and claim themselves; the book will not do it for you.

## How later research tested it

### Method: theory carried by images

Adorno's 1962 criticism put it most clearly. He liked that Canetti ignored disciplinary boundaries, but pointed out that in the book "the imagination—the representation of these concepts or facts" carried more weight than real crowds and real power; he also praised crowd symbols (fire, the sea, the forest as images of the crowd) as a real advance over Freud's archaic symbols and Jung's archetypes. Those two remarks together set the basic shape of the later assessment: insight without method.

In 1996 the Australian journal *Thesis Eleven* ran a special issue on Canetti. Johann Arnason called the book incomplete and insufficiently systematic, with core concepts of uncertain contour: "it may sometimes seem more appropriate to speak of theory-laden images". Axel Honneth went further and rejected it outright, finding Canetti's account of human nature one-sided and arbitrary, flattened onto the single image of killing and survival. In 2011 Andrea Brighenti replied in the same journal that Honneth misread the book, which never claims to offer a complete theory of human nature; Canetti, he argued, gives us "concepts without theory", and that is exactly its value.

Susan Sontag's 1980 essay "Mind as Passion" gave both the highest praise and the most precise description. She called it "his marvelous book", said it was "constructed as a work of polyphonic fiction", and noted that its method is deliberately ahistorical: "History is used only to furnish examples", with tribal anecdotes treated as having the same illustrative value as events in modern societies. She also said that the claim to universal categories lets Canetti avoid the obvious reference: Hitler. That needs a discount, since the two sections above analyze Hitler directly; the more accurate version is that he did not write the book as a study of Nazism.

### The reversed fear of touch: when crowding feels good

Canetti said density might one day be measured. Crowd psychologists working in the social identity tradition later did measure it.

Novelli, Drury, Reicher and Stott published two surveys in *PLOS ONE* in 2013. At a free outdoor music event in Brighton, UK (48 people), the more people identified with the crowd, the less crowded they felt (β = −.52), and through feeling less crowded, the more positive their emotions; among low identifiers, feeling more crowded meant less positive emotion, while high identifiers stayed positive however crowded it got. At a London march in support of the National Health Service (112 people), higher identifiers moved toward the densest centre of the crowd, and central location was linked to positive emotion. The authors concluded that the results are "inconsistent with approaches that suggest that crowding is inherently aversive".

This partly supports Canetti: people do seek out dense crowds and enjoy being in them. But it adds a condition Canetti did not have: the enjoyment depends on shared identity, not on the crowd as such. Reicher gives an example. On a packed commuter train, bodies are pressed together, everyone avoids eye contact and dislikes any contact, and the fear does not reverse at all. Only when the train is delayed and the explanation offered falls short do passengers start to see themselves as aggrieved commuters against the train company, and then they turn to each other, talk, and share sandwiches. A physical crowd is not a psychological crowd. Both studies are correlational with small samples, as the authors themselves note.

### Did inflation produce the Nazis? Economic historians check

"Hyperinflation drove the Germans to Hitler" is the core of Canetti's section and a very common story. In *Explorations in Economic History* (2023), Gregori Galofré-Vilà matched 1923 price data from over 280 cities against every election from 1924 to 1933: cities hit harder by inflation did not give the Nazis a higher vote share. Inflation predicted the vote for a different party, the Volksrechtspartei, founded for inflation victims, and lower turnout. In a separate study in the *Journal of Economic History* (2021), he and co-authors found that areas hit harder by the Brüning government's austerity in 1930–1932 voted more for the Nazis. And

So at the level of votes, Canetti's causal chain breaks, at least at the inflation link. But votes were not all he cared about: Galofré-Vilà's 2021 working paper also reports that where inflation was worse, mortality and anti-Semitism rose. The psychological mechanism, that devalued people look for something worth even less, is not directly refuted by these studies; it just can no longer explain who voted for the Nazis.

### Commands passed downward: a close finding in organizational psychology

The sting itself (unchanging, released only when roles reverse) has not been directly tested, and the concept is hard to test. The closest work is on the trickle-down of abusive supervision. Mawritz and colleagues (*Personnel Psychology*, 2012) surveyed 288 work groups across several industries in the southeastern United States: the more managers abused their supervisors, the more those supervisors abused their own employees, and supervisor abuse was in turn linked to hostile behavior among members of the work group, more strongly in teams with a hostile climate.

That points in the direction Canetti predicted: people who have been ordered about and humiliated pass the same thing down. But the explanation is different. The researchers use social learning theory: supervisors imitate those above them, rather than pulling out their own stings. They also note that the data are cross-sectional and cannot rule out influence flowing upward.

### What survives

- **Crowds without leaders.** There is no leader among the four attributes. That fits the leaderless riot he lived through in 1927, and it is closer than Le Bon's leader-and-suggestion model to the crowds later research found: social identity studies show that a crowd's norms are mostly worked out together on the spot, not poured in by one person.
- **Why people seek crowds.** Le Bon saw only how crowds make people worse; Canetti saw what crowds give people: equality, relief, freedom from the fear of being touched. Crowding research brought that idea back, with shared identity added as a condition.
- **The cost of commands.** The observation that people who acted under orders do not feel guilty predates both Milgram and the Eichmann trial. "Every command has a cost, and the cost gets passed down" is still a useful lens for any hierarchy.
- **Crowd symbols.** How nations imagine themselves (he writes that the Englishman likes to imagine himself at sea, while the German crowd symbol was the army, "the marching forest") struck even Adorno as a real contribution, and it is a question that can be checked against contemporary data.

## What the book adds

[Le Bon](/posts/learning/2026-11-17-lebon-the-crowd-en) asked what happens inside a crowd; [Hoffer](/posts/learning/2026-11-18-hoffer-true-believer-en) asked what kind of person walks in. Canetti asks how crowds and power produce each other: power works through a series of isolated commands that build up stings in everyone who obeys; the crowd is where those stings are discharged all at once.

For this series, that is an answer quite unlike the other classics: **people do not lose their reason in a crowd; they seek crowds out, because a crowd gives them what they cannot get otherwise: equality, release, freedom from the fear of being touched, and a chance to shed the accumulated weight of long obedience.** On this view, an apparently uncontrolled outburst may sit on top of a whole power structure that only issues orders downward and allows no discussion. It is still an image, not a tested cause; but it is a reminder that when a crowd erupts, you should also ask who has been giving them orders.

The next post moves from the group back into the individual head: [Kahneman](/posts/learning/2026-11-18-kahneman-thinking-fast-slow-en) used experiments to study how judgment goes wrong, the route of answering with data.

## Three questions for reading a theory made of images

When you read a sentence like Canetti's ("the crowd always wants to grow", "every command leaves a sting"), ask three questions: **Is this an observation, an analogy, or a causal claim? If it is a claim, what data would test it, and has anyone tested it? What is left once you take the examples away?** The three cases in this post happen to have three different outcomes: density and enjoyment has been measured and partly holds, with a condition attached; inflation leading to the Nazis has been measured and does not hold at the level of votes; the sting is an analogy, with research pointing the same way but the mechanism itself untested. An image can show you a question; it cannot answer it for you.

**Something you can do tonight**: don't start at page one. Read the opening four sections (pp. 15–20 in the English edition) and the chapter "The Command" (pp. 303–333), about forty pages, and then decide whether to read the whole book. While reading "The Command", think of an order you carried out and still remember clearly, and ask yourself: did you later say the same words to someone else?

## Taiwan's new workplace bullying law, and the other half Canetti would ask about

On 1 July this year, the workplace bullying chapter of Taiwan's Occupational Safety and Health Act took effect. Article 22-1 defines workplace bullying as conduct by people in the organization who, "利用職務或權勢等關係，逾越業務上必要且合理範圍，持續以冒犯、威脅、冷落、孤立、侮辱或其他不當之言詞或行為" (using their position or power, beyond what is necessary and reasonable for the work, persistently offend, threaten, ignore, isolate, insult or otherwise mistreat a worker), harming the worker's physical or mental health. Employers with ten or more workers must provide a complaint channel, and when the alleged bully is the head of the organization, the worker can complain directly to the local labor authority.

Read through Canetti, this law deals with the side giving the orders: specific, repeated acts that can be reported. Canetti would ask about the other half: what sting stays in the people treated this way, and do they pass it down? Many people's intuition about nursing is exactly this version: senior nurses bully junior ones, and the juniors grow up to bully the next cohort.

The data are more complicated than the intuition. Ma Shu-ching's 2014 doctoral dissertation at Kaohsiung Medical University surveyed hospital nurses in Taiwan: 157 (19.4%) had themselves been bullied at work; the main perpetrators were physicians (29.6%), followed by peers (26.8%); and nurses over 34 were 1.74 times as likely to be bullied as those 34 and under. The highest risks came from organizational conditions: nurses whose supervisors leaned autocratic faced 3.63 times the risk of those with a mixed leadership style, and those in bureaucratic organizational cultures 3.72 times the risk of those in mixed cultures.

Read through Canetti's images, this points to the system of commands itself rather than individual bad seniors: the more a place only issues orders downward without discussion, the more trouble it has. That is this post's extension of his idea, not Canetti's own analysis. The study is a cross-sectional survey and cannot tell us whether people who were bullied go on to bully others, which is exactly the part of Canetti's claim that most needs data and has least of it.
