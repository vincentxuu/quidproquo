In September 1954, at a Republican breakfast in Los Angeles, President Eisenhower said he had heard a very interesting definition of an intellectual: "a man who takes more words than are necessary to tell more than he knows."

Two years earlier, during the election, the press had started calling the intellectuals who backed the Democratic candidate Adlai Stevenson "eggheads." After the vote, the right-wing novelist Louis Bromfield drafted a dictionary entry for the word, which included "a person of spurious intellectual pretensions," "over-emotional and feminine in reactions to any problem," and "so given to examining all sides of a question that he becomes thoroughly addled while remaining always in the same spot."

Nine years later, the Columbia University historian Richard Hofstadter put these remarks on display as exhibits in the first chapter of *Anti-Intellectualism in American Life*, and then made a claim that runs against intuition: American resentment of intellectuals had grown stronger not because their position had declined but because they had become more important. "Once the intellectual was gently ridiculed because he was not needed; now he is fiercely resented because he is needed too much."

"Anti-intellectual" (反智) is also a common insult in Taiwan. Hofstadter warned in the same chapter that one reason the term is hard to define is that "its very vagueness makes it more serviceable in controversy as an epithet." This post tries to turn the epithet back into a concept: what Hofstadter actually said, which parts sixty years of work have overturned, which parts social science has picked up and turned into something measurable, and how readers can tell "anti-intellectualism" apart from distrust that has reasons.

## The book and its background

*Anti-Intellectualism in American Life* was published by Knopf in 1963 and won the 1964 Pulitzer Prize for General Nonfiction. It was Hofstadter's second Pulitzer; the first was the 1956 History prize for *The Age of Reform*. He was born in 1916, died in 1970, and taught American history at Columbia.

The book has six parts and fifteen chapters: two introductory chapters on definition, then religion (chapters 3–5), politics (6–8), business and practical culture (9–11), and education (12–14), with a final chapter on the situation of intellectuals themselves. In the prefatory note the author says up front that this is not a formal history "but largely a personal book," and that its theme is developed "by choice rather impulsive and by necessity only fragmentary."

The book is a product of the 1950s, and the first chapter says so: McCarthyism made intellectuals feel the critical mind was at a ruinous discount; the 1952 election, Stevenson against Eisenhower, was read by both sides as America's repudiation of intellectuals; and when the Soviet Union launched Sputnik in 1957, anti-intellectualism suddenly turned from a national disgrace into a hazard to survival. Hofstadter wrote about the remoter American past, but his question came from the politics in front of him.

The Traditional Chinese edition, 《美國的反智傳統》, was translated by 陳思賢, a political science professor at National Taiwan University, and published by 八旗文化 in 2018, with a fully revised second edition in January 2025 that includes a short recommendation by Yu Ying-shih, a foreword by the NTU philosophy professor 苑舉正, and the translator's introduction. The Simplified Chinese edition, 《美国生活中的反智主义》, translated by He Bochao, was published by Yilin Press in 2021.

The 2025 revised edition's marketing copy says that "the anti-intellectual undercurrent represented by Trump has always been carved into Americans' bones." That sentence sits some distance from what the author himself said; as the next section shows, Hofstadter spent a good number of pages trying to stop readers from reading his book that way.

This post is not a chapter-by-chapter guide, and it does not cover Hofstadter's essay of the following year, "The Paranoid Style in American Politics" (see [the paranoid style post](/posts/learning/2026-11-18-hofstadter-paranoid-style-en)). It looks at three things only: how he defined anti-intellectualism, where he thought it came from, and how much of that account survives sixty years later.

## What Hofstadter says

Quotations below are from the 1963 Knopf first edition, cited by chapter.

### Definition: resentment and suspicion, usually mixed with ambivalence

The first chapter's definition is broad. Hofstadter says anti-intellectualism is not a single proposition but a complex of related propositions; as an attitude, it "is not usually found in a pure form but in ambivalence"; and as a historical subject, it is not a constant thread but "a force fluctuating in strength from time to time and drawing its motive power from varying sources." He wrote the common strain that binds these attitudes together as:

> a resentment and suspicion of the life of the mind and of those who are considered to represent it; and a disposition constantly to minimize the value of that life.

He says this is as close to a definition as he finds it useful to venture, and he deliberately avoids a stricter one because it would be logically defensible but historically arbitrary.

He also marks what does not count. Intellectuals criticizing each other does not count; that is part of their job. Nor do the "anti-rationalist" philosophies of Nietzsche, Bergson, or William James. His concern is "widespread social attitudes, with political behavior, and with middle-brow and low-brow responses."

### Intelligence is praised; intellect is suspected

The second chapter separates "intelligence" from "intellect." Americans never doubt the value of intelligence, and intelligent people are always praised; what gets suspected is something else. Intelligence, in his account, works within a narrow, immediate, and predictable range; it is "a manipulative, adjustive, unfailingly practical quality." Intellect is "the critical, creative, and contemplative side of mind."

> Whereas intelligence seeks to grasp, manipulate, re-order, adjust, intellect examines, ponders, wonders, theorizes, criticizes, imagines.

His example is that the inventor Thomas Edison was all but canonized by the American public, while Josiah Willard Gibbs, who laid the theoretical foundations of physical chemistry, taught at Yale for thirty-two years in near obscurity.

The distinction applies to occupations too. The work of lawyers, doctors, engineers, and most professors depends on ideas but is not necessarily intellectual work. Borrowing Max Weber's distinction about politics, he says the professional "lives off ideas, not for them."

### Four invented oppositions

In the second half of chapter 2, Hofstadter lists the four premises behind the anti-intellectual case. He says the case against intellect is founded upon "a set of fictional and wholly abstract antagonisms":

- Intellect against **feeling**: it is supposedly inconsistent with warm emotion.
- Intellect against **character**: it supposedly stands for mere cleverness, which easily turns sly or diabolical. He quotes an 1843 account of frontier Indiana: "We always preferred an ignorant bad man to a talented one."
- Intellect against **practicality**: theory is held to be opposed to practice.
- Intellect against **democracy**: intellect is felt to be a form of distinction that defies egalitarianism.

Once these oppositions are accepted, the case for the intellectual is already lost, because who would sacrifice feeling, character, practicality, and democracy for a type of person who "at best is deemed to be merely clever and at worst may even be dangerous"? His answer is to reject the framing: intellect is not a claim against the other human excellences but a complement without which they cannot be fully realized.

The four parts that follow are, roughly, the history of these oppositions in four American arenas:

- **Religion** (chapters 3–5): "The American mind was shaped in the mold of early modern Protestantism." With no firm established church and fierce competition among denominations, evangelicals who stressed direct experience of God repeatedly beat a learned clergy. By the anti-evolution crusade of the 1920s, its leader William Jennings Bryan was calling the intellectual elite behind evolution a "scientific soviet." Hofstadter says Bryan combined two ancestral pieties: evangelical faith and populistic democracy.
- **Politics** (chapters 6–8): the 1828 election was described as a contest between "John Quincy Adams who can write / And Andrew Jackson who can fight." Jackson's side charged Adams's learning not as a compensating virtue but as an additional vice.
- **Business** (chapters 9–11): the typical self-made man made a virtue of his lack of formal schooling. But Hofstadter also concedes that the hostility ran both ways: chapter 9 opens by noting that for three quarters of a century American intellectuals had stigmatized business as the classic enemy of intellect, and that the businessman in American fiction was almost always crass and predatory.
- **Education** (chapters 12–14): while the U.S. Office of Education was promoting "life adjustment education," a national conference in Chicago in May 1947 adopted a resolution drafted by Charles Prosser. The movement's claim was that twenty percent of high school students were being prepared for college and another twenty percent for skilled occupations, while the remaining sixty percent were unfit for either and needed education for life adjustment. Hofstadter says this, in the name of "democracy," wrote off most of the nation's children as "more or less uneducable."

### Resented for being needed too much

The book's most important argument is in the part of chapter 2 about the twentieth century. The more complex society becomes, the fewer things ordinary citizens can judge for themselves: "Today he knows that he cannot even make his breakfast without using devices, more or less mysterious to him, which expertise has put at his disposal."

So the ridicule changed character: "What used to be a jocular and usually benign ridicule of intellect and formal training has turned into a malign resentment of the intellectual in his capacity as expert." The line about being resented for being needed too much comes from here, followed by: "Intellect is resented as a form of power or privilege."

He does not say this resentment is groundless. In the public eye, intellectuals usually act in one of two capacities, as experts or as ideologues, and both evoke "profound, and, in a measure, legitimate, fears and resentments." The expert quickens the public's resentment of being constantly manipulated; the ideologue arouses the fear of subversion. He lists the New Deal brain trust, the wartime Office of Strategic Services, and, in his own day, the CIA, the Atomic Energy Commission, and the Rand Corporation: agencies dealing with issues beyond the ordinary person's scrutiny that can, and often do, determine that person's fate.

Chapter 8, "The Rise of the Expert," stretches this history out: from the Progressive era on, intellectuals returned to the center of politics as experts, and this time the claim rested not on birth and gentility but on usefulness.

### The limits he drew himself

Hofstadter spends several pages of chapter 1 heading off misreadings, and these passages are the most often ignored:

- **Not a binary.** "I do not suffer from the delusion that the complexities of American history can be satisfactorily reduced to a running battle between the eggheads and the fatheads." Most of the public is simply "non-intellectual," with both a distrust of eggheads and a genuine yearning for enlightenment and culture, and is pulled different ways on different issues.
- **Pervasive, but rarely dominant.** "Although I am convinced that anti-intellectualism is pervasive in our culture, I believe that it can rarely be called dominant." The mild forms are the most widespread; the most malign forms are found mainly among small if vociferous minority groups.
- **Not a comparative study.** He admits that writing only about America reflects a "possibly parochial" interest, says anti-intellectualism exists in some form in most societies, and even quotes Leonard Woolf's remark that no people has ever despised intellectuals more than the British.
- **Usually a by-product of other aims.** "Men do not rise in the morning, grin at themselves in their mirrors, and say: 'Ah, today I shall torment an intellectual and strangle an idea!'" Anti-intellectualism usually attaches to justifiable, or at least defensible, causes: evangelical religion also spread humane and democratic sentiments, political anti-intellectualism was bound up with a passion for equality, and anti-intellectualism in education came from evangelical egalitarianism. So it has to be handled by "constant and delicate acts of intellectual surgery" that cut it away from those benevolent impulses without discarding the impulses.
- **Its leaders are not the unschooled.** Its spokesmen are mainly "marginal intellectuals, would-be intellectuals, unfrocked or embittered intellectuals, the literate leaders of the semi-literate."
- **Not a way to label individuals.** He says classifying or stigmatizing individuals is no concern of his. The book criticizes the anti-intellectual consequences of John Dewey's educational theories, but to call Dewey an anti-intellectual "would be absurd and impertinent."

### The conclusion: alienation and incorporation

The last chapter turns to intellectuals themselves. In the 1950s they were beginning to be accepted and recruited into government, and many came to fear the loss of alienation more than rejection. Hofstadter treats joining power and staying outside to criticize as personal choices, each with its hazards: the critic's characteristic intellectual failure is not understanding the limits under which power operates, and his characteristic moral failure is "an excessive concern with his own purity"; the expert who advises the powerful characteristically fails by being unwilling to bring independent thought to bear as criticism.

What worries him is a split of the intellectual community into two camps: technicians concerned only with power and implicitly accepting its terms, and willfully alienated intellectuals more concerned with their own purity than with making their ideas effective. The book's final paragraph says that apocalyptic predictions of liberal culture's collapse may be right or wrong, but they are more likely to breed self-pity and despair than the will to resist.

## Sixty years on: what was overturned, and what was taken up

People questioned the book in 1963, and later criticism runs along three lines: the author's position, his method and reading of populism, and the concept itself. In the last decade a new line has appeared: social science has turned "anti-intellectualism" into a measurable variable.

### Doubts from the start: he skipped the hardest stretch

Benjamin DeMott's 1963 review in *The New York Review of Books* was broadly favorable but pointed to a gap: Hofstadter built his argument that anti-intellectualism attaches to humane and democratic sentiments on nineteenth-century examples, and did not deal directly with the episodes closest to him and most damaging to intellectual culture, the Hiss case (the 1948 accusation that former State Department official Alger Hiss had spied for the Soviet Union, ending in a perjury conviction and becoming a landmark of the anti-communist mood) and the McCarthy years. Drawing on his own time as a soldier, DeMott suggested that the disdain of "educated" officers for everyone else during the war may have been part of the resentment that McCarthy later drew on; at moments when ordinary people feel their way up is blocked, anti-intellectualism becomes "impure," an outlet for decent aspiration as well as for vicious envy.

### The author's position: confusing intellect with intellectuals' interests

Hofstadter's student, the historian Christopher Lasch, wrote a retrospective essay on his teacher in *The New York Review of Books* in 1973 and was blunt about this book: "The defense of the intellectuals' tradition against popular anti-intellectualism ignores the anti-intellectualism of the intellectuals themselves and confuses intellect with the interests of intellectuals as a class."

In 2020, reviewing the Library of America collection of Hofstadter's work in *The New Republic*, Chris Lehmann extended this critique to Cold War liberalism: farmers and workers appear in the book as a fixed anti-intellectual force always ready to be mobilized, while the readiness of Hofstadter's generation of Cold War liberals to embrace state power carried its own risks, with the Kennedy administration's Vietnam war, a project of "the best and the brightest" (the title of David Halberstam's book mocking Kennedy's elite advisers), as the example.

This line of criticism does not say Hofstadter was wrong; it says his position kept him from seeing certain things. He admitted something similar himself in chapter 1: intellectuals apply a double standard to criticism from inside and outside their circle, and the same criticism coming from outside gets labeled anti-intellectual.

### The populism line: archival work dismantled the psychological reading

Hofstadter's reading of American populism is laid out mainly in *The Age of Reform* (1955): the farmers of the late-nineteenth-century People's Party appear as anxious, nostalgic, and given to nativism and anti-Semitism, and their movement later "soured" into 1950s McCarthyism. Traces of this argument show up in the political and religious chapters of *Anti-Intellectualism* too, for example in Bryan as a fusion of evangelical faith and populistic democracy.

The historian Charles Postel's 2019 article summarizes half a century of archival research: historians of populism (Woodward, Nugent, Pollack, and Rogin) argued in the 1950s and 1960s that the thesis was ahistorical, exaggerated, and unfounded, and the work of later decades, varied as its methods and interpretations are, points the same way: "Essentially none of this archival research has sustained the Hofstadter thesis. Quite the contrary, it has left it in ruins." Postel's target is *The Age of Reform*, not this book; but both books use the same method, describing mass movements in the language of social psychology and reading participants' political demands as symptoms of anxiety and disorder.

In a 2022 article in the *Journal of the History of Ideas*, Andrew McKenzie-McHarg named the risk of this method the pitfalls of "psychologizing history." In an interview he put it more concretely: first observing something in the present and then searching the past for earlier versions of it is risky for a historian, and Hofstadter's interpretations were "by no means fully immune" to that risk. *Anti-Intellectualism* says at the outset that it was written in response to the 1950s, which is exactly this structure.

### Three kinds: anti-intellectualism is not one thing

In 1991 the sociologist Daniel Rigney reread the book in *Sociological Inquiry* and argued that, broad as Hofstadter's definition is, it has a clear structure that splits into three analytically distinct kinds of anti-intellectualism, each from a different institution:

- **Religious anti-rationalism**: the sense that reason is emotionally barren, or the fear that it will shake absolute beliefs and lead to relativism. Mainly from evangelical churches.
- **Populist anti-elitism**: distrust of an educated elite's claims to superior knowledge, especially the suspicion that such knowledge serves class privilege. Mainly from political movements.
- **Unreflective instrumentalism**: valuing only thought that promises immediate practical payoff, without asking what the means are for. Mainly from the economic institutions of capitalism.

Education is where the three converge and are reproduced, not a source of its own. Rigney also noted that Hofstadter did not anticipate the influence of mass media, and proposed a possible fourth kind, "unreflective hedonism." His 1991 judgment was that the first two may have been receding, and that instrumentalism could be called the dominant form of anti-intellectualism in America at the time.

The value of this split is that the three kinds call for different remedies. Dialogue that works against religious anti-rationalism does nothing for instrumentalism; institutional reform that works against populist anti-elitism does nothing for religious anti-rationalism. Calling all of them "anti-intellectualism" hides which treatment fits.

### From attitude to variable: narrowed to "distrust of experts"

Over the past decade, political science and communication research have turned Hofstadter's concept into quantitative work, but with a changed definition. In a 2020 article in *Public Opinion Quarterly*, Eric Merkley defined anti-intellectualism as "the generalized mistrust of intellectuals and experts," and one common item comes from Oliver and Rahn (2016): "I'd rather put my trust in the wisdom of ordinary people than the opinions of experts and intellectuals."

Results under this definition are quite consistent:

- Merkley (2020), using the General Social Survey and an online sample of about 3,600 Americans, found that higher anti-intellectualism goes with stronger opposition to expert consensus positions on climate change, nuclear power, GMOs, and water fluoridation, more strongly among the politically interested. In an experiment, telling respondents that experts agree made highly anti-intellectual respondents more opposed. Randomly exposing respondents to populist rhetoric, even rhetoric unrelated to experts, made anti-intellectual predispositions weigh more heavily in how they processed expert cues.
- Merkley and Loewen (2021), in *Nature Human Behaviour*, followed 27,615 Canadians from March to July 2020. Among those lowest in anti-intellectualism, mask use rose from 21% in the third survey round to 81% in the eleventh; among those at the 95th percentile it rose only to 46%, and at the extreme it barely rose at all. The authors state the limits: anti-intellectualism cannot be randomly assigned, and the data come only from Canada.
- Matthew Motta's *Anti-Scientific Americans* (Oxford University Press, 2024) defines anti-intellectualism as the dislike and distrust of scientists, academics, and other experts. Using hundreds of polls going back to the 1940s and six decades of individual-level surveys, he estimates that about a third of Americans hold anti-intellectual attitudes at any given time; the share has been broadly stable over the long run, with peaks that line up with the rise of right-wing populists such as George Wallace and Donald Trump, and it became markedly politicized among Republicans after the Tea Party. He also finds that in periods of high public anti-intellectualism, Congress invites public health, climate, and economic experts to testify much less often.

A Pew Research Center survey released in January 2026 (5,111 adults interviewed in October 2025) adds another angle: 77% of U.S. adults have a great deal or fair amount of confidence in scientists to act in the public's best interests, about the same as the year before but still below the 87% of April 2020; 90% of Democrats and leaners versus 65% of Republicans and leaners, a gap that has been broadly unchanged since 2021.

These studies make Hofstadter's concept testable, but they also change it. Hofstadter's object of resentment was "intellect" and "intellectuals," and he specifically separated experts from intellectuals; the measurement studies measure distrust of experts, which is precisely the kind he called "in a measure, legitimate." An item like "I'd rather trust the wisdom of ordinary people" may capture anti-intellectualism, anti-elitism, and reasonable doubts about some experts' track records all at once.

### What has not been cleared away

- **The intelligence/intellect distinction.** Americans prize intelligence and suspect intellect; this is still the best first cut for analyzing "anti-intellectualism." What gets suspected is usually not competence but the kind of mind that questions and thinks too much.
- **The expert/intellectual distinction.** He said in 1963 that twentieth-century resentment was mainly of "the intellectual in his capacity as expert," and partly justified. Later measurement work effectively continued along this line, only treating it as the whole story.
- **Anti-intellectualism fluctuates; it does not simply decline.** He said American regard for intellectuals "is subject to cyclical fluctuations." Motta's picture from polling data, a baseline of about a third with peaks that move with political figures, fits this.
- **Resentment rises with importance.** In Pew's data, Americans' confidence in scientists peaked at 87% early in the pandemic, then fell through the years when experts were in the news every day and pandemic policy became a political fight. This is consistent with "resented for being needed too much," but these data cannot show that this mechanism caused it.
- **Anti-intellectualism rides on defensible causes.** Evangelical egalitarianism, political hostility to privilege, educational egalitarianism: these are his own examples. Merkley's finding that populist rhetoric alone can activate anti-intellectual predispositions says the same thing, that anti-intellectualism hitches a ride on another appeal.

## What the book adds

Books about crowds look at what a group does in a moment; Hofstadter looks at a society's long-run attitude toward slow thinking, and how religion, politics, business, and education pass that attitude down the generations. On why groups lose their reason, he offers two answers that still hold.

First, **the more a society depends on experts, the stronger the resentment of experts may become.** The targets of resentment he lists in Chapter 2 are exactly the slow, procedure-bound institutions: think tanks, intelligence agencies, the RAND Corporation, the Council of Economic Advisers. [The Kahneman post](/posts/learning/2026-11-18-kahneman-thinking-fast-slow-en) says organizations do better than individuals; Hofstadter points to the other side: the more a society hands decisions to such institutions, the more those left out may see intellect as power rather than a tool.

Second, **anti-intellectualism rarely appears alone; it usually rides on defensible causes**: equality, opposition to privilege, distrust of elites. Calling a group "anti-intellectual" therefore often misses what they are actually protesting. The Taiwan survey data below point the same way: Taiwanese respondents hardly doubt scientists' competence; they doubt their motives.

The next post is Hofstadter's ["The Paranoid Style in American Politics"](/posts/learning/2026-11-18-hofstadter-paranoid-style-en) from the following year. One way to remember the division: anti-intellectualism is an attitude; the paranoid style is a way of talking. The first answers "why distrust people who think too much," the second "how distrustful people tell their stories."

## Four questions before you call someone anti-intellectual

The next time someone is called "anti-intellectual," or you want to call someone that, ask four questions first:

1. **Is the doubt about competence or about motives?** "This expert doesn't know" and "this expert is speaking for someone" are two different things. The first is closer to what Hofstadter called anti-intellectualism; the second is closer to the resentment he called partly legitimate.
2. **Is it general or aimed at a specific target?** "No expert can be trusted" is very different from "this institution has a conflict of interest on this issue." The measurement studies define anti-intellectualism as generalized distrust.
3. **Could evidence change it?** If the complaint is about an expert's track record or interests, could new evidence change the view? Only if nothing could change it does it look more like an attitude than a judgment.
4. **What is the person saying "anti-intellectual" protecting?** Hofstadter warned that the word is handy as a label; Lasch warned that intellectuals can present their class interests as intellect itself. Before calling someone anti-intellectual, check whether you are defending a way of thinking or defending a circle.

**Something to do tonight**: read the first four pages of chapter 2 (from "Before attempting to estimate" through Weber's "lives off ideas, not for them"), then the two pages on experts in the same chapter (from "In the practical world of affairs" through "the expert by quickening the public's resentment"). Then find a recent comment or politician's remark that was called "anti-intellectual," and use the four questions above to decide which kind it is. In the Traditional Chinese edition, chapter 2 is titled 〈智識不受歡迎〉.

## Two threads in Taiwan: Yü Ying-shih, and a survey of trust in scientists

The idea of anti-intellectualism in Taiwan has two threads worth following.

The first is how the concept reached Taiwan. In the recommendation he wrote for the Traditional Chinese edition, Yu Ying-shih recalled reading the book in the late 1960s and writing a long essay in 1975, "Anti-intellectualism and the Chinese Political Tradition." While the essay was being serialized in the *United Daily News*, he wrote, it "touched, intentionally or not, the political nerve of Taiwan's academic and cultural circles, and drew a wide and sustained strong reaction." That, he said, is how some of Hofstadter's arguments reached Taiwan indirectly.

Read side by side, the two essays are more interesting for their differences than their similarities. Yu says at the outset that he does not regard anti-intellectualism as the main feature of the Chinese political tradition; judged by the civil service examinations, traditional Chinese government was, at least in form, relatively respectful of intellect. His subject is something else: how rulers control intellectuals. He traces it from the *Laozi*'s line that those who practice the Way do not enlighten the people but keep them ignorant, through Han Feizi's "take officials as teachers," to Li Si's order to burn the books. That is anti-intellectualism from above, from power. Hofstadter writes about anti-intellectualism from below, from evangelical egalitarianism, Jacksonian democracy, and the practicality of businessmen. Yu's essay is an analysis in the history of political thought, not an empirical study of anti-intellectual attitudes; but the word 反智 in Taiwanese readers' hands carries both uses: sometimes it means the public distrusting experts, sometimes it means power suppressing criticism.

The second thread is data. The TISP survey, published in *Nature Human Behaviour* in 2025, interviewed 71,922 people in 68 countries; the Taiwan sample was 1,204 people interviewed online with quota sampling in March 2023. The paper does not publish country breakdowns; the Taiwan figures below are calculated from the team's public raw data using each country's weights, and "agree" means choosing 4 or 5 on a five-point scale.

- **Overall trust**: Taiwanese respondents' mean trust in domestic scientists is 3.37 (on a 1–5 scale), 61st of 68 countries and below the global mean of 3.62; Japan is also at 3.37.
- **But the low score is not about competence.** The trust scale has four dimensions. Taiwan versus the global mean: competence 3.93 vs 4.02, almost no gap; integrity 3.25 vs 3.58, benevolence (concern for people's well-being) 3.26 vs 3.55, and openness to others' views 3.04 vs 3.33, gaps of about 0.3 points each. Agreement that "scientific research methods are the best way to find out if something is true or false" is 74% in Taiwan and 75% globally.
- **Doubts about scientists' motives are especially high.** 52% of Taiwanese respondents agree that "scientists are only interested in their own advantage," against 27% globally; 43% agree that "scientists are in cahoots with politicians and businesses," against 35%.
- **But common sense is not seen as more reliable than science.** 21% agree that "our society should rely more on common sense than on scientific studies," below the global 30%; 33% agree that "ordinary people should trust their life experience more than the recommendations of scientists," against 31% globally.

In Hofstadter's terms, Taiwan in this data looks less like a society suspicious of intellect itself and more like one suspicious of the motives of "the intellectual in his capacity as expert": it grants experts competence and trusts scientific method, but doubts that they work for everyone. That is the kind of resentment he called "in a measure, legitimate," and also the thing Merkley-style scales most easily blend in. So the next time you hear 反智 in Taiwan, it is worth asking first: is the doubt about knowledge, or about the people holding it?

There are three caveats on reading these data. The research team notes in its supplement that the Chinese word for "science" (科學) covers technology and engineering but not the humanities, which may affect Taiwan's results; cross-country comparisons of the scale achieve only the most basic level of measurement invariance, so differences between countries should be read conservatively; and online quota samples do not fully represent the whole population.
