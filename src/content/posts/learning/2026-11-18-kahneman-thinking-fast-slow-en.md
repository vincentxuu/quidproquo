Most articles about how irrational people are end up citing *Thinking, Fast and Slow*. Its author, Daniel Kahneman, was a psychologist who won the 2002 Nobel Prize in economics for bringing psychology into economics, and who died in 2024. The 2011 book brought decades of his work with his longtime collaborator Amos Tversky to general readers. In the introduction he says it is written for the office watercooler: "Mine is the proverbial office watercooler." He wanted to give people a richer vocabulary for discussing other people's judgments and choices, because "it is much easier, as well as far more enjoyable, to identify and label the mistakes of others than to recognize our own."

Earlier posts in this series asked what happens to people in crowds. Kahneman narrows the question further: you do not need a crowd; a single person judging alone already errs systematically. If that is true, group mistakes can be explained starting from the individual mind.

This post reads several of the book's most cited and most tested claims against more than a decade of large-scale replications, asks which hold up and which collapsed, and then looks at how these individual biases connect to groups losing their reason. You will come away with four questions for judging, on your own, how solid the evidence is the next time you see a headline saying research shows people are deeply irrational.

## The book's claims: two systems and a long list of biases

**Two systems, which the author calls fictional.** System 1 is fast, automatic, and effortless; System 2 is slow and effortful, and checks System 1's output, but it is lazy and often just accepts it. This is the book's backbone, but Kahneman is explicit: "they are fictitious characters," not two parts of the brain. "You should treat 'System 1' and 'System 2' as nicknames, like Bob and Joe."

**Priming: things you never noticed change your behavior (Chapter 4).** After seeing the word EAT, you are more likely to complete "SO_P" as SOUP; that is semantic priming. Kahneman then presents a series of more striking "social priming" experiments: students who unscrambled sentences containing words associated with old age, such as "Florida," "forgetful," and "bald," walked down the hallway more slowly; people shown images of money were less willing to help others; when polling stations were in schools, support for more school funding was higher. He tells the reader: "disbelief is not an option... You have no choice but to accept that the major conclusions of these studies are true."

This chapter also contains the lines closest to this series: some societies "prime obedience by large images of the Dear Leader," and "Can there be any doubt" that such images reduce independent thought? And: "reminding people of their mortality increases the appeal of authoritarian ideas."

**Ego depletion: willpower runs out (Chapter 3).** After a task that requires self-control, self-control on the next task is weaker. He says this line of experiments "has shown conclusively" the point. The chapter's most retold study concerns Israeli parole boards: approval rates jumped to about 65% after each meal break and fell to nearly zero before the next one, as tired and hungry judges fell back on the easiest option, denial.

**Anchoring: the first number you see pulls your judgment (Chapter 11).** Kahneman and Tversky rigged a wheel of fortune to stop only at 10 or 65. After spinning it, students estimated the percentage of African nations in the United Nations: those who saw 10 guessed 25% on average, those who saw 65 guessed 45%. He calls anchoring "one of the most reliable and robust results of experimental psychology."

**Loss aversion: losses hurt more than gains please (Chapter 26).** Toss a coin: tails you lose $100, heads you win $150. The expected value is positive, yet most people decline; they want to win about $200 before they will play. He says this "loss aversion ratio" is usually in the range of 1.5 to 2.5.

**Conclusion: individuals cannot be fixed, organizations do better.** The concluding chapter is quite pessimistic: "System 1 is not readily educable." After a lifetime of studying these problems, he says, he has only become better at recognizing situations where errors are likely. He puts his hope in organizations: "Organizations are better than individuals when it comes to avoiding errors, because they naturally think more slowly and have the power to impose orderly procedures," such as checklists.

One more chapter is worth keeping in mind. In Chapter 10, "The Law of Small Numbers," he admits that as a young researcher he too had "routinely chosen samples that were too small" and often obtained results that made no sense. His first paper with Tversky warned researchers to compute rather than rely on impressions. The same book warns about small samples, yet Chapter 4 asks readers to accept a batch of small-sample studies without question; he would later point out that contradiction himself.

## A decade later: what holds up and what collapsed

First, two terms for judging evidence. **Statistical power** is how likely an experiment is to detect an effect if the effect is real; with small samples power is low, and what gets detected is often luck. **Effect size** is how big an effect is; on the d scale psychologists use, 0.2 is small, 0.5 medium, 0.8 large, and something around 0.05 is close to nothing. From the 2010s psychologists began running "multi-lab replications": dozens of labs rerun a classic experiment following the same publicly registered procedure (preregistration), with samples large enough that power is no longer a concern. Another tool is the **meta-analysis**, which pools dozens or hundreds of studies on the same question into one average. A paper posted publicly before peer review is a preprint, and should be cited with caution. The judgments below rest mainly on these kinds of studies.

| Claim | What the book says | After replication | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Social priming | "Disbelief is not an option" | The Florida effect, money, flags, and mortality reminders all failed to reproduce in large replications | Largely collapsed; the author agrees |
| Ego depletion | "Shown conclusively" | Two multi-lab tests, effect sizes 0.04 and 0.06 | Largely collapsed |
| Hungry judges | Approvals jump from near zero to 65% after meals | Case order was not random; the effect is likely overstated | Disputed |
| Anchoring | Among the most reliable results | The phenomenon replicates, but random anchors like a wheel are much weaker | Survives, details revised |
| Loss aversion | Ratio of 1.5 to 2.5 | A meta-analysis of 607 estimates finds about 1.96 | Survives |
| Two systems | Fictional characters, a storytelling device | Criticized as a simplistic dichotomy; theorists say that version is outdated | Usable as a metaphor |

**Social priming: the author spoke up first.** In 2012 Doyen and colleagues reran the Florida effect with automatic timing and a larger sample and did not reproduce it; slower walking appeared only when the experimenters believed participants would walk slowly, meaning the experimenters' expectations shaped the result. That September Kahneman wrote to priming researchers: "I see a train wreck looming." He proposed that five labs replicate each other's effects in a chain, with large samples, full video recording, and a commitment to publish whatever the outcome.

Large replications followed. Holding a pen in your teeth to force a smile makes cartoons funnier: none of 17 labs reproduced it. Seeing money makes people more supportive of the existing system, and seeing a flag makes them more conservative: Many Labs 1 in 2014 reproduced neither across 36 samples and more than six thousand people. Mortality reminders make people defend their own group more: more than twenty labs in Many Labs 4 did not reproduce it either. In 2017 three researchers examined the 31 studies cited in Chapter 4 and found that all were successful, yet their median power was only 57%. Power of 57% means that even if the effects were real, about four in ten studies should have failed; a set of such studies that all succeeded means the failures mostly went unpublished. Kahneman replied in a comment: "I placed too much faith in underpowered studies." He did not retreat entirely; he still believes behavior can be primed, but "they cannot be as large and as robust as my chapter suggested."

**Ego depletion: two tests, both near zero.** In 2016, 23 labs and more than two thousand people reran it under one shared procedure: effect size 0.04. Supporters objected to the task design, so in 2021 supporters themselves led a test with 36 labs and more than 3,500 people: 0.06. Even if the effect is not zero, it is far smaller than the 0.30 the researchers expected. The hungry judges study is a different problem: its interpretation was challenged. Later work found that the board finished one prison's cases before each break, and prisoners without lawyers usually came last in each session, with lower approval rates anyway; simulations can produce a similar decline without any hunger. It should not serve as textbook evidence that tired people cut corners.

**Anchoring: the phenomenon is solid, but the wheel is the weakest kind of anchor.** Many Labs 1 reran Kahneman's own anchoring questions and reproduced them consistently. A meta-analysis of 393 effects and more than seventeen thousand people (still a preprint, not peer-reviewed, as of 2026) found a large average effect, but obviously random anchors such as wheels and dice had much smaller effects. Anchoring is real; the book's opening example just happens to be the weakest kind, and why anchoring happens, and whether some people are more susceptible, remain open.

**Loss aversion: challenged, and it held.** In 2018 Gal and Rucker argued that current evidence does not show losses to be, on balance, more impactful than gains. The rebuttals were strong: a 2020 study of more than seventeen thousand people found that more experienced people are less loss averse, but people at every level are loss averse; a 2024 meta-analysis of 150 papers and 607 estimates found an average ratio of about 1.96, within the book's 1.5 to 2.5. Prospect theory's original choice problems, rerun in 2020 with more than four thousand people in 19 countries, mostly reproduced as well.

**Two systems: usable as a metaphor, disputed as a theory.** In 2018 critics called the practice of sorting mental processes into two types, fast and slow, "a convenient and seductive myth"; dual-process theorists replied that they had long since abandoned that tidy dichotomy. Because Kahneman had already called the two systems fictional characters, the criticism barely touches him; the problem is that readers usually remember the simplified version, that the brain really contains a fast system and a slow one. The most persistent critic of the whole heuristics-and-biases program is Gigerenzer, who argues that many "biases" are judged against overly narrow standards; rephrase a probability question as "how many out of 100 people," for example, and far more people get it right. He is one side of a thirty-year debate, and his criticism should be read with that in mind.

## What the book has to do with groups losing their reason

Kahneman barely discusses crowds, but the book connects to this series' question in at least three ways.

**1. The passages closest to crowd politics are the least supported.** Flags making people more conservative, money making them more supportive of the system, mortality reminders making them cling to their own: these experiments all failed to reproduce in large replications. The line about leaders' portraits reducing independent thought cites no study at all; he extrapolated it from money priming. The idea crowd psychology most likes to borrow, that symbols in the environment change political attitudes without people noticing, is exactly where this book's evidence is weakest. That points the same way as the revision of Le Bon's "suggestion like hypnosis" in [the Le Bon post](/posts/learning/2026-11-17-lebon-the-crowd-en).

**2. The biases that hold up can add up to a whole market's losses.** Taiwan has unusually complete data. Using every trade on the Taiwan Stock Exchange from 1995 to 1999 (more than a billion trades by nearly four million traders), Barber, Lee, Liu, and Odean found that Taiwanese investors were about twice as likely to sell a stock at a gain as one at a loss, a widespread reluctance to take losses; individual investors as a group earned 3.8 percentage points a year less because of their trading, a total loss equal to 2.2% of Taiwan's GDP, while institutions earned 1.5 points a year more. A 2024 study of Taiwanese futures traders likewise found that the more reluctant traders were to take losses, the more they lost as they traded more often.

Two things should be kept apart here. The reluctance to realize losses (the disposition effect) is often treated as field evidence of loss aversion, but they are not the same thing: some research suggests investors care about gains and losses realized at the moment of sale rather than paper movements. What is certain is that this is not a quirk of a few small investors but a collective tendency of a market, with a measurable cost. For individual bias to become a group outcome, people do not need to infect each other; it is enough for many people in the same market to make the same mistake. In Taiwan this is not just history: in 2025 domestic individuals still accounted for 52.11% of trading value on the main market.

**3. Priming research is itself a case of a group belief rising and falling.** In 2017 Kahneman explained why he had believed: "What impressed me was the unanimity and coherence of the results reported by many laboratories." He now sees where that reasoning went wrong: a set of underpowered studies that all succeed is evidence that many failures were filed away. His 2012 letter worried about "the much larger population of colleagues who in the past accepted your surprising results as facts." A group of scholars who pride themselves on rationality believed collectively because the results were consistent and the stories memorable; that is the same phenomenon as the tulip story copied by the herd for two centuries in [the Mackay post](/posts/learning/2026-11-17-mackay-popular-delusions-en).

How did the field correct itself? Multi-lab studies, preregistered procedures, and publishing regardless of results: procedures, not individual self-discipline. That bears out the judgment of his concluding chapter: individuals cannot be fixed; organizations do better. Which raises the next question: if organizations and institutions matter so much, why do some societies collectively distrust these slow-thinking people and institutions? That is what [Hofstadter's Anti-Intellectualism in American Life](/posts/learning/2026-11-18-hofstadter-anti-intellectualism-en) takes up.

## Four questions for judging "people are irrational" research

1. **Were the samples large enough? Did every study succeed?** A series of small studies that all succeed is a warning sign in itself.
2. **Has there been a large replication?** Multiple labs, preregistered procedures? What did they find?
3. **How big is the effect?** Did replication shrink it? Ego depletion shrinking from an expected 0.30 to 0.06 means something entirely different.
4. **What is the dispute about?** Whether the effect exists, or under what conditions and how large? The loss aversion debate ended up being about what makes it larger or smaller, and the anchoring debate about which kinds of anchors; neither was overturned.

**Something to do tonight**: read Chapter 4 (about twenty pages), then Kahneman's 2017 comment (five paragraphs, linked in the references). Reading the same person's two accounts of the same studies six years apart is the fastest way to learn these four questions.

## If you want to read the book

- There are 38 chapters in five parts, and you do not need all of them. Read Chapter 1 (the two systems), Chapter 10 (the law of small numbers), Chapter 11 (anchoring), Chapter 26 (loss aversion), and the conclusion; read Chapters 3 and 4 as a record of how a field came to believe something and then saw it collapse.
- The traditional Chinese edition (Commonwealth Publishing, translated by Hung Lan) drew a 2013 controversy over mistranslations that led to refund requests; the 2023 new edition has the same translator. If you want to study the passages quoted here closely, compare with the English original.
- This post does not cover *Noise* (2021), which Kahneman co-wrote and which deals with the same person judging the same thing inconsistently.
