> 🌏 [中文版](/posts/learning/2026-11-18-kahneman-thinking-fast-slow)

In 2011, after walking readers through a series of priming experiments in Chapter 4 of *Thinking, Fast and Slow*, Daniel Kahneman told them: "disbelief is not an option. The results are not made up, nor are they statistical flukes. You have no choice but to accept that the major conclusions of these studies are true."

A year later, on 26 September 2012, he sent an email to researchers working on social priming: "your field is now the poster child for doubts about the integrity of psychological research." And: "I see a train wreck looming."

Five years after that, in February 2017, three researchers used a statistical method to examine the studies cited in Chapter 4 and concluded that readers should not treat them as evidence. Kahneman left a comment under their post: "What the blog gets absolutely right is that I placed too much faith in underpowered studies."

A Nobel laureate admitting that a chapter of his own book does not stand up usually gets reduced to one of two takes: "*Thinking, Fast and Slow* has been debunked", or "it's just one chapter, the rest is fine". Neither is right. This post is a survival audit: it checks the original text chapter by chapter against later evidence, to see what collapsed, what survived and what is still disputed, and how the way we read books like this should change.

## What this book is

*Thinking, Fast and Slow* was published by Farrar, Straus and Giroux in 2011 and dedicated to Amos Tversky, Kahneman's long-time collaborator, who died in 1996. It has five parts and thirty-eight chapters: Two Systems, Heuristics and Biases, Overconfidence, Choices, Two Selves. Kahneman was a psychologist who received the 2002 Nobel Prize in Economics for bringing psychological research into economics. He died on 27 March 2024, aged 90.

In the introduction he says the book is written for the watercooler: "Mine is the proverbial office watercooler, where opinions are shared and gossip is exchanged." He wants to enrich the vocabulary people use to talk about other people's judgments and choices, because "it is much easier, as well as far more enjoyable, to identify and label the mistakes of others than to recognize our own." He says it again in the Conclusions: the book is aimed at critics and gossipers rather than decision makers.

This framing matters. It explains why the book relies on so many vivid experimental stories, and it explains why Chapter 4 went wrong: a book meant to be remembered at the watercooler needs memorable results, and memorable results are often large effects found in small samples.

The Chinese editions matter too. The traditional Chinese edition, 《快思慢想》, was published by Commonwealth Publishing (天下文化) in October 2012, translated by Hung Lan (洪蘭). In 2013 it became one of Taiwanese publishing's rare translation scandals: readers on PTT listed errors sentence by sentence, the philosopher Wong Wai-hung (王偉雄) criticized it publicly, the Eslite bookstore chain agreed to refund it as a defective product, and the publisher said the translator had "digested" the original before rewriting it, agreeing only to case-by-case exchanges. One example cited was the second paragraph of Chapter 1: "you could solve it, with paper and pencil, if not without" (you could work it out on paper even if not in your head) was rendered as "if you have paper and pencil you will try to work it out; if you don't, you won't, because you know your mental arithmetic ability". In July 2023 Commonwealth released a "new edition" (576 pages), still translated by Hung Lan, and the product page does not say whether the translation was revised. The simplified Chinese edition, 《思考，快与慢》, was published by CITIC Press in 2012, translated by Hu Xiaojiao, Li Aimin and He Mengying. Anyone reading the passages discussed here closely should check them against the English.

This post is not a guide to the whole book, and it leaves aside *Noise* (2021), written with Sibony and Sunstein, which deals with a different problem: the same person judging the same case inconsistently. It looks only at the five claims that have been cited most and tested most.

## What the text says

Quotations are from the FSG English edition, cited by chapter.

### Two characters the author calls fictitious

System 1 is fast, automatic and effortless; System 2 is slow and effortful and checks System 1's output, but it is lazy and often just endorses it. This framework is the skeleton of the book, but at the end of Chapter 1 Kahneman makes a point of saying: "System 1 and System 2 are so central to the story I tell in this book that I must make it absolutely clear that they are fictitious characters." They are not two places in the brain, nor systems in the standard sense.

He admits that sentences like "System 2 calculates products" are "considered a sin" in his professional circles, because they seem to explain a person by the little people inside their head. His defense is that this is description, not explanation, and that sentences with agents as subjects are easier to remember. "You should treat 'System 1' and 'System 2' as nicknames, like Bob and Joe."

### Chapter 4: disbelief is not an option

Chapter 4, "The Associative Machine", starts with semantic priming: if you have just seen the word EAT, you are more likely to complete SO_P as SOUP. Then he writes: "you must accept the alien idea that your actions and your emotions can be primed by events of which you are not even aware."

The examples get more striking as they go. In Bargh's experiment, New York University students who unscrambled sentences containing words like Florida, forgetful and bald walked more slowly down the corridor afterward (the "Florida effect"). People made to smile by holding a pencil in their teeth found cartoons funnier. Support for school funding was higher when the polling station was in a school. People exposed to images of money were less willing to help others and set chairs farther apart (118 vs. 80 centimeters). Thinking of a shameful act made people more inclined to buy soap.

The chapter also contains the sentences closest to this series. From money priming he extrapolates to politics: some societies "prime obedience by large images of the Dear Leader", and "Can there be any doubt" that the ubiquitous portraits reduce spontaneous thought and independent action? Then: "reminding people of their mortality increases the appeal of authoritarian ideas."

Then comes the "disbelief is not an option" passage. He anticipates the reader's doubt and explains it in advance: you don't believe it because these effects happen in System 1, and "you have no conscious access to them." He also adds a qualifier: "The effects of the primes are robust but not necessarily large."

### Chapter 3: ego depletion and the hungry judges

Chapter 3, "The Lazy Controller", introduces Baumeister's ego depletion: after an act of self-control, subsequent self-control gets worse. His language is strong: these experiments have "shown conclusively" that all variants of voluntary effort draw at least partly on a shared pool of mental energy. He also describes the glucose studies: people who drank lemonade sweetened with glucose were not depleted, those who drank it with Splenda were.

The most retold part of the chapter is the Israeli parole study: after each meal break the approval rate jumped to about 65%, then fell steadily to nearly zero before the next break. His reading: "tired and hungry judges tend to fall back on the easier default position of denying requests for parole."

### Chapter 11: an anchor on a wheel of fortune

Kahneman and Tversky once rigged a wheel of fortune to stop only at 10 or 65. After spinning it, students estimated the percentage of African nations among UN members: those who saw 10 guessed 25% on average, those who saw 65 guessed 45%. He calls anchoring "one of the most reliable and robust results of experimental psychology", and adds that "the list of anchoring effects is endless."

### Chapter 26: losses weigh more than gains

The third principle of prospect theory is loss aversion: "losses loom larger than gains." He asks the reader to consider a coin toss: tails you lose $100, heads you win $150. The expected value is positive, yet most people refuse. How much would you need to win to balance an equal chance of losing $100? For many people, about $200. "The 'loss aversion ratio' has been estimated in several experiments and is usually in the range of 1.5 to 2.5."

### Chapter 10: he already knew about small samples

Before reading Chapter 4, it is worth reading the opening of Chapter 10, "The Law of Small Numbers". Kahneman describes reading, in 1969, an article criticizing psychologists for using small samples: "Like most research psychologists, I had routinely chosen samples that were too small and had often obtained results that made no sense." His first paper with Tversky, "Belief in the Law of Small Numbers" (1971), advised researchers to regard their statistical intuitions with "proper suspicion" and to "replace impression formation by computation whenever possible."

Chapter 10 of the same book warns against small samples, while Chapter 4 asks readers to accept a body of small-sample studies without reservation. Kahneman later pointed out this contradiction himself.

### Conclusions: System 1 can't be taught, organizations do better than individuals

The Conclusions are pessimistic about improving judgment: "System 1 is not readily educable." Apart from some effects of age, his own intuition is as prone to overconfidence as it was before he studied it. All he has gained is a better ability to recognize situations where errors are likely, and "I have made much more progress in recognizing the errors of others than my own."

He puts his hope in organizations: "Organizations are better than individuals when it comes to avoiding errors, because they naturally think more slowly and have the power to impose orderly procedures." Examples include checklists and the premortem.

## What later research corrected

The summary first, then the details.

| Claim | What the book says | Later evidence | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Social priming (Ch. 4) | "disbelief is not an option" | Cited studies get an R-Index of 14; Florida effect, money, flag and mortality reminders failed in large replications | Largely collapsed; author concedes |
| Ego depletion (Ch. 3) | "shown conclusively" | Two multi-lab tests: d=0.04, d=0.06 | Largely collapsed |
| Hungry judges (Ch. 3) | Approval rate jumps from near zero to 65% after meals | Case order not random; simulation shows effect overestimated | Disputed; effect size not credible |
| Anchoring (Ch. 11) | One of the most reliable results | Phenomenon replicates; random anchors much weaker; mechanism and individual measurement shaky | Phenomenon survives, details revised |
| Loss aversion (Ch. 26) | Ratio of 1.5 to 2.5 | Challenged; meta-analysis of 607 estimates gives about 1.96 | Survives, with moderators |
| Two systems (the framework) | Fictitious characters for telling the story | "Two types" typology called a myth; theorists reply that is an outdated version | Usable as a metaphor, disputed as theory |

### The author spoke first

In January 2012, Doyen and colleagues published a replication of the Florida effect in PLOS ONE. With automated timing and a larger sample, their first experiment found no effect. The second was more troubling: they led half the experimenters to believe participants would walk slower, and the slowing appeared only when the experimenters believed it would. Manual timings were also skewed by the experimenters' expectations.

Kahneman sent his letter that September. It is worth seeing what it says and does not say. He writes "Count me as a general believer", and he is skeptical of replications by people new to priming research, because these effects are "subtle" and require high-level skills. His concern is reputation: the problem is not the few people who have challenged priming results, but "the much larger population of colleagues who in the past accepted your surprising results as facts" and have now attached a question mark to the field. His proposed solution is concrete: five reputable labs each pick the priming effect they are most confident in and replicate one another's in a daisy chain, with adequate samples, video documentation of every detail, and a public commitment to publish whatever the outcome.

The 2017 comment is his judgment on the evidence itself. He accepts the basic conclusions of the analysis while explicitly withholding an opinion on its statistical techniques and on the validity of the individual studies. He explains why he had believed: "What impressed me was the unanimity and coherence of the results reported by many laboratories." He now sees the flaw: "Unanimity of underpowered studies provides compelling evidence for the existence of a severe file-drawer problem", because underpowered studies must occasionally fail even when the hypothesis is true.

He did not retreat completely. He still believes actions can be primed. "But I have changed my views about the size of behavioral priming effects – they cannot be as large and as robust as my chapter suggested." The comment ends: "I am still attached to every study that I cited, and have not unbelieved them", and he would be happy to see each of them replicated in a large sample.

### Chapter 4: individual results after the R-Index of 14

In 2017 Schimmack, Heene and Kesavan analyzed the 12 articles and 31 studies cited in Chapter 4. All were significant, but median observed power was only 57%. Thirty-one studies with 57% power all succeeding is wildly improbable, which means the published record was filtered. After correcting for that inflation, their R-Index was 14 (out of 100). The authors note that the R-Index is not a direct estimate of true power, and that below 50 it is a conservative one, suggesting the results were obtained by capitalizing on chance.

The metric itself is debated, and Kahneman pointedly declined to endorse it. The large replications that followed are more persuasive:

- **The Florida effect**: Doyen 2012 did not reproduce it and found an explanation in experimenter expectations.
- **Facial feedback with a pencil in the teeth**: a 2016 registered replication across 17 labs, in which not a single lab found a significant result. In 2022 the Many Smiles Collaboration redesigned the test with 3,878 people in 19 countries: mimicking a smiling face in a photo, or moving facial muscles as instructed, made people slightly happier; for the pen-in-mouth version, the evidence was "less conclusive". Facial feedback may be real, but the classic experiment the book uses is not good evidence for it.
- **Money priming**: Lodder et al. (2019) meta-analyzed 246 experiments and found an overall effect of g=0.31, with clear signs of publication bias; the 47 preregistered experiments combined gave g=0.01, effectively zero. The authors caution that most of those 47 measured the same outcome (support for the existing social system), so the result cannot be extended to all money priming. In 2014, Many Labs 1 also failed to reproduce "seeing banknotes increases support for the existing system" across 36 samples and 6,344 people.
- **Flags and death**: Many Labs 1 also tested "seeing the flag makes people more conservative", and it did not replicate. The effect of mortality reminders on defending one's group's worldview was tested by Many Labs 4 across twenty-odd labs and more than two thousand people, with and without the original authors' involvement in the design, and it did not replicate under any condition (it tested another classic study from the same theory, not the one the book cites).
- **Not all of them**: in the same R-Index analysis, the study on nodding versus shaking one's head while hearing persuasive messages scored 100, and the study on watching-eyes posters in an office kitchen scored 44. Not every study in the chapter is equally weak.

For this series, the point to remember is this: the passages in Chapter 4 that come closest to groups and politics correspond to experiments that did not reproduce in large replications. Flags, banknotes and mortality reminders all failed. The sentence about leader portraits reducing independent thought cites no study at all; it is his extrapolation from money priming. The claim crowd psychology most likes to borrow, that symbols in the environment change political attitudes without people noticing, is exactly where this book's evidence is weakest.

### Chapter 3: two multi-lab tests of ego depletion

In 2016, Hagger and colleagues organized a registered replication of ego depletion across 23 labs and 2,141 people. The effect size was d=0.04, with a 95% confidence interval that included zero. Supporters of the theory objected that the task used was not a good one. So in 2021 a test was led by Vohs (author of the money priming studies in Chapter 4 and a proponent of ego depletion) across 36 labs and 3,531 people, letting each lab choose one of two procedures, with preregistered analyses. The preregistered result was d=0.06, not significant; a Bayesian analysis found the data about four times more likely under no effect than under an effect. An exploratory analysis without exclusions found d=0.08. Even if the effect is not zero, it is far smaller than the expected d=0.30, and nothing like "shown conclusively".

The hungry judges are a different problem: not a failed replication but a contested interpretation. The same year, PNAS published a response by Weinshall-Margel and Shapard. After interviewing lawyers, a judge and prison staff, they found that cases were not heard in random order: the board tried to finish all the cases from one prison before a break, and prisoners without lawyers usually came last in each session, with a 15% success rate compared with 35% for those with counsel. They also noted that decisions were made by three-member panels of a judge, a criminologist and a social worker, not by a single judge. In 2016 Glöckner used simulations to show that a few reasonable assumptions about how judges decide when to take breaks, with no hunger or fatigue involved, produce a decline of similar size, so the original effect was probably overestimated. The original authors responded that unrepresented prisoners were not systematically heard last, and the dispute has not been settled. What is clear is that the study should not be used as textbook evidence that tired people take shortcuts.

### Chapter 11: anchoring survives, but the wheel is the weakest kind of anchor

The anchoring phenomenon is robust. Many Labs 1 tested the very anchoring items from Kahneman's own 1995 study with Jacowitz, and they were among the ten of thirteen effects that replicated consistently.

The details are being revised. Röseler and Schütz (2022) pooled the most complete set of openly available anchoring data (393 effects, 17,708 people) and found a large average effect, g≈0.69; but random anchors, such as wheels, dice or the last digits of a phone number, had much smaller effects than fixed or informative anchors, and subliminal anchors had none. As of 2026 this meta-analysis is still an unreviewed preprint and should be cited with caution. A meta-analysis the same group published in a journal in 2024 found that "who is more susceptible to anchoring" can barely be measured: the reliability of every scoring method was very low. Four studies published in 2025 (898 people) did not support a key prediction of insufficient adjustment, the classic explanation.

In other words, the book's opening example, a wheel of fortune, is the kind of anchor with the weakest effect. Anchoring is real, but why it happens, and whether it differs between people, are open questions.

### Chapter 26: loss aversion was challenged and held up

In 2018 Gal and Rucker published "The Loss of Loss Aversion", concluding from a review of the evidence that "current evidence does not support that losses, on balance, tend to be any more impactful than gains". Part of the article asks why the concept is so hard for social scientists to give up.

The reply came with two large bodies of evidence. In 2020 Mrkva, Johnson, Gächter and Herrmann tested five samples totaling 17,720 people: more domain knowledge and experience went with less loss aversion, and older people were more loss averse, but "people of all knowledge and experience levels were loss averse." The title borrows from Mark Twain: loss aversion has moderators, but reports of its death are greatly exaggerated.

More decisive was the 2024 meta-analysis by Brown, Imai, Vieider and Camerer in the *Journal of Economic Literature*: 607 estimates of the loss aversion coefficient from 150 articles across economics, psychology and neuroscience. The mean coefficient was 1.955, with a 95% credible interval of 1.820 to 2.102, ruling out both 1 (no loss aversion) and Tversky and Kahneman's 1992 estimate of 2.25. The authors note that readers worried about publication bias might prefer a lower value such as 1.74. The book's "1.5 to 2.5" falls within that range.

The basic experiments of prospect theory held up as well. In 2020 Ruggeri and colleagues reran Kahneman and Tversky's original 1979 choice problems with 4,098 people in 19 countries and 13 languages: 94% of items replicated and 12 of 13 theoretical contrasts replicated, with some attenuation.

### Two systems: a usable metaphor, a disputed theory

In 2018 Melnikoff and Bargh argued in *Trends in Cognitive Sciences* that dividing mental processes into "unintentional, uncontrollable, unconscious, efficient" and "intentional, controllable, conscious, inefficient" is "a convenient and seductive myth": no one has tested whether those features actually cluster together, and counterexamples are plentiful. The co-author, Bargh, is the author of the original Florida-effect study.

That same year Pennycook, De Neys, Evans, Stanovich and Thompson replied that this "list of features" version had long been abandoned by dual-process theorists themselves. Current theories distinguish defining features from typical correlates and do not require all the features to align. They also quoted Morewedge and Kahneman to the effect that System 1 often produces skilled responses automatically, not just errors.

Kahneman's "fictitious characters" line works as a defense here: he never claimed there were two systems in the brain. The trouble is that readers usually remember not that disclaimer but the simplified version, "the fast one makes mistakes and the slow one corrects them".

The most persistent criticism of the whole heuristics-and-biases program comes from Gigerenzer, whom Kahneman called "our most persistent critic" in the book's endnotes. In a long essay written after Kahneman's death in 2025, Gigerenzer credits him with turning statistical thinking into a mainstream research topic, but argues that the program judged people by "narrow norms". His examples: when the Linda problem is rephrased from "which is more probable" to "how many of 100 people like Linda", 93% answer correctly; when participants draw descriptions from an urn themselves, base-rate neglect largely disappears; when a surgeon says "90% are alive" rather than "10% are dead", the choice of frame carries information, so reacting to it is not necessarily a bias. He also points out that *Thinking, Fast and Slow* barely mentions this opposing work. Gigerenzer was the other side of a thirty-year debate, and the essay is explicitly a "personal view"; read it with that in mind.

### What was not swept away

- **Prospect theory and loss aversion.** This is the core of Kahneman's Nobel, and the part that has been replicated and meta-analyzed at the largest scale. It held up.
- **The anchoring phenomenon.** Numbers pull estimates toward them, reliably. What needs revising is the mechanism and which anchors work.
- **The warning in Chapter 10.** Small samples, all results significant, large effects: when all three appear together, be suspicious. This is the chapter readers should most take away, and the standard the author later applied to himself.
- **The organizational advice in the Conclusions.** His judgment that individuals cannot change but organizations can do better matches how psychology reformed itself after the replication crisis: preregistration, multi-lab studies and commitments to publish regardless of outcome all rely on procedure rather than individual self-discipline. All of them were in his 2012 letter.
- **The author's conduct.** He admitted the error in public, explained where his reasoning went wrong, and stated clearly what he still believed and what he no longer did. That is more useful than either a full retraction or a full defense.

## Where this book sits in the chain of explanations

The three books in L2 (Le Bon, Hoffer, Canetti) are about crowds and rely on observation, analogy and history. Kahneman shrinks the problem to one person, one lab, one question: people already err systematically when judging alone, no crowd required. This layer's contribution is testable claims; its cost is distance from the group. The book says almost nothing about crowds.

But there are at least two connections. The first is empirical. In a 2025 experiment published in *Games and Economic Behavior*, Kogan, Schneider and Weber found that self-serving bias in processing information, already documented for beliefs about one's own outcomes, also shapes beliefs about collective outcomes; and adding a market institution to aggregate private information produced, "if anything", slightly more collective self-delusion rather than correcting it. Individual bias can grow directly into collective belief.

The second connection is what happened to the book itself. In his 2012 letter Kahneman described an entire academic community that had "accepted your surprising results as facts"; in 2017 he admitted being persuaded by "the unanimity and coherence" of many labs' results. When Gal and Rucker ask why loss aversion is so hard to question, they are also asking how a scientific community forms and keeps a shared belief. The replication crisis is itself a case of a group belief forming and coming apart, among people who considered themselves the most rational of all.

The next post, Hofstadter's *Anti-Intellectualism in American Life*, takes the scale back up to society. Kahneman says organizations are more reliable than individuals because they think slowly and follow procedures; Hofstadter asks why some societies come to distrust exactly those slow-thinking people and institutions.

## One criterion to take away

Next time you see a report that "research shows humans are deeply irrational", ask four questions:

1. **Power**: How big were the samples? Was every study in the series significant? Kahneman's own lesson is that a run of underpowered studies all succeeding is itself a warning sign.
2. **Replication**: Is there a preregistered large-scale or multi-lab replication? What did it find?
3. **Effect size**: How large is the effect, and did replications shrink it? Ego depletion shrinking from an expected 0.30 to 0.06 means something entirely different.
4. **The shape of the dispute**: Is the argument about whether the effect exists, or about when and how big? Who is speaking? The loss aversion debate ended up as "it has moderators", the anchoring debate as "which anchors"; neither ended as "debunked".

**Something you can do tonight**: read Chapter 4 (about twenty pages), then Kahneman's 2017 comment (five paragraphs, linked in the references). Putting the same person's two accounts of the same studies, six years apart, side by side is the fastest way to learn these four questions. If you have the traditional Chinese edition, check the "disbelief is not an option" passage against the English.

## The Taiwan connection

What does this mechanism look like in Taiwan in 2026? The part of the book that holds up best is loss aversion, and Taiwan has some of the most complete data in the world for watching something like it in a real market.

The Taiwanese stock market is a retail market. According to the Taiwan Stock Exchange's Fact Book 2026, domestic individuals accounted for 52.11% of trading value on the exchange in 2025 (67.99% in 2021). In the fourth quarter of 2025, 5.83 million individuals traded less than NT$100 million each that quarter, a record since statistics began; in December, day trading made up 37.12% of trading value.

Barber, Lee, Liu and Odean used the exchange's complete trading records for 1995 to 1999 (more than a billion trades by nearly four million traders) for two studies. The first looked at the disposition effect, the tendency to sell winning stocks too early and hold on to losers: in aggregate, Taiwanese investors were about twice as likely to sell a stock when holding it at a gain as at a loss, and 84% of investors sold winners faster than losers. Individuals, corporations and dealers were reluctant to realize losses; mutual funds and foreigners, together less than 5% of trading value, were not. The second did the accounting: individuals' aggregate portfolio lost 3.8 percentage points a year to trading, a total equal to 2.2% of Taiwan's GDP, almost all of it traceable to aggressive orders; institutions gained 1.5 percentage points a year.

These data are more than twenty years old, but newer research points the same way. In 2024 Cheng, Lee and Lin tracked trade-by-trade records of traders on the Taiwan Futures Exchange: traders with a strong disposition effect lost more the more they traded, while those with a weak or reversed disposition effect profited more the more they traded.

Two readings need to be kept apart here. First, the disposition effect is often presented as field evidence of loss aversion, but the two are not the same thing: Barberis and Xiong (2009) found that prospect theory defined over annual gains and losses often fails to predict a disposition effect, and predicts it more reliably when gains and losses count only once realized. So the Taiwanese data show that Taiwanese investors systematically avoid realizing losses; whether loss aversion is the cause has other candidate explanations. Second, the title of Barber and colleagues' paper asks about the "aggregate investor". This is not a quirk of a few retail traders but a collective tendency of a whole market, with a measurable cost. That is the question this series keeps asking: when do individual-level biases add up to group-level outcomes?

## Limitations

- The original was read in the full FSG English edition, but only the introduction, the "Useful Fictions" section at the end of Chapter 1, the ego depletion section of Chapter 3, the priming sections of Chapter 4, the openings of Chapters 10 and 11, the loss aversion section of Chapter 26 and the part of the Conclusions on improving judgment were read closely; the other chapters were not checked paragraph by paragraph.
- The traditional Chinese translation was not compared sentence by sentence; the translation example comes from 2013 news reports, with the original sentence checked against the English book. Whether the 2023 new edition revised the translation cannot be determined from the publication information.
- For Gal and Rucker 2018 and Mrkva et al. 2020 only the abstracts were read; Glöckner's 2016 simulation and the original authors' response are taken from the account in Daljord, Urminsky and Ureta 2017; the 2016 17-lab facial feedback replication is taken from Schimmack and colleagues' post and the British Psychological Society's report.
- Röseler and Schütz 2022 is a preprint. Its abstract says random anchors "did not have effects", while the discussion says their effects were significantly smaller but still above zero; this post follows the discussion.
- The R-Index is a contested statistical tool; the judgment on Chapter 4 rests mainly on the later large-scale replications, not on that metric alone.
- Many Labs 4 tested a different study from the same theory, not the Greenberg et al. 1990 study the book cites.
- The Taiwanese disposition-effect data cover 1995 to 1999; the 2024 study covers the futures market, not the stock market. Neither directly proves loss aversion.
- Gigerenzer is a party to the debate; Schimmack's blog also takes a clear position on priming research.
