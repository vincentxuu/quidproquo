In 1969, an internal proposal titled "Smoking and Health Proposal" at the American tobacco company Brown & Williamson said:

> Doubt is our product since it is the best means of competing with the "body of fact" that exists in the mind of the general public. It is also the means of establishing a controversy.

The same document goes on: "Truth is our message," because of its power to withstand a conflict and sustain a controversy; if the company sticks to well-documented fact, it can dominate a controversy.

The document was made public during the tobacco litigation of the late 1990s and is now held in the University of California, San Francisco's Industry Documents Library. What is most striking about it is that the plan was not to lie but to manufacture doubt with true facts.

The philosopher Lee McIntyre quotes this memo in his 2018 book *Post-Truth* and treats it as a key to understanding "post-truth." His argument is that post-truth did not appear suddenly in 2016; its playbook had already been written during decades of campaigns by the tobacco industry and climate denial, and 2016 only widened the battlefield from one scientific theory to the whole of factual reality. This post looks at which parts of his argument hold up, which have been revised by later research, and how to turn it into a usable test.

## What this book is

*Post-Truth* was published by MIT Press in February 2018 as a small-format introduction in its Essential Knowledge series, with seven chapters: what post-truth is, science denial as a road map, the roots of cognitive bias, the decline of traditional media, social media and fake news, whether postmodernism led to post-truth, and fighting post-truth. McIntyre is a philosopher of science, at the time a research fellow at Boston University's Center for Philosophy and History of Science, and later wrote *The Scientific Attitude* (2019), *How to Talk to a Science Denier* (2021), and *On Disinformation* (2023).

The Traditional Chinese edition, 《後真相：真相已無關緊要，我們要如何分辨真假》, translated by 王惟芬, was published by China Times Publishing in December 2019, at about two hundred pages. The first line of the publisher's copy reads "Fake news is interfering in Taiwan's election!", and the book came out a month before the 2020 presidential election.

The word is older than 2016. Oxford Dictionaries chose "post-truth" as its 2016 word of the year after a roughly 2,000 percent spike in usage over the previous year; "alt-right" and "Brexiteer" were on the same shortlist. But according to Oxford, the usage first appeared in the playwright Steve Tesich's January 1992 essay in *The Nation*, "A Government of Lies." Tesich was writing about America after Watergate, Iran-Contra, and the Gulf War:

> In a very fundamental way we, as a free people, have freely decided that we want to live in some post-truth world.

Tesich's emphasis was on the demand side: the public itself did not want bad news and preferred to be protected by the government rather than disturbed by the truth. McIntyre's emphasis is on the supply side: who manufactures doubt and how. Both angles come up again below.

This post does not cover *On Disinformation*, and it does not go chapter by chapter through the two chapters on media history. It looks at his definition, the genealogy of science denial, the psychological evidence he relies on, and the most contested argument, about postmodernism.

## What the original says

Quotations below are from the 2018 MIT Press English edition, cited by chapter.

### Definition: not denying facts but subordinating them to a position

Chapter 1 starts from Oxford's definition: post-truth denotes circumstances in which objective facts are less influential in shaping public opinion than appeals to emotion and personal belief. McIntyre notes that "post" here does not mean "after" in time but that truth has been eclipsed and made irrelevant.

He then lays out a spectrum of relationships to truth: "falsehood," saying something untrue without meaning to; "willful ignorance," saying it without bothering to check; "lying," saying something false with intent to deceive; what the philosopher Harry Frankfurt calls "bullshit," indifference to whether something is true; political "spin," knowingly bending the truth to one's advantage; and finally self-deception and delusion, actually believing what virtually all credible sources would dispute. The purest form of post-truth, he says, is "when one thinks that the crowd's reaction actually does change the facts about a lie."

His example is a 2016 exchange between CNN's Alisyn Camerota and former House Speaker Newt Gingrich. Camerota cites FBI statistics showing violent crime falling; Gingrich replies that the average American doesn't feel crime is falling, and ends with: "As a political candidate, I'll go with how people feel and let you go with the theoreticians."

But his core definition is not about emotion; it is about a double standard:

> Deniers and other ideologues routinely embrace an obscenely high standard of doubt toward facts that they don't want to believe, alongside complete credulity toward any facts that fit with their agenda.

This, he says, "is not the abandonment of facts, but a corruption of the process by which facts are credibly gathered and reliably used to shape one's beliefs about reality." So his summary is that post-truth is not the claim that truth does not exist but that "facts are subordinate to our political point of view"; it is a form of "ideological supremacy."

### Science denial as a road map

Chapter 2 is the backbone of the book. To understand post-truth, he says, we have a ready-made road map: the past decades of science denial on climate change, vaccines, and evolution. Drawing on the historians of science Naomi Oreskes and Erik Conway's *Merchants of Doubt* and Ari Rabin-Havt's *Lies, Incorporated*, he starts the line with tobacco:

- In 1953, the heads of the major tobacco companies met at the Plaza Hotel in New York to decide how to respond to a study linking cigarette tar to cancer in mice. The public relations expert John Hill advised them to stop arguing over whose cigarettes were healthier and instead jointly "fight the science" by sponsoring more "research." The Tobacco Industry Research Committee was created and ran full-page ads in newspapers across the country, reaching 43 million people, saying there was "no proof" that smoking caused cancer.
- This continued for four decades, until in 1998 the companies agreed to a $200 billion settlement in exchange for protection from future lawsuits, in the process releasing thousands of internal documents showing they had known all along. They were later found guilty of fraud under the federal racketeering statute.
- Oreskes and Conway show that the same "tobacco strategy" was later used in disputes over Reagan's Strategic Defense Initiative, nuclear winter, acid rain, the ozone hole, and global warming, sometimes with funding directly from the tobacco industry. McIntyre boils the strategy down to: "Find and fund your own experts," use them to tell the media there are two sides to the story, push your side through public relations and lobbying, and use the resulting public confusion to question whatever scientific result you wish to dispute.
- A leaked 1998 memo from an American Petroleum Institute meeting listed "Victory Will Be Achieved When," including when average citizens and the media "understand" uncertainties in climate science and media coverage reflects "balance."

The figures he cites in this chapter: a 2013 survey of about 4,000 peer-reviewed papers taking a position found 97 percent agreeing that global warming was caused by human activity, while only 27 percent of American adults thought "almost all climate scientists agree." The chapter concludes: "In a world where ideology trumps science, post-truth is the inevitable next step."

### Bias, media, and social media

Chapter 3 covers cognitive bias: cognitive dissonance, conformity, confirmation bias, and two findings he stresses. One is the political scientists Brendan Nyhan and Jason Reifler's "backfire effect": shown evidence correcting a mistaken belief, some partisans became more convinced of the original error. The other is the Dunning–Kruger effect: the least competent overestimate themselves the most. He also stresses that bias affects liberals and conservatives alike.

Chapters 4 and 5 cover media: 24-hour news, talk radio, false balance, and social media becoming a main source of news. In chapter 3 he writes a line well suited to criticizing "balanced" reporting: "The halfway point between truth and error is still error."

### Postmodernism as the godfather of post-truth?

Chapter 6 is the book's most contested. McIntyre boils postmodernism down to two theses: there is no objective truth; and any claim to truth is only a reflection of the speaker's political ideology. He revisits the "science wars" of the 1990s and the Sokal affair and argues that the right borrowed this toolkit for doubting truth from the left-wing academy.

His direct evidence is mainly two items: the philosopher Daniel Dennett telling *The Guardian* in 2017 that what the postmodernists did "was truly evil," and the pro-Trump blogger Mike Cernovich telling *The New Yorker* that he had read postmodern theory in college: "If everything is a narrative, then we need alternatives to the dominant narrative." The chapter's conclusion: "Thus is postmodernism the godfather of post-truth."

He adds a caveat: postmodernists are not entirely to blame for how their ideas were misused, but they must accept some responsibility for undermining the idea that facts matter in assessing reality.

### Fighting back

The final chapter's advice is direct. First, always fight back against lies. His example is John Kerry's 2004 decision not to respond to the "Swift Boat Veterans" smears for two weeks, followed by his loss: the point of challenging a lie is not to convince the liar, because "every lie has an audience." Second, the media should stop false balance; he cites the BBC's decision from 2014 to stop giving climate deniers equal airtime. Third, repeating true facts works: research he cites found that even motivated reasoners reach an "affective tipping point" after encountering enough disconfirming information.

The book's last pages say: "Post-truth is not about reality; it is about the way that humans react to reality," and "Truth still matters, as it always has."

## What later research revised

Eight years after publication, some parts of the book have been strengthened and some overturned, and the overturned parts happen to make his advice more defensible.

### The backfire effect: hard to find

The backfire effect that chapter 3 relies on is the part most revised since. In 2019 the political scientists Thomas Wood and Ethan Porter published five experiments in *Political Behavior*, with more than 10,100 subjects and 52 issues where backfire should be most likely, including Nyhan and Reifler's original question on Iraqi weapons of mass destruction: "we found no corrections capable of triggering backfire." On the whole, subjects' views moved closer to the facts after a correction, among liberals and conservatives alike.

Nyhan himself published a review in *PNAS* in 2021 titled "Why the backfire effect does not explain the durability of political misperceptions." He notes that only two of the original five experiments showed backfire, that the study was often misread as showing all corrections are counterproductive, and that later research suggests backfire effects are "extremely rare in practice," with corrections moderately effective on the whole.

His new explanation is that misperceptions persist not because corrections fail but because their effects decay over time, political elites keep sending the opposite message, and corrections rarely reach the people who need them. His figure: in the weeks before the 2016 election, only 3% of those who read an article from an untrustworthy website that had been fact-checked also read the fact-check.

This is good news for McIntyre. Chapter 3 uses the backfire effect to show how hard people are to persuade, while chapter 7 cites research showing that repeated facts eventually work; the later evidence supports chapter 7.

### The Dunning–Kruger effect: much smaller

In 2020, Gilles Gignac and Marcin Zajenkowski argued in *Intelligence*, using data from 929 members of the public, that the conventional quartile comparison is confounded by two statistical phenomena: most people think they are above average, and regression toward the mean. Using the tests they recommend, the accuracy of people's self-assessed intelligence was about the same across all levels of ability; the title says outright that "the Dunning–Kruger effect is (mostly) a statistical artefact." Gignac's 2024 follow-up concludes that the effect has a negligible influence on a limited segment of the population.

The debate is not over. Nyhan's 2021 review notes that recent research finds people more aware of what they don't know than earlier work suggested, though the least informed are still the most likely to overestimate themselves. A cautious statement: the effect exists but is much smaller than the book describes.

### The science-denial line: the evidence got stronger

Chapter 2's genealogy, on the other hand, has been reinforced. In a 2023 paper in *Science*, Geoffrey Supran, Stefan Rahmstorf, and Naomi Oreskes analyzed 16 global warming projections in ExxonMobil's internal documents and scientists' publications from 1977 to 2003. They projected an average of 0.20°C of warming per decade, almost identical to the 0.19°C of contemporary academic and government models; most were consistent with later observations, and the company's in-house models had an average skill score of 72%. The conclusion: Exxon knew as much in the 1970s–1990s as academic and government scientists did. In other words, the side manufacturing doubt had science as good as its opponents'.

The tobacco end has original documents too. The 1969 memo at the top of this post is publicly available in UCSF's Industry Documents Library, where anyone can read the full text.

### The postmodernism chapter: the weakest link

The philosopher Darrell Arnold's 2018 review is very positive about the book overall but says the postmodernism chapter "will strike many as overly facile": it does not examine any postmodern theorist's views in detail, and it does not deal with developments such as Rorty's pragmatic turn or the late Foucault's move toward something like Habermas's discourse theory. Arnold agrees that the postmodern view that everything is interpretation may have played into right-wing hands, but that is a long way from "godfather."

In terms of evidence, this chapter is indeed different from the earlier ones: chapter 2 has court judgments and leaked internal documents, while chapter 6's direct evidence is one philosopher's comment and one blogger's remark. Arnold also notes two other problems: McIntyre uses "post-truth" inconsistently, sometimes meaning "there is no reality except the agreed-upon one" and sometimes only "emotion outweighs facts"; and the final chapter, titled "Fighting Post-Truth," offers few concrete methods.

### The "post-truth era" itself: exposure is smaller than assumed

The book's premise is that we have entered a new era. Later research splits on that premise. A 2024 Perspective in *Nature* by Ceren Budak, Brendan Nyhan, Duncan Watts, and colleagues argues that public discussion often overstates the effects of online misinformation; existing evidence shows low exposure to false and inflammatory content, concentrated among a narrow fringe with strong motivations to seek it out. In the same issue of *Nature*, Ullrich Ecker, Naomi Oreskes, Stephan Lewandowsky, and colleagues published a Comment arguing that calling concern about misinformation a "moral panic" rests on a selective reading of the evidence; false claims are believed more when repeated, and corrections are only partially effective.

The two sides actually agree on a lot: average exposure is low, but the part concentrated among a few people can cause real harm. They differ on whether to focus on the average or on the tail. The long-run data on conspiracy theories from the previous post point the same way: the intuition that "more and more people don't care about truth" itself needs evidence. McIntyre himself writes in the final chapter: "We are not post-truth any more than we are pre-truth, unless we allow ourselves to be."

### What has not been cleared away

- **The double standard as a test.** Demanding unlimited evidence for unwanted facts and swallowing wanted ones whole is the book's most useful diagnostic, and it does not depend on any particular psychological effect.
- **The genealogy of the tobacco strategy.** It rests on original documents and court judgments, and the 2023 Exxon study added another piece. It is the best-evidenced part of the book.
- **False balance.** The criticism of journalistic practice in "the halfway point between truth and error is still error" holds whether or not a post-truth era exists.
- **Persistent correction works.** His final chapter's optimism now has better evidence than it did then; what needs adding are the three problems Nyhan identifies: effects decay, elite messages offset them, and corrections don't reach the right people.
- **The spectrum.** Falsehood, willful ignorance, lying, bullshit, spin, and delusion call for different responses. Calling them all "post-truth" or "fake news" hides how to respond.

## Where this book sits in the explanatory chain

This is the first book of L5, the contemporary layer. The previous post's Hofstadter described the paranoid style's "piling up evidence," the rhetoric of a few political movements in the 1960s; McIntyre describes that rhetoric after it was industrialized: with budgets, PR firms, its own experts and research institutes, aiming not to make people believe a conclusion but to make them feel "it's still controversial."

It also connects back to L3. The Kahneman post said individual judgment errs systematically; McIntyre points out that organized interests know this and exploit it. But the backfire effect he leaned on later failed to replicate, which is the replication crisis from two posts back appearing in another field: a striking, memorable, easily quoted finding that vanished in large samples.

The next post is Rauch's *The Constitution of Knowledge*. McIntyre diagnosed the problem and gave advice at the individual level; Rauch asks at the institutional level: what rules and institutions a society uses to produce and test knowledge, how those institutions are attacked, and how they should be defended.

## One test to take away

The next time you meet a discussion where "scientists still disagree," ask four questions:

1. **Is the standard of doubt consistent?** Does the strictness demanded of opposing evidence also apply to the evidence the speaker cites?
2. **Who pays, and who benefits?** Is there an industry interest behind the call for "more research"? Is the funding disclosed?
3. **Is the dispute about evidence or about the dispute itself?** Is new data being offered, or only the claim that "there are two sides to this"? The tobacco memo states its goal plainly: "establishing a controversy" is the point.
4. **What kind of relationship to truth is this?** An honest mistake, not bothering to check, a deliberate lie, indifference to truth, or sincere delusion? Correction is enough for the first two; the others call for exposing interests or dealing with trust.

**Something to do tonight**: read the 1969 "Smoking and Health Proposal" in UCSF's Industry Documents Library; it is only nine pages. Then take a recent claim you've seen that "the research is not settled" and check it with the four questions above.

## Back to Taiwan

What does this mechanism look like in Taiwan in 2026? The regulation of heated tobacco is almost a present-day version of chapter 2's genealogy.

Start with what Taiwan did. On January 12, 2023, the Legislative Yuan passed amendments to the Tobacco Hazards Prevention Act, in force from March 22 that year: e-cigarettes were banned outright, and heated tobacco products must pass a health risk assessment by the Ministry of Health and Welfare before they can be manufactured, imported, or sold. On July 29, 2025, the Health Promotion Administration announced that the first 14 products from two companies had passed with conditions; the first 8 products' licenses took effect on October 11. One of the seven conditions bans misleading wording on packaging such as "safer than cigarettes," "can help you quit," or "lower risk."

Now the other side. In December 2025 the anti-tobacco advocacy organization STOP and France's Contre-Feu published a report analyzing documents obtained from the European Commission through freedom-of-information requests. The documents show that Philip Morris International (PMI), at a previously undeclared meeting in September 2023, raised with the Commission's trade department what it regarded as "trade barriers" for heated tobacco products, with Taiwan on the list; Taiwan's health risk assessment was described as "burdensome, long and costly" and as contradicting Taiwan's World Trade Organization obligations. PMI also asked the EU to raise the issue at Taiwan's WTO Trade Policy Review and in the EU–Taiwan trade dialogue.

Set against McIntyre's chapter 2, three things stand out:

- **The strategy hasn't changed; the frame has.** In the 1950s the line was "there's no proof smoking causes cancer"; in this document a health review is repackaged as a trade issue. Neither requires denying any fact.
- **Taiwan's regulatory design targets exactly that genealogy.** McIntyre asks in chapter 2: since science can never "prove" any theory, why isn't the burden of proof on the challengers? Taiwan puts the burden of proof on the company that wants to sell, review before sale, and bans "lower risk" claims outright.
- **But real scientific questions must be kept separate.** Whether heated tobacco is less harmful than cigarettes, and by how much, is itself an empirical question still under study, not a settled matter. McIntyre's test is not "everything the industry says is false" but: who bears the burden of proof, whether claims are backed by independent evidence, and whether anyone is using "it's still controversial" as the product itself.

There are two caveats on reading this material. STOP is an advocacy organization with a clear position, and the report relies on documents that were partly disclosed and partly redacted; the Health Promotion Administration's announcements do not name the companies, and this post does not link Taiwan's review results to any particular company.

## Limitations

- The book was read from a full-text copy of the 2018 MIT Press edition circulating online. I read chapters 1, 2, 6, and 7 closely, plus the chapter 3 passages on the backfire effect and Dunning–Kruger, and skimmed the media-history chapters 4 and 5. The Traditional Chinese translation was not compared line by line; the Chinese renderings in the Chinese version of this post are my own.
- I did not read Tesich's 1992 essay in full; the quotation comes from *The Nation*'s 2016 retrospective and Oxford Dictionaries' account.
- For Wood and Porter 2019 I read the abstract, excerpts of the body, and the UK fact-checker Full Fact's 2019 summary; for Gignac 2024, only the abstract.
- The body of Budak et al. 2024 is paywalled, so I read only the abstract and references; I read Ecker et al. 2024 in full.
- For the STOP and Contre-Feu report I read the executive summary and the sections on Taiwan. The date of Taiwan's legislation comes from an article excerpt by Health Promotion Administration officials in *Nicotine & Tobacco Research*; the review's progress comes from Central News Agency and the Ministry of Health and Welfare's press releases.
