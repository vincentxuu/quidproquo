In January 2014, Tom Nichols, a professor of national security affairs at the U.S. Naval War College, published an essay on the conservative site *The Federalist* titled "The Death of Expertise." He complained that any claim to expertise now drew charges of "appeals to authority" and "elitism." The essay ended with five rules for dealing with experts, followed by a question and its answer: "And how do I know all this? Just who do I think I am? Well, of course: I'm an expert."

The essay grew into a book of the same name from Oxford University Press in 2017. At the end of that book he was not optimistic: "Tragically, I suspect that a possible resolution will lie in a disaster as yet unforeseen." It might be a war, he wrote, or an economic collapse.

Three years later the pandemic came. In the preface to the 2024 second edition, he recalls saying, both when he wrote the first edition and later on the road discussing it, that a war, an economic downturn, or even a pandemic might be the moment that snaps the public back to its senses, and then concedes: "I was distinctly wrong on all three of these possibilities." The irony is that in chapter 6 of his own book, he lists the move from explanation to prediction as one of the most common ways experts go wrong.

This post looks at three things: what illness the book diagnoses, how the pandemic tested it, and why critics say he gave the illness the wrong name. You will come away with three questions for judging, the next time you find yourself unwilling to believe an expert, whether your reasons hold up.

## The book and its author

*The Death of Expertise: The Campaign against Established Knowledge and Why It Matters* was published by Oxford University Press in 2017, with an introduction, six chapters, and a conclusion: chapter 1 asks what an expert is; chapters 2 through 5 deal in turn with why conversation became exhausting, universities, the internet, and journalism; chapter 6 covers how experts themselves go wrong; and the conclusion covers experts and democracy. The updated and expanded second edition of 2024 adds a new preface and a seventh chapter, "The Experts, the Public, and the Pandemic." By the time the second edition came out, Nichols was a staff writer at *The Atlantic* and a professor emeritus at the Naval War College; according to the second edition's preface, the book had been translated into fourteen languages as of 2023.

The Traditional Chinese translation is 《專業之死：為何反知識會成為社會主流，我們又該如何應對由此而生的危機？》, published by 臉譜 in 2018 and translated by 鄭煥昇; a second edition came out in May 2024. But the Chinese second edition's table of contents still contains the 2017 edition's six chapters and conclusion, with no pandemic chapter, and the author bio still describes him as a current professor at the Naval War College. Readers who want his pandemic reflections need the 2024 English edition.

For chapter 7's reflections on the pandemic, this post relies on the second edition's preface, the essay Nichols published in *The Atlantic* in March 2024 adapted from that chapter, and an interview he gave GQ the same year.

## What Nichols says

### What has died

Nichols first clarifies the title: he does not mean that expert ability has died. Doctors, lawyers, and engineers are all still here, and we take it for granted that we depend on them when we break a leg, get arrested, or board a plane. What has died is something else:

> This, however, is a reliance on experts as technicians. It is not a dialogue between experts and the larger community, but the use of established knowledge as an off-the-shelf convenience as needed and only so far as desired. Stitch this cut in my leg, but don't lecture me about my diet.

He calls this "the death of the ideal of expertise itself," and describes it with a sentence from the 2014 essay: a Google-fueled, Wikipedia-based, blog-sodden collapse of any division between professionals and laypeople, students and teachers, knowers and wonderers.

Two examples in the introduction set the tone for the whole book. One is former South African president Mbeki, who accepted the claim that AIDS was not caused by a virus and rejected offers of drugs; Nichols cites estimates by doctors at the Harvard School of Public Health that the decision cost well over three hundred thousand lives. The other is a 2014 *Washington Post* poll: after Russia's invasion of Ukraine, only one in six Americans could identify Ukraine on a map, and the median answer was off by about 1,800 miles; the less people knew where Ukraine was, the more they supported U.S. military intervention, and those who placed it in Latin America or Australia were the most enthusiastic.

He sees this as something worse than ignorance: "it is unfounded arrogance, the outrage of an increasingly narcissistic culture that cannot endure even the slightest hint of inequality of any kind."

### Four drivers

Chapters 2 through 5 are his list of drivers.

- **Human nature.** Confirmation bias, plus the Dunning–Kruger effect, in his plain-language version: "the dumber you are, the more confident you are that you're not actually dumb."
- **Universities.** Tuition has turned college into a consumer experience in which students learn, above all, that "the customer is always right." He gives a counterintuitive example: in the U.S., the parents most resistant to childhood vaccines are not small-town mothers with little schooling but educated parents in Marin County, north of San Francisco, who are "educated just enough to believe they have the background to challenge established medical science."
- **The internet.** He does not see the internet as the main cause but as an accelerant: it creates the impression of a shortcut to erudition, and whatever you believe, you can find enough material to support it.
- **Journalism.** Under intense competition, the media ask audiences what they want to know instead of telling them what matters.

### How experts fail: four kinds of failure

Chapter 6 is the book's most substantial chapter. It opens with a story of an expert losing to a layperson: in 2002, a historian argued that the "No Irish Need Apply" job notices of nineteenth-century America were a myth of victimization invented by Irish immigrants, and most historians accepted this for over a decade; in 2015, an eighth grader searching databases of old newspapers found dozens of such notices and proved him wrong. Nichols then sorts expert failure into four kinds:

- **Ordinary failures of science.** A whole field guesses wrong with limited knowledge: in the 1970s, America's top nutritional scientists told the government that eggs, rich in cholesterol, might be lethal, and only in 2015 did the government say eggs were acceptable. He hardly counts this as failure, since "science is a process, not a conclusion."
- **Cross-expertise.** Experts carry their authority from one field into another. His leading example is the chemist Linus Pauling, winner of two Nobel Prizes, who in later life promoted mega-doses of vitamin C against colds and cancer and kept at it even as study after study showed he was wrong.
- **Prediction.** Science's task is to explain, but society demands prediction. In 1982 the Soviet expert Seweryn Bialer wrote in *Foreign Affairs* that the USSR would not face a true systemic crisis in the next decade; less than eight years later the Soviet Union ceased to exist. On June 6, 1991, the MIT professor Stephen Meyer testified to the Senate that hints of a Soviet military coup were "pure flights of fancy"; nine weeks later the coup happened.
- **Deception.** Fabrication, falsified data, faked credentials. He considers this the rarest but most dangerous, citing Andrew Wakefield, who lost his UK medical license in 2010 over misconduct in the research linking vaccines and autism. He also cites a 2005 survey: about 2% of scientists admitted to fabricating, falsifying, or "modifying" data at least once, and 14% said they had seen colleagues do it; a third admitted to lesser questionable practices, and more than 70% said they had seen colleagues do so.

On psychology's replication crisis, he cites a 2015 *New York Times* report that when 100 psychology studies were repeated, more than half did not hold up; but he then quotes the Harvard scholar Gary King's rebuttal calling the replication project "completely unfair—and even irresponsible."

### Citizens' duties: Russell's three rules

The last section of chapter 6 is about repairing the relationship. Experts must own their mistakes publicly; laypeople must learn the difference between failure and fraud. He cites Philip Tetlock's work in arguing for tracking the accuracy of experts' and pundits' past predictions, and quotes Bertrand Russell's three principles of skepticism from 1928:

> (1) that when the experts are agreed, the opposite opinion cannot be held to be certain; (2) that when they are not agreed, no opinion can be regarded as certain by a non-expert; and (3) that when they all hold that no sufficient grounds for a positive opinion exist, the ordinary man would do well to suspend his judgment.

When experts agree, the opposite view cannot be held as certain; when they disagree, a layperson should treat no view as certain; when they all say there are no sufficient grounds, the ordinary person should suspend judgment.

The conclusion separates experts from decision-makers: experts are "the knowers," elected officials are "the deciders." "Experts can only propose; elected leaders dispose." In a democracy, he writes, experts should be "servants, not masters"; but if citizens will not learn, experts will take over by default, and democracy will slide toward mob rule or technocracy.

## How the pandemic and later research tested it

Critiques of this book come from two directions: scholars who say he gave the illness the wrong name, and the pandemic, which tested his prediction directly. The first to revise the book was its author.

### The author's revision: the crisis did not bring people back

The second edition's preface notes that vaccines were developed in record time and that the feared economic collapse never came, yet the public did not believe experts had much to do with these outcomes, or even that the outcomes had happened. He writes: "I never expected that people would reject vaccines—literally choosing to die rather than let go of their tribal affiliations," nor that prominent scientists would have to travel with armed security guards.

He also reweights the causes: he still ties the problem to how our brains work, the commodification of education, the internet, and journalism, but "I now give more weight to the influences of narcissism, existential levels of ennui and boredom, and high living standards."

U.S. polling supports his judgment that people did not come back, but the details are more complicated than his account. Pew Research Center's tracking shows that the share of Americans with confidence in scientists to act in the public's best interests was 87% in April 2020, early in the pandemic, slightly above its pre-pandemic level; it then fell steadily to 73% by October 2023. The decline was concentrated among Republicans: the share with not too much or no confidence in scientists rose from 14% in April 2020 to 38%. In other words, the crisis did at first push people toward experts; the effect just did not last.

### Two mistakes experts made during the pandemic

The *Atlantic* essay adapted from chapter 7 spreads the pandemic's failures across politicians, the public, and experts. For experts, he names two "fundamental mistakes":

- **Taking the place of elected officials.** In the vacuum left by failed leadership in Washington, scientists issued policy guidance directly and took on decisions that belonged to elected officials. This breaks the division of labor in his own book, where experts propose and leaders decide.
- **Letting political calculation override principle.** After George Floyd was killed by a police officer in May 2020, protests broke out across the country, at a time when ordinary people still could not gather, attend church, or hold weddings and funerals. More than 1,200 medical professionals signed an open letter supporting the protests. His verdict: if doctors believed that masks and being outdoors reduced the risk of gatherings, they should already have been pressing to lift restrictions for everyone; if they still held to the theories behind the lockdowns, then endorsing the protests meant consciously setting those concerns aside for a political cause they agreed with. "Either way, they were acting as advocates rather than scientists."

He also concedes that schools stayed closed too long, harming students academically and emotionally. But his summary still leans toward defending experts: much of what the public saw as failure was really the normal trial and error of science working with limited data.

### What experiments say: the cost of experts taking sides

Whether "acting as advocates rather than scientists" damages trust has since been tested. In 2023 the economist Floyd Jiuyun Zhang published a preregistered experiment in *Nature Human Behaviour*: in summer 2021, half of 4,260 U.S. participants read a summary of *Nature*'s 2020 editorial endorsing Joe Biden, while the other half read about *Nature*'s website redesign.

Trump supporters who read about the endorsement rated *Nature* as informed 0.85 standard deviations lower; when later offered articles on how well vaccines work against new variants, their rate of choosing *Nature* articles fell 14.2 percentage points, a relative drop of about 38%. Their trust in U.S. scientists in general also fell, though by much less. Biden supporters' trust barely changed. The endorsement also did not change participants' views of the two candidates.

The result supports Nichols's intuition: when a scientific institution takes sides, it pays in the other camp's trust and gains no persuasive power in return. There are two caveats. Zhang himself notes that this was a one-shot experiment and cannot show how long the effect lasts; an independent 2024 reproduction confirmed the negative effect on *Nature* itself but found weaker evidence that it spilled over to the scientific community as a whole.

### The scholarly critique: a crisis of trust, not of ignorance

The science and technology studies scholar Paolo Volonté was blunt in his review in the Italian journal *Tecnoscienza*. He first raises a reflexive problem: Nichols criticizes experts for overstepping, yet a political scientist writing a book on the sociology of knowledge without citing the scholars who study the crisis of expertise is itself "a blatant example of a political scientist overreaching into a field in which he lacks competence."

His central critique is that "the crisis of expertise is not a crisis of ignorance, but a crisis of trust." He turns Nichols's own example against him: the vaccine-resistant parents in Marin County are educated, which suggests that what drives them is not a rejection of experts but the awareness that experts disagree and that institutionally recognized experts are not necessarily right. The question is not whether to recognize expert authority but "whose epistemic authority should be recognized."

The sociologist Gil Eyal's 2019 *The Crisis of Expertise* is the fullest version of this critique. According to the Cambridge scholar Federico Brandmayr's review, Eyal explicitly rejects diagnoses like Nichols's that blame the public's willful ignorance, and makes three points: we are seeing unprecedented reliance on experts coupled with unprecedented suspicion of them; what is under attack is not science as a whole but the "regulatory" and "policy" sciences that sit between academic research and policymaking; and who counts as an expert has always been uncertain. His prescription is the opposite of Nichols's: instead of denouncing the public, expert organizations should make their procedures transparent and let outsiders, even skeptics, take part. His example is a citizen science project started by climate skeptics to show that U.S. weather station data were flawed, which ended up finding that actual temperatures were higher than many stations had recorded.

Justin Chu's 2020 review in the bioethics journal *Ethics & Medicine* is milder and points to two gaps: the book relies mainly on anecdotes, without statistical evidence that the problem is systemic; and it does not deal with cases where experts disagree but a decision still has to be made. Nichols covers this only with Russell's line that when experts disagree no view can be treated as certain by a layperson, but pandemic decisions rarely could wait for experts to agree.

### The psychology he leaned on is being revised too

Nichols treats the Dunning–Kruger effect (the least competent overestimate themselves most) as part of human nature. But Gignac and Zajenkowski showed in 2020 that the effect is mostly a statistical artifact: most people already think they are above average, and extreme scores tend to drift toward the middle; those two facts alone are enough to draw a "worst performers overrate themselves most" pattern in the data.

The replication crisis has also gone differently from what he suggested. He cited Gary King's rebuttal, implying psychology's problems were overstated; but in 2018 Camerer and colleagues, in *Nature Human Behaviour*, used preregistered designs with samples about five times the original size to repeat 21 social science experiments published in *Nature* and *Science* between 2010 and 2015. Only 13 (62%) replicated, and the replications' effect sizes averaged about half the originals. Other large psychology replications found something similar: the classic priming effects (words you glimpse without noticing change your behavior) mostly failed to reproduce, while anchoring (the first number you see pulls your judgment) held up.

These corrections weaken the psychological tools Nichols uses to explain the public; but the corrections themselves are exactly what he called the ordinary failures of science: errors found by the same community of experts using stricter methods, and corrected in public.

### Humility or planting flags

In the 2024 GQ interview, Nichols concedes that one failing of the book is its ending: "I probably should have said something more about what to do about it." His new answer is: "I don't think it's that helpful to call on the average citizen to be more epistemically humble." Experts, he says, should get out there and "plant some flags," and say outright, "I'm not your therapist!"

Later research does not fully agree. In five preregistered studies with 2,034 participants published in *Nature Human Behaviour* in 2024, Jonah Koetke and colleagues found that a scientist described as low in intellectual humility, for example unwilling to admit what they do not yet know, was trusted markedly less, and people believed their research less. But the last study also found that communication strategies scientists use to signal humility, such as stressing the limitations of their methods, made them seem more humble without making them seem more trustworthy, and some even made people trust the research itself less.

So each side is half right: arrogance costs trust, which counts against planting flags; but how to show humility in a way that earns trust is still an open question.

### What has not been cleared away

- **The four kinds of failure.** Ordinary failures of science, cross-expertise, prediction, and deception are a good tool for sorting cases of "the experts were wrong." The first is part of how science works; only the other three are reasons to lower trust.
- **Knowers and deciders.** During the pandemic, experts drew the fiercest attacks when they crossed this line and made political decisions in place of elected officials. It is also the line Nichols himself concedes experts crossed.
- **Reliance on technicians persists.** Eyal's critique actually confirms this: people still rely on doctors and engineers every day, and the disputes concentrate at the policy level.
- **Russell's three rules.** Experts agreeing, disagreeing, or all saying they don't know call for three different attitudes; this remains the most practical set of rules a layperson has.

## What the book adds

Nichols writes about the relationship between institutions and citizens: everyone depends every day on knowledge they cannot check. He diagnoses the public's turn away from experts as ignorance plus narcissism; critics and later data point to another name for the illness: **this is not a crisis of ignorance but a crisis of trust.** The problem is not that people know too little but that they no longer agree on whom to believe, or which procedures for producing knowledge to believe.

On why groups lose their reason, that is a very practical answer: once the procedures that produce knowledge get tied to party lines, the same conclusion is read as two different things by two groups, each feeling perfectly rational. Taiwan's domestic vaccine dispute, below, is exactly that.

Nichols himself also shows the way out. He studied how experts err, wrote that prediction is a common expert mistake, then made a wrong prediction himself and publicly admitted it in the next edition. Individuals can rarely avoid collective mistakes by knowing more; what they can rely on is a set of procedures that lets errors be found and publicly corrected, including the experts' own. ## Three questions when you don't want to believe an expert

There can be legitimate reasons to distrust experts. The next time you notice you don't want to believe one, ask three questions to see which kind of reason yours is:

1. **Interest: who are they speaking for?** Do they have undisclosed conflicts of interest? Did their position shift just when it was politically convenient? Wakefield failed to disclose financial conflicts of interest; the protest letter made public health standards look bendable for a political cause. Zhang's experiment shows that reasons of this kind do, and should, affect trust.
2. **Track record: are they accurate in this field?** Are they speaking within their expertise or outside it? Looking back, how accurate were their past judgments on similar questions? Pauling's authority in chemistry did not extend to vitamins; Bialer's prediction about the Soviet Union can be used to test his next one.
3. **Institution: how was the conclusion reached?** Were the criteria set in advance? Are the data and the deliberations public? Can others check them? This is Eyal's point: the more transparent the procedure, the less it has to rest on personal authority.

All three reasons can be legitimate, and all three can be checked. What does not hold up is a fourth kind: distrusting someone because they are an expert, or because they are on the other side. If that is the only reason you have left, Russell's three rules are more reliable than your instinct.

**Something to do tonight**: read Nichols's original 2014 essay in *The Federalist* (free, about thirteen minutes) and go through the five rules at the end, asking yourself which ones you agree with. Then pick a commentator or expert you follow regularly, find a prediction they made three years ago, and see how it turned out.

## A comparison from Taiwan: the domestic vaccine dispute

The dispute over Taiwan's domestic COVID-19 vaccine is a point of comparison for Nichols's chapter 7: Taiwanese did not refuse vaccines on a large scale; the fight was over which set of expert procedures to trust.

In June 2021, Taiwan's Food and Drug Administration published its criteria for emergency use authorization of domestic COVID-19 vaccines: instead of phase 3 efficacy data, they would use "immunobridging," requiring a domestic vaccine's neutralizing antibodies to be shown non-inferior to those of Taiwanese who received the AstraZeneca vaccine. On July 18, the agency convened an expert review meeting on the Medigen vaccine: 21 attended and the chair did not vote; 18 approved (15 of them conditionally), 1 asked for more data, and 1 voted against. The health ministry approved production the next day, requiring the company to submit monthly safety monitoring reports and an effectiveness report within one year.

The dispute centered on process. The meeting was not fully recorded; Chen Shih-chung (陳時中), then health minister and head of the epidemic command center, said that because another domestic vaccine, from 聯亞, was still under review, the AstraZeneca comparison data would be withheld for the time being for fairness. The virologist 施信如 told *The Reporter* at the time: "The less the numbers are made public, the more speculation there is." The agency later published the meeting minutes on its website, and in a 2024 statement stressed that the review criteria had been set before any of the trial results came in.

What followed can be seen in research. A 2025 study by Yun-Yeh Chiang and Jason Kuo in the *Japanese Journal of Political Science* opens by noting that, unlike in other industrial societies, Taiwan's partisan fight over COVID-19 vaccination centered on brand choice rather than acceptance. Surveying 1,642 adults in fall 2022, they found that supporters of the ruling party, especially weak supporters, were markedly more likely to choose Medigen for their first shot if they trusted the government; but for the booster, party affiliation had no additional effect, and what mattered was whether someone had received Medigen before. A 2024 study by Chun-Tung Kuo and Ruoh-Rong Yu, using panel data from 2,361 people spanning 2020 to 2023, likewise found that national identity and trust in government were associated with brand choice: those who trusted the government were more likely to prefer Medigen, while those who did not were less likely to want Medigen or AstraZeneca and more willing to take Pfizer-BioNTech. Both studies are correlational: they show who tended to choose which brand, not why.

Through this post's framework, the episode has three layers:

- **It looks more like a crisis of trust than of ignorance.** Volonté's question of whose authority to recognize became, in Taiwan, concrete: trust the FDA's review procedure, or the phase 3 standards of international regulators? Both sides had expert backing, and which one people chose overlapped heavily with party.
- **Transparency of procedure determined the room for speculation.** Setting the criteria in advance and publishing the vote were good procedural design; not recording the meeting and withholding the comparison data left room for speculation. Eyal's prescription is concrete here: the more transparent the procedure, the less it has to rest on "trust us."
- **Partisan influence was strongest when information was scarce.** The study's most interesting finding is that party affiliation only shaped the first shot, when information was scarcest; once people had firsthand experience and more data, the effect disappeared. That looks less like Nichols's narcissism and more like borrowing under uncertainty: when people cannot judge for themselves, they borrow the judgment of the camp they trust.

This post does not assess the Medigen vaccine's efficacy; that is a medical question this post cannot answer. What is clear is that once an expert procedure gets tied to party positions, the same review conclusion is read as two different things by two groups of people.
