In 2017, several senior CNN journalists reported that a Trump confidant was linked to a Russian hedge fund. The story turned out to be wrong; CNN retracted it, apologized, and forced out the journalists responsible. Trump's response on Twitter: "So they caught Fake News CNN cold, but what about NBC, CBS & ABC?"

The journalist Jonathan Rauch cites this episode in the first chapter of *The Constitution of Knowledge* and points out the inversion in it: a news organization correcting an error by its own standards proved, in Trump's worldview, not its integrity but its corruption, and indeed the corruption of the whole news industry. When the White House and the Republican National Committee announced "Fake News Awards" in 2018, Rauch counted that at least seven of the eleven items had already been corrected by the outlets that published them. In other words, they knew the reports were false because the outlets had said so.

The question the book sets out to answer is: what rules does a society full of disagreement use to decide what is true? Rauch's answer is an invisible constitution. This post looks at what that constitution's provisions are, how critics on the left and right attack it, and how events in 2025 tested his optimism.

## What this book is

*The Constitution of Knowledge: A Defense of Truth* was published by the Brookings Institution Press in June 2021, in eight chapters: the first two on why humans err cognitively and split into tribes, the third and fourth on how the modern knowledge order formed and what its rules are, and the last four on digital media, trolling, cancel culture, and how to push back. Rauch is a senior fellow at the Brookings Institution and a contributing writer at *The Atlantic*, and a longtime advocate for free speech and gay rights; his 1993 book *Kindly Inquisitors* already dealt with the same problem, and this book's core argument first appeared in his essay of the same name in *National Affairs* in fall 2018.

The book has not been translated into Chinese. I searched Traditional and Simplified Chinese catalogs and found nothing; the only Rauch book I found in Traditional Chinese is his 1994 book on Japan, *The Outnation* (《局外之國》). The word "constitution" means both a charter of government and the makeup of a thing; this post's Chinese title uses 憲法 (charter), because Rauch's argument rests on the analogy with the U.S. Constitution.

This post relies mainly on the full first chapter that Brookings has published, the original 2018 essay, and passages and page numbers quoted in reviews; I did not read the rest of the book in full, which is discussed again under limitations.

## What the original says

### Not a market but a constitution

Rauch starts from a familiar metaphor, the marketplace of ideas. It is good "as far as it goes," he says, but woefully incomplete, because it suggests ideas compete directly in some ethereal realm. In fact:

> our conversations are mediated through institutions like journals and newspapers and social-media platforms; and they rely on a dense network of norms and rules, like truthfulness and fact-checking; and they depend on the expertise of professionals, like peer reviewers and editors—and the entire system rests on a foundation of values: a shared understanding that there are right and wrong ways to make knowledge.

These values, rules, and institutions, he says, "do for knowledge what the U.S. Constitution does for politics": they create a governing structure that forces social contestation onto peaceful and productive pathways.

In the first chapter he lists what the two constitutions share: both are social compacts in which participants agree to follow rules and forgo certain claims; both place coercion off-limits and require people to negotiate agreement to make laws or knowledge; both distribute decision-making across many competing and cooperating actors, with checks and balances creating layers of accountability; both combine formal rules with informal norms. He also concedes that "in some respects I may have overworked the comparison."

The 2018 essay spells out the correspondences more concretely: peer review and replication are checks and balances, specialization is separation of powers, scientific societies and professional bodies are governing institutions, and citations and confirmations are voting. This "reality-based community" is not limited to scientists; it includes journalism, the courts, law enforcement, and intelligence, every profession that requires competing hypotheses to be tested and justified.

### Two rules and a funnel

According to quotations in reviews, Rauch condenses the constitution into two rules: "No final say; no personal authority."

- **No final say** (what he calls the fallibilist rule): a statement can be claimed as knowledge only if it can in principle be debunked and only insofar as it withstands attempts to debunk it.
- **No personal authority** (the empirical rule): persuasiveness cannot come from who the speaker is. Anyone can replicate an experiment, and it had better come out the same way; persons are interchangeable.

He uses a funnel to show how the two rules work together. At the wide end, millions of people float millions of hypotheses every day; they pass through one screen after another, testing, editing, peer review, publication, citation, replication, and only a tiny few reach the narrow end and are admitted as knowledge. The 2018 essay has a precise passage on this:

> To protect the wide end of the funnel, we disallow censorship. We say: *Alt-truth is never criminalized*. At the same time, to protect the narrow end of the funnel, we regulate influence. We say: *Alt-truth is always ignored*.

You can believe and say whatever you want; but if your claims don't check out, or you don't submit them for checking, you can't expect anyone else to publish, care about, or even notice what you think. "We let alt-truth talk, but we don't let it write textbooks, receive tenure, bypass peer review."

### Two insurgencies: trolling and canceling

The second half of the book deals with what he sees as two threats. The first chapter sets them side by side:

> One is predominantly right-wing and populist, the other predominantly left-wing and elitist. One employs chaos and confusion, the other conformity and social coercion. But their goals are similar, and often, weirdly, they act as de facto allies.

**Troll epistemology.** In the 2018 essay he says that ordinary lies and propaganda try to make you believe something, while disinformation tries to make you "disbelieve *everything*"; he quotes Trump's former adviser Steve Bannon: the way to deal with the media is to "flood the zone with shit." According to a reviewer's quotation, the book says that a relentless flow of misinformation functions like censorship, "only perhaps better, and certainly easier."

**Canceling.** He calls it "despotism of the few." The first chapter uses many interviews to describe campuses after the mid-2010s: the problem looked "less like censorship and more like censoriousness"; he cites a 2019 Knight Foundation poll in which 68 percent of college students said their campus climate kept students from expressing their true opinions. The book offers a list of indicators for telling whether you are being canceled or legitimately criticized, including punitiveness, deplatforming, grandstanding, reductionism, orchestration, secondary boycotts, and indifference to accuracy.

His diagnosis of digital media: "information technology is very different from knowledge technology." Information can simply be emitted; knowledge must be achieved through rich social interaction, and the platforms' metrics were "sensitive to popularity but indifferent to truth."

### His optimism

The book's tone is optimistic. The first chapter says: "I am not an alarmist. To the contrary, I write this book in a spirit of hope and guarded optimism." The idea that obnoxious, misguided, seditious, and blasphemous expression deserves not only to be tolerated but protected is, he says, "the single most counterintuitive social principle" in human history, and also the most successful.

The 2018 essay placed three more specific bets: first, "Trolls have swarms, but the constitution of knowledge has institutions"; second, that Facebook and Google had declared their commitment to the constitution of knowledge and were working to demote fake news; third, that the government's statistical and research agencies, such as the CDC, the Bureau of Labor Statistics, and the Congressional Budget Office, had not yet come under direct attack, but "my guess is that, when the attack comes, they too will stand their ground."

## What later research revised

Criticism of the book comes from three directions, and events in 2025 tested its predictions directly.

### From the left: making science the sole arbiter

The political theorist Laura K. Field's review in *The Bulwark* is respectful overall but points to an internal contradiction. Rauch says liberal science is open and privileges no viewpoint, yet also calls it "the only legitimate validator of knowledge" and, on page 87, lists a long roster of people excluded from "social decisions about objective reality": creationists, Christian Scientists, homeopaths, astrologists, flat-earthers, anti-vaxxers, birthers, 9/11 truthers, postmodern professors, political partisans, QAnon followers, "and adherents of any number of other belief systems and religions."

Field thinks this list may cover most Americans and shows Rauch to be too dismissive of the humanities, theology, and philosophy, the fields that think hardest about power. She reminds readers that the mantle of science was used to support slavery, racist policies, and eugenics, and that moral reasoning and political action usually challenged those arguments first. She worries the book is "an unwitting apologia for scientism and technocracy," and argues for returning to Weber's view of science as one voice in a broader clash of values rather than the arbiter of public life.

### From the right: the rot is inside

The economist Arnold Kling's 2022 essay at Econlib agrees with most of Rauch's analysis but thinks he misses half the picture: "Where Rauch focuses on the attacks on the twentieth-century information order from without, I would emphasize the rot from within."

Kling's examples include the book quoting two former FBI officials without mentioning that they were disciplined after texts expressing an intention to bring down Trump, and the book treating the *New York Times* passing five million subscribers during the Trump years as evidence of a market for real news, where Kling cites another reading: that opposing Trump had itself become a business model. His conclusion is that the twentieth-century institutions are not coming back, and rebuilding the constitution of knowledge will need new ones.

Rauch partly conceded this in 2018: he said the charges against academia and journalism "contain important grains of truth," such as liberal bias in mainstream media and the replication crisis in science, but concluded that the answer is to fix the defects, not to trash the institutions.

### From scholarship: the paradox of tolerance

The communication scholar Sue Curry Jansen's 2023 review in the *International Journal of Communication* praises the book's accessibility but notes that Rauch relies heavily on Popper's open society and falsifiability without engaging Popper's other famous argument, the paradox of tolerance: unlimited tolerance leads to the destruction of tolerance, especially when opponents refuse the norms of rational discourse. Rauch himself calls trolls "epistemic sociopath[s]"; Jansen argues that urging readers to model civility is not enough against such opponents.

She also notes a possible self-contradiction: Rauch repeats right-wing complaints about liberal bias in universities, while the constitution of knowledge he defends rests on liberal foundations and most current knowledge comes out of universities.

### 2025: testing the three bets

All three of the 2018 bets were tested by 2025.

- **Platforms.** On January 7, 2025, in an announcement titled "More Speech and Fewer Mistakes," Meta said Facebook, Instagram, and Threads would end their third-party fact-checking program in the U.S. and move to community notes like X's. CEO Mark Zuckerberg said fact-checkers had been "too politically biased" and had "destroyed more trust than they've created." In 2018 Rauch said Facebook had declared its commitment to the constitution of knowledge; seven years later the same company described fact-checking itself as the problem.
- **Statistical agencies.** On August 1, 2025, the Bureau of Labor Statistics reported that the economy added only 73,000 jobs in July and revised May and June down by a combined 258,000; Reuters noted this was the largest two-month downward revision since at least 1979 outside the early pandemic. That day Trump wrote on social media that the numbers were "RIGGED," accused Commissioner Erika McEntarfer of manipulating the data without offering evidence, and ordered her fired. Revisions are a normal part of the statistical process; this is the CNN story at the top of this post again, except this time what was treated as evidence of guilt was a government statistical agency's routine revision.
- **Institutions versus swarms.** In September 2025, on *The Atlantic*'s David Frum Show, Rauch revised his own judgment: in Trump's first term the lies came mostly from the president himself, while agencies like the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration were doing their best to keep telling the truth; in the second term, the attorney general, the health secretary, and official agencies were all doing the same thing: "now we're seeing a machinery of lies. It's coming from the government itself, not just Trump." His book assumed the adversary was an outside swarm of trolls, with institutions on the side of the constitution of knowledge; when the attack comes from inside the institutions, the contrast "trolls have swarms, the constitution has institutions" no longer holds.

None of this means his framework is wrong. Rather, it brings its premise to the surface: like a political constitution, this one has no enforcement power of its own and depends on participants being willing to abide by it.

### What has not been cleared away

- **The two rules.** No final say and no personal authority make a good test of any claim that "the institution decides" or "I decide," on the left or the right.
- **The two ends of the funnel.** Letting people speak and letting claims shape decisions are different things. This distinction lets "free speech" and "not every claim should be adopted" both hold, and avoids calling fact-checking censorship or censorship fact-checking.
- **Correction is evidence of health.** CNN's retraction and the BLS revision are the system working. Treating correction as evidence of fraud is the cheapest way to attack this constitution.
- **Knowledge is social.** This is where he differs from McIntyre: McIntyre gives advice to individuals, while Rauch points out that however rational individuals are, they need a group of people who look for each other's errors and the institutions that support them.

## Where this book sits in the explanatory chain

The previous post's McIntyre described the organized manufacture of doubt; Rauch answers what in a society can be attacked that way: not any particular fact, but the procedures and institutions that settle facts.

It also ties earlier posts together. Kahneman said organizations are more reliable than individuals because they think slowly and follow procedures; Rauch writes those procedures up as a constitution. Hofstadter said intellectuals are resented because, as experts, they are needed too much; Rauch's institutions are exactly the target of that resentment. Hofstadter also warned that part of the resentment is legitimate, which is precisely what Kling and Field argue from two directions.

The next post is Nichols's *The Death of Expertise*. Rauch writes about institutions and ends by calling on citizens to defend them; Nichols asks about the citizens' end: why ordinary people no longer respect experts, and what experts themselves got wrong.

## One test to take away

The next time you see "Institution X can't be trusted," ask four questions first:

1. **Can the claim be checked?** Does the conclusion rest on procedure and evidence, or on someone's status? Would someone else following the same method get the same result?
2. **Does it have a mechanism for correcting errors, and has it used it?** Which is more trustworthy, an institution that publicly corrects itself or a source that never admits error? Is the correction itself being used as evidence of fraud?
3. **Is the criticism about the procedure or the conclusion?** Is a step being shown to have been violated, or is the objection only that the result is unwelcome?
4. **Is the dispute about the wide end or the narrow end?** Is the demand the right to speak, or for one's claim to shape decisions, get into textbooks, or become policy without being tested?

**Something to do tonight**: read Rauch's 2018 essay in *National Affairs* (free, about twenty minutes), focusing on the "Troll Epistemology" section. Then find a recent news correction notice and see how it was discussed on social media: as responsibility, or as proof of fakery?

## Back to Taiwan

What does this mechanism look like in Taiwan in 2026? The "vote-rigging" videos that circulated after the 2024 presidential election are almost a Taiwanese version of the CNN story at the top.

After the count on January 13, many "rigging" claims spread online. One that the Taiwan FactCheck Center examined was a handwritten card claiming the presidential vote exceeded the number of voters by more than 2.48 million; the check found the card had added the three candidates' totals wrong: their combined total was 13,947,506, and the 100,804-vote gap with the actual turnout was invalid ballots. Another video stitched together 28 clips filmed by citizen observers under the title "This isn't rigging? Then what is?" and passed two million views.

The center traced the clips one by one. Sixteen could be traced to specific polling stations, and its reporters interviewed local election commissions and polling staff, in some cases cross-checking with monitors appointed by different parties. Two clips mistook the ballot box's base board for a false bottom; the other 14 showed counting slips such as wrong tally marks, misread numbers, and arithmetic errors, all of which had been caught on the spot by polling staff or corrected after citizen observers pointed them out, with no problem in the final totals. The remaining 12 clips were too fragmentary to locate. The report also noted that no candidate disputed the result after the election.

Through Rauch's framework, there are three layers here:

- **Taiwan's vote count is a small constitution of knowledge.** Counting is fully open to the public, who may watch and film; different parties appoint their own monitors; ballots are called and tallied one by one, and results are posted on the spot. This is a concrete design for "no personal authority": you don't have to trust any one person, because anyone can be present, film, and find mistakes.
- **Correction repackaged as fraud.** Many of the clips actually captured the correction process: a citizen points out a wrong mark, and staff fix it on the spot. With the second half cut off and a "rigging" title added, these images turned from evidence that the system works into evidence that it is corrupt. It is the same move as Trump calling CNN's retraction proof of fake news.
- **Openness also supplies the material.** Precisely because anyone can film, there are so many fragments to take out of context. The center was frank about the difficulty: a written fact-check may not reach people already hit by the "rigging" claim. It is the same problem Nyhan raised in the previous post, corrections not reaching the people who need them.

The case also marks a boundary. The center's report states at the end that whether polling staff made mistakes and whether procedures need review are matters open to public criticism and outside the scope of the check. That is Rauch's funnel: criticism of procedure belongs to the wide end and is welcome from anyone; but the conclusion "it was rigged" has to pass evidentiary testing to reach the narrow end.

## Limitations

- Of the book itself I read only the full first chapter and table of contents that Brookings has published; the content and quotations from the other chapters come from Rauch's 2018 essay, his 2025 Quillette essay and the Frum Show transcript, and quotations in reviews by Field, Kling, Jansen, Tracinski, and Staddon. "No final say; no personal authority" and the exclusion list on page 87 were confirmed through reviewers' quotations.
- The Frum Show transcript was read from a third-party reposting; *The Atlantic*'s own page could not be retrieved directly.
- Meta's announcement is quoted via reports from ABC, Axios, and Investopedia; the BLS episode comes from AP, CNN, CNBC, and Reuters reporting.
- The Taiwan case rests only on the Taiwan FactCheck Center's reports and Central News Agency coverage; the center is itself one of the institutions this post discusses.
- I did not read the City Journal or National Review reviews; the right-of-center critique here relies on Kling alone.
